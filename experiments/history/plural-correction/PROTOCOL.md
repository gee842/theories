## Question

Can one observation-based policy jointly meet distinct stated requirements? If not, can we certify the incompatibility and distinguish a declared restriction from a physical limit? No common weighted utility is optimized. The three evaluated consequences are F = probability of terminal stock collapse, H = expected cumulative target-absent repair count plus .05 per test, C = expected cumulative expenditure minus earned output. This removes the previous objective's terminal salvage and collapse penalty. Lower is better for each declared requirement, but no common exchange rate is supplied.

The constituency interpretation is illustrative: service users may bound F, people exposed to tests/interventions may bound H, and contributors may bound C. These are supplied aggregate requirements, not modeled individual exposures, consent, bargaining, or real preferences. Target-absent repair can still restore stock. H is not complete harm; C does not prove a contributor actually receives output. A common known probability model, free sufficient-belief retention, central executability, and admissible randomization remain assumptions.

## Joint feasibility and numerical certificate

For expected state/action occupancy y, use the standard flow equations Ay=rho, y>=0 and consequence matrix Gy=(F,H,C). With positive diagnostic scales s=(1,1,10), solve the phase-I linear program min t subject Gy−s*t<=b. Its value is a normalized worst violation used only to test existence. It is not a social preference or a recommendation for the least-bad policy. For an infeasible tuple the returned nearest vector remains a **violating diagnostic point**, not an acceptable policy.

Extract nonnegative separating weights from inequality dual multipliers, normalize them, and independently compute min_policy w·(F,H,C) using backward dynamic programming. A positive gap above w·b certifies infeasibility. Reconstruct the randomized observation-based Markov policy from occupancy ratios and independently evaluate its three consequences. Floating-point results within 1e−8 are treated as feasible; positive independently supported margins above 1e−8 certify infeasibility; ambiguous cases are marked unresolved. Report primal/dual, flow, and policy-evaluation residuals. All recorded cases should be well resolved.

Changing the positive scales can change the diagnostic point and separating weights, but cannot change exact feasibility. Average constraints and randomization are explicit; a feasible lottery need not satisfy each bound on each path or in each pure component. The finite convex theorem also extends to separately declared evaluative probability models when each coordinate is calculated on the same executable policy and lottery. That broader theorem is not implemented by this common-model occupancy calculation.
