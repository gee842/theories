# Research agenda

2026-09-13 · Assistant proposals based on the [founding message](../../sources/15-codex-theory-conversations/2026-09-13-architecture-for-a-living-earth.md). These are future investigations; no simulations or engineering tests have been performed.

## 1. Compare settlement forms on equal terms

Use the same resident population, usable floor area, public functions, accessibility, and service quality for three designs: branching towers, elevated frameworks, and compact ground settlement. Include a baseline landscape without the proposed construction to understand ecological changes. Meeting housing needs elsewhere belongs in the wider comparison.

Study farmland, intact forest, and previously disturbed land separately. Vary latitude, climate, terrain, support spacing, inhabited height, and openness of the structure. Initial dimensions should be labeled illustrative rather than presented as feasible limits.

| Question | Evidence to obtain | Finding that could change the design |
|---|---|---|
| Does elevation release useful habitat? | Permanent and temporary disturbance, buried works, access routes, remaining connected habitat | A compact ground alternative leaves more functioning habitat overall |
| Does enough light reach the vegetation? | Hourly and seasonal light at canopy and ground; species-specific response; overlapping shadows | Vegetation persists initially but regeneration or productivity declines |
| Can narrow supports carry the settlement? | Loads, geotechnical conditions, lateral stability, movement, foundation geometry | Buried works or required bracing erase the expected land advantage |
| Can it be built with little disturbance? | Installation sequence, staging, robot access, logistics, soil compaction | Assembly requires extensive roads or clearance beneath the final structure |
| Can it support normal life? | Accessible routes, service capacity, daylight in rooms, acoustics, evacuation and service failures | Daily access is burdensome or dependable escape routes need much more structure |
| Is removal beneficial? | Full dismantling sequence, handling, component rejection, site recovery time | Moving repeatedly imposes more harm than maintaining a stable settlement |

Do not collapse these into a single score prematurely. Report both absolute effects and effects per resident; a favorable ratio can conceal increasing total damage.

## 2. Follow one bay through its entire life

Specify a conceptual frame bay with enclosure, interior, service interfaces, and robot attachment points. Draw where joints can be reached and how a member is supported while disconnected. Trace installation, inspection, damage, repair, removal, storage, and a second use.

Separate direct component reuse from remanufacture and material recycling. Record recovered mass, usable quality, rejected components, replacement materials, energy, water, and transport. Include foundations rather than ending the account at the column base. Ask whether extracting a foundation or leaving part of it causes less lasting disturbance in each site condition.

The useful output is a construction sequence and material ledger that exposes missing operations. A rendering of a robot holding a beam is insufficient evidence of a complete process.

## 3. Model the enabling industrial system

Map the supply chain for one settlement: raw and recovered material, processing plants, actuators, electronics, tooling, robot manufacture, energy, transport, and maintenance. Identify what can already be automated, what has only been demonstrated in bounded settings, and what the scenario assumes.

Track how new capacity increases output, how much output must maintain existing capacity, and what limits the next expansion. Compare growth with a mature system mainly replacing worn components. Count the land and ecological burden of the supporting industry in the settlement comparison.

Keep cheap energy as the user’s intended scenario, then test variations in reliability, embodied material, recovery yield, and throughput. This asks how the future works under abundance rather than dismissing it for failing today’s cost structure.

## 4. Make regeneration observable

Choose a site-specific ecological baseline and comparison areas. With relevant ecological expertise, identify measurements of vegetation recruitment, habitat connectivity, soils, water, and selected species across seasons and after removal. Define what would trigger changes in structure, lighting, drainage, traffic, or occupancy.

Record preservation, avoided harm, and demonstrated recovery separately. Retain negative findings even when the settlement is visually appealing.

## 5. Explore the life it enables

Write a day in one neighborhood, following a resident, a maintenance robot, a reusable cup, and water through the same coherent place. Include meals, solitude, play, care, learning, public gatherings, and ordinary repair. Then show a household changing its room arrangement or moving while another household chooses to remain.

Compare three forms of seasonal adaptation: changing the enclosure, moving modules between established supports, and relocating an entire structure. Explore the cultural value and ecological cost of each. Keep variety in architecture and ways of living compatible with reusable connections.

## Open choices

No site, population, dimensions, construction material, ownership model, relocation frequency, or transport technology has been selected. The next bounded artifact proposed here is a comparative architectural section and one reusable-bay sequence, followed by a simple seasonal shadow model if the geometry is coherent.
