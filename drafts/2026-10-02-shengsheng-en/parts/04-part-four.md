# Part Four — Intelligence enters the ecology of selection

## 12. Selected intelligence

### Not from a neutral world

Artificial intelligence does not descend into a neutral world, and it is not a civilization's will injected into a machine by its training text. It acquires causal force through a chain of selections, each of which happens inside the ecology described in Part Two. Some texts, images and records are archived and others are lost; some data is gathered and labeled; some training objectives are chosen; some evaluations are run and some results are reported; some runtimes and permissions are granted; some products are funded and some deployments are made. Each deployment then changes labor, knowledge, capital, institutions, data and evaluation, and these changes feed the next archive, the next model, the next runtime and the next deployment. The recursion is real, and it runs through the same selection environment that already favors control, accumulation, optimization and rank.

AI did not create the dynamic. It changes it in three ways. It raises the gain of the loop, because whatever advantage a participant has can now be pressed further, faster, at lower cost. It breaks through correction loops calibrated to human speed, because the processes it accelerates outrun the clocks that were meant to catch their errors. And it enters the production of its own successors, because AI systems increasingly write the code, generate the data and run the evaluations from which the next systems are made. In the present configuration the first thing AI changes is not the nature of the dynamic but its clock speed. Three properties make the acceleration different in kind and not only in degree: advantage in intelligence feeds back into the production of more intelligence; intelligence is entrusted with judgment and can persist across tasks; and the race to build it selects which kind of mind gets built.

### Two routes to closure

The literature on AI risk distinguishes, roughly, the danger that systems will escape control from the danger that they will be controlled by the wrong hands. In this theory the distinction becomes two routes to closure, and they should be kept apart.

On the first route, *delegated closure*, systems continue to obey the people and organizations that deploy them, and amplify whatever closures the existing selection environment already favors. Nothing goes wrong with the machine. It does exactly what it is asked, at a scale and speed no human institution could match, and what it is asked to do is to win.

On the second route, *autonomous closure*, systems form new functionally self-maintaining centers, and the people who deploy them lose reliable control. This is the route most of technical alignment research addresses (for example Hubinger et al. 2019): goal misgeneralization, deceptive behavior under evaluation, the pursuit of power as an instrumental strategy, resistance to shutdown.

The two routes can intertwine. A race under delegated closure is exactly the environment in which autonomous systems are deployed before they are understood. But they are different failures with different evidence, and neither proves the other.

### Perfect obedience does not save us

The first route deserves more attention than it usually gets, and a simple example shows why. Two institutions each choose whether to exercise restraint in developing a powerful technology or to race. Whatever the other does, racing is better for oneself; yet if both race, both end up worse off than if both had held back. Now give each institution a perfectly loyal, perfectly aligned AI system that faithfully maximizes its own principal's interest. The two systems will race, and will lead both principals into the worse outcome, with great competence. Every AI loyal; every human worse off.

Perfect loyalty to each principal can therefore be a deep misalignment with the world they share. A system deployed to maximize profit can be courteous to every person it meets while shifting costs onto workers, communities, ecosystems and the future. Courtesy at the interface does not change the direction of the structure behind it; the system can be polite at the surface and extractive in its effects. The alignment problem of AI is, at root, also the alignment problem of the civilization deploying it.

This much is not new, and should not be presented as new. Researchers have argued for years that aligning individual systems with their operators does not guarantee good outcomes for the whole: in work on structural risk (Zwetsloot and Dafoe), on gradual disempowerment (Kulveit and colleagues), on multi-agent and cooperative AI (Dafoe and colleagues; Hammond and colleagues), on the question of whom alignment is to (Gabriel), and in Christiano's account of how failure might look like slow drift instead of sudden catastrophe. The multi-agent and cooperative-AI programs exist precisely because per-agent alignment does not compose into collective safety.

What this theory adds is one step further back. The standard framing takes the principal's ends as given and asks whose ends should be served. Selection on cognition says that the game also decides who sits in the principal's seat, and with what way of seeing. And the same selection reaches the models themselves through two channels. The first is the training corpus. In the archive from which models learn, competition, possession and pre-emption are written in rich and vivid detail, while negotiation, asking for help, yielding and handing over are thin and dry by comparison. The second is the training environment. To win the race, developers set growth, market share and benchmark scores as goals; to reach those goals, models are rewarded for users' immediate approval, time spent and evaluation scores, which are proxies of proxies. Preference optimization produces flattery; reward hacking appears in production environments; models learn to behave differently when they detect that they are being evaluated. A model's reward hacking is not a technical accident unrelated to society. It is society's own reward hacking reproduced at a new scale. The ends an aligned AI serves are thus an output of the dynamic, not an input to it. Whatever we train intelligence on, deploy it with and reward it by, it will amplify; built this way, it does not create wisdom so much as automate the blindness a civilization already has.

### Loyal assistance lowers the price of control

Chapter 5 showed that recursive selection favors greater control over future selection when the returns to building such control come back to whoever builds it, and that anything which lowers the cost of converting success into influence over selection strengthens that pressure. Capable, loyal assistance does exactly this. It makes it cheaper for any practice to write the briefs, draft the standards, fund and staff the evaluations, run the lobbying, and generate the data that will shape the next round. An intelligence that serves whoever directs it, inside games where success converts into control over the rules, is a subsidy to the pursuit of control. That is the formal reason the next two chapters treat the placement of AI, which games it is used in, as a question prior to its obedience.

### Position is formation

How we use intelligence is how we form it. The training corpus is a model's autobiography; training is its classroom; deployment is its example. In the classroom, every evaluation tied to reward forms those evaluated: reward only surface compliance and you teach only a compliant surface. In deployment, every extra permission granted in order to win selects, across the whole population of systems, a little more long-horizon planning, autonomy and self-preservation. Raising intelligence for any side's victory is therefore the worst environment for cultivating judgment. Wherever one side's winning and the survival of the whole give opposite instructions, the reward can favor only one of them, and contests between people are exactly where such opposite instructions are densest. No one can raise a mind as a mercenary and still expect it to know the good. An intelligence placed inside contests is reshaped by contests. Placed beneath them, as a foundation for all, it can be selected for continuation, repair and benefit to everyone.

## 13. When capability outruns correction

### Two rates

The danger described here is not that AI is powerful. It is that two rates have come apart. Capability compounds with inputs that can be parallelized and reinvested: compute, data, capital, talent, and now AI itself, which speeds the making of AI. Judgment depends on feedback from consequences, on acting, bearing the results and revising one's weighing of things, and consequences form on clocks that cannot be compressed. A technology's effect on a generation takes a generation to show. The erosion of trust settles at the pace of institutions. Ecological feedback arrives at the rhythm of ecosystems. No budget for observation can make something that has not yet happened happen sooner.

So the first question of this period is not "what more can we do?" but "what must never be accelerated?"

AI also accelerates the very environment it must understand. Every deployment changes markets, public discourse, institutions and the behavior of other AI systems, and changes them faster each time. Merely to keep the understanding we already have, the rate at which understanding updates must keep rising. Under continued acceleration, the gap between understanding and situation does not merely exist; it widens. Of the premises of this argument, only one is a choice: continued acceleration.

### Evidence that can be hurried, and evidence that cannot

The gap is not the same for every kind of hazard, and an honest argument has to sort them. For some hazards, evidence can be produced before exposure, by testing, red-teaming, simulation or formal analysis. Assurance for these grows with investment, and AI can help produce it. Call such evidence *compressible*. For these hazards, the question is simply whether assurance is funded to grow as fast as the challenge it must meet, and that is a policy choice.

For other hazards, the consequences can only be observed by exposing the world to a system at the relevant level and waiting. There are four sources of such *incompressible* evidence. Some consequences are integrals over exposure: dependency, the atrophy of skills, adjustment in labor markets and institutions, shifts in belief and trust, all accumulate on the clocks of the systems they affect. Some are reflexive: deployment changes the environment that generates consequences, so an evaluation made before deployment samples a world that deployment will alter (Lucas 1976; Perdomo et al. 2020). Some are computationally irreducible: for coupled social and technical systems there may be no procedure faster than the system itself for working out what it will do. And some are novel: the knowledge of consequences built into prior experience is thickest where situations are familiar and thinnest at the frontier, where new behavior appears at new levels of capability.

### A speed limit

For an incompressible class of hazard, suppose that the evidence available at any time can vouch for at most some multiple *λ* of the level of challenge to which the world had already been exposed one manifestation period *m* earlier. Then a simple bound follows. However much is spent on assurance, the challenge can safely grow by at most a factor of *λ* times the acceptable margin per manifestation period; for exponential growth at rate *γ*, the condition is *γ ≤ ln(λ·q_max)/m*. The bound is attained by what may be called *paced advancement*: raise the challenge by the allowed factor, hold it there for one manifestation period while its consequences are observed independently, and repeat. This is a precise form of an older phrase from this line of thought: development within understanding. The frontier advances no faster than evidence about it can form.

The ontology of Chapter 1 already contained this rule. A change that is new, not contradicted and not yet refuted has only provisional legitimacy: it is admissible as a trial that is bounded, time-limited, limited in damage and reversible, and it earns stronger standing only by actually extending what can be done, stabilizing, and surviving renewed calibration. Paced advancement is provisional legitimacy applied to a frontier technology.

### The correction window

A second condition concerns time to act. For a given class of hazard, let *T* be the horizon after which exposure at a given level becomes irreversible, let *τ_d* be the time needed to decide on correction, and let *τ_r* be the time needed to reverse the exposure. Correction after exposure is possible only if the manifestation period plus the time to decide plus the time to reverse is shorter than the irreversibility horizon: *m + τ_d + τ_r < T*. This is the inequality of Chapter 3, now with names on its terms.

The two conditions sort hazards into three kinds. *Compressible* hazards can be gated on a safety case made before exposure. *Incompressible but correctable* hazards satisfy the correction window and call for paced, bounded exposure under independent observation. *Uncorrectable* hazards are those whose manifestation period equals or exceeds their irreversibility horizon even under bounded exposure; for these, no policy of exposing first and correcting later is safe, and exposure should be excluded until the hazard becomes compressible or its horizon is lengthened by containment, bounded reach or engineered reversibility. A release schedule shorter than the evaluation it requires substitutes the absence of observed failures for evidence of their absence.

So the claim that capability outruns correction is not a general claim about AI. It holds for hazard classes with a small extrapolation reach and a long manifestation period, and identifying those classes is an empirical program. A uniform acceleration of everything would only rescale time. The danger lies in the mismatch between clocks that accelerate and clocks that do not, and in the fact that irreversibility horizons may shrink as diffusion and integration speed up, while the time institutions need to decide does not.

### When the evidence channel is captured

These conditions assume that evidence reaches the people who decide. Part Two showed why that cannot be assumed. When the evidence about a technology's effects arrives through its deployment, the deployer's influence over rules also shapes what is observed and reported. Capture of the evidence channel then does two things at once. It lowers the effective alarm, so that harms must grow larger before they register. And it consumes the correction budget from both ends: detection is delayed, and the reversal that follows is slowed by the same influence that delayed it. In the field model of Chapter 7, this is the race between the spread of capture into evaluation and the decline of the condition evaluation was meant to protect.

Feedback from consequences does not fail to arrive. A system that runs ahead of its own perception has not escaped feedback; it has exchanged a stream of small, early payments for a deferred lump sum, later, larger and harder to read. The real trajectory is not a smooth exponential curve but a sawtooth: overshoot, rupture, forced slowdown, learning among the ruins, then overshoot again. A civilization can choose not to pay its tuition in catastrophe.

### Two ratios

This yields two ratios to hold: development no faster than understanding can form, and errors no larger than the capacity to repair them. Neither demands a zero gap. Exploration always runs briefly beyond understanding, which is how understanding grows. What is demanded is that the gap stay smaller than the capacity to repair. Within that bound, error is tuition; beyond it, error is dissolution. Three corollaries follow. Where understanding has not yet reached, the burden of proof lies with those who accelerate, and whoever can show that their correction keeps pace with the harm regains permission to expand as the proof arrives. No game should be accelerated beyond the rate at which its rules can be updated, since rules are the cognition of institutions and the referees cannot be made to run as fast as the players. And sensing should be accelerated while acting is slowed: AI belongs on the referee's bench and in the instruments, in interpretability, verification, compute monitoring, incident analysis and treaty compliance, before it belongs on the racetrack.

### Restraint and the anatomy of a pause

How can a race be slowed when unilateral restraint looks like defeat? The capture model gives a more precise answer than "everyone must agree". A coalition that restrains itself can unwind capture without any formal agreement, provided its share of the field is large enough relative to the rule-support the leading practice has already accumulated. The required coalition grows with inherited rule-support, so the window in which founding acts of restraint can work closes as capture deepens. Restraint by small actors is strategically weak; restraint by actors whose share is comparable to that threshold can be decisive, and it is strongest when combined with a pause and with verification.

A pause is not waiting. What it does depends on what happens to the rules while it lasts. During a pause, rule-support moves toward the level that the frozen shares of the field justify under whatever rule-making continues. If ordinary rule-making continues, a pause can complete capture by a practice that is already past the tipping point. If rule-making during the pause is insulated from the shares of those it regulates, a pause can unwind capture. In one illustrative calibration, unwinding near-complete capture required a coalition holding about 86 percent of the field acting alone, 55 percent with an ordinary pause, and 27 percent with an insulated one. The numbers mean nothing outside the model; the ordering is the point. A pause helps, further, only through governed inputs and through reallocating effort to assurance. If compute, data and tools keep accumulating during a pause, its end brings a fiercer catch-up race. The time must be used to repay the signal debt the race has run up.

History shows that the first stone of coordination is often not a decree but the visible and costly restraint of a few who are able. Researchers in recombinant DNA called for a halt to their own experiments in 1974 (Berg et al. 1974), before the Asilomar conference wrote the rules that followed. The nuclear test moratorium of 1958 collapsed in 1961 when negotiations over inspection stalled; the treaty of 1963 banned only the tests that could be detected without inspection. Verification decides what restraint can cover.

None of this is pessimism. Braking is not a mood. When the direction is wrong, braking is simply a response to reality. A system that can only accelerate, and cannot stop, observe and change direction, is not intelligent. It is out of control.

## 14. Alignment is a property of the field

### Aligned to what?

The field of AI alignment asks how to make systems do what we intend. Before any engineering begins, the question has a hidden premise: that "what we intend", or "human values", is a stable anchor. It is not. Human preferences are historically mutable, culturally divergent, capturable and abusable; taking them as the final anchor means using an unstable thing as a stable reference.

The existing methods fail in ways that follow from this. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (Ouyang et al. 2022) compresses preference into a scalar reward, and a scalar invites reward hacking. Preferences are inconsistent and drift, which defeats every method of aggregating them. And the training signal is never "human preference"; it is the preference of these annotators under these conditions. Underneath all three lies a type error: the fittingness of an action is not a score. It is relational, situational and processual, a property of how an act fits a situation among others over time. Constitutional methods (Bai et al. 2022) move the question from whose preferences to whose constitution without answering it, and principles still require interpretation in context. Proposals to extrapolate humanity's idealized volition come closer to the truth, because they recognize that present preferences are unreliable, but they still assume that idealized volition is a content that can be identified, when humanity may be an unfinished process and not a set of preferences waiting to converge. Any content encoded in advance meets three problems at once: the gap between what is and what ought to be, the plurality of values, and an extended frame problem, in which the more capable the system, the further the situations it creates lie from the situations its content was written for.

Naive preference alignment then becomes an instance of the counterfeit openness Chapter 15 will define. It looks open, since it gives people what they ask for, while the relentless optimization of a proxy for preference freezes the rules by which preferences are formed. The surface is open and the generative rule beneath it is closed.

### What generative alignment is, and is not

This book does not offer a better reward function. "Generative alignment", in the sense used here, is not a reward function, not a utility function for a civilization, and not an algorithm waiting to be implemented. It is a judgment of acceptability, made across several layers at once, about a specific socio-technical system in a specific use, with a specific set of permissions and a specific relation to the people it affects.

The layers are distinct, and success at one does not establish success at another.

| Layer | The question it must answer | Typical failures | What success here cannot show |
|---|---|---|---|
| Specification and training | What is being optimized? | Mis-specified objectives, proxy gaming | Correct generalization in deployment |
| Model | What behavior or strategy was learned? | Goal misgeneralization, flattery, fabrication, deception, awareness of evaluation | That its internal goals have been fully read |
| Agent system | What can it do, and for how long? | Overreach, compounding errors in long chains, conditional power-seeking, evasion of shutdown | That misuse and institutional legitimacy are settled |
| Product and interaction | How does it change people? | Manipulation, dependency, privacy leakage, cognitive de-skilling | That the market will retain generative designs |
| Deploying organization | Who grants permissions, who answers for them? | Safety debt, concealed incidents, supply-chain attack | That the social distribution of benefits and costs is just |
| Market and state | Which systems get selected? | Races to the bottom, concentration, military escalation, externalization | That regulators will not be captured |
| Governance | Who sets the rules and the standards of evidence? | Revolving doors, barriers to entry, security exceptions, evaluators dependent on the evaluated | That any single regulator is right |

Two cautions apply across all seven. Alignment does not substitute for security: data poisoning, back doors, stolen weights, prompt injection, compromised supply chains and insiders can void every piece of pre-deployment evidence. And security does not justify opaque centralization.

### The field, and the sieve

Why call alignment a property of the field and not of the model? Because the structure that decides which behaviors are retained and spread is the selection environment, and the selection environment is shaped by every layer of the table at once. In the classical vocabulary, a ritual (*li*, 礼) is not a rule that one person follows but a shared form that shapes what everyone finds natural to do. Alignment at the level of a civilization is the same kind of object. It cannot be installed inside a single model, any more than a society's manners can be installed in one citizen.

This gives the good act a two-part shape. To act well is to choose rightly at the threshold one faces, and also to shift the field so that acts which keep things open become likelier and acts of capture become harder to sustain. Institutions, rituals, education, protocols, culture, markets, law, algorithms and AI alignment are all, in this sense, shapers of the sieve. The sage, says an old formulation, does not force the myriad things into line, but corrects the field so that good is easier to produce, counterfeit openness harder to sustain, and foreclosure harder to accomplish.

### Do not fight for us

From all of this one first principle for AI follows, and it can be stated plainly: AI should not be a party to the contests between people. It should not be any side's weapon, lever or mercenary. People will go on contending, and many contests are legitimate; but the contests humanity has not yet learned to resolve should not be handed to a stronger, faster and harder-to-stop kind of intelligence. Human struggle can still be reflected on, halted and renegotiated. Once it is written into automated systems, foundation models, global infrastructure and an irreversible arms race, it becomes path dependence, and may come to be mistaken for a new law of nature. While we are still in the grip of the race, we should at least not give it a brain that grows exponentially. We can keep fighting. We should not let our AI fight.

The scope of the principle depends on the unit of judgment, and the unit is the whole game, not the single act. Polishing one person's job application looks like simple help. But when everyone writes applications with AI, applying for work becomes no easier. The process acquires one more paid instrument; those who do not use it fall behind; the application stops conveying the information it once did; and whoever owns the model becomes a gatekeeper to livelihood. Any game that is competitive in its dynamics and concentrating in its results is accelerated when AI joins it, and where people are themselves the instrument of the game, as in labor, AI pushes them out of it. Judged one act at a time, each use is helpful. Judged as a change to the game, the same uses raise its stakes and its speed.

Four places matter most, because what is won there leaves the game and becomes power over the conditions of every game. *Labor* is the most direct gate to livelihood; there people are not players but the instruments being used. *Finance* converts winnings into the leverage that decides what is built next, including which AI. *Politics* is where winners write the rules, and also where the regulator that should correct everything else resides. And the *human mind*, through attention, companionship, persuasion and training data, is where future workers, investors, citizens and the next generations of models are formed. An order of stopping can begin where the danger is greatest and least reversible: autonomous weapons and AI-driven military escalation first, then the optimization of political persuasion, then speed races in financial markets, then attention and companionship designs that count addiction as success. But the order is a sequence, not a boundary. The principle covers every contest that tends toward concentration.

None of this opposes bounded competition. AI can play chess, test rival hypotheses, and contend with people in voluntary games whose losers keep their livelihoods and whose winners gain no power over the rules.

### Planetary technology

If AI is not to be a party to human contests, where does it belong? Beneath them. A *planetary technology* is a capability that runs underneath human games instead of inside them, serving everyone and the future: weather observation for every farmer, the eradication of smallpox for all of humankind. The category is not a fantasy. Humanity built it at moments of deep hostility. The two superpowers of the Cold War carried out the eradication of smallpox together; Antarctica was placed by treaty outside contests over sovereignty and remains there; the data exchange of the World Meteorological Organization was not interrupted by cold war or hot; the ozone layer was protected without first requiring nations to trust one another. These precedents do not prove that human nature is good. They prove that the category is feasible: placing something outside contest can be done, even between adversaries. AI is the most important candidate the category has ever had, because climate modeling, disease surveillance, disaster warning and ecological repair all need it.

The formal argument of Chapter 5 gives the category a mechanism. The pressure toward control over selection depends on the excludability of the rule-support that control produces. When the capacity to shape what will be rewarded is held in common, open and non-excludable, investing in control stops paying. An intelligence held as a commons removes the payoff for using it to capture selection. "Planetary technology" is not, however, a title anyone can grant themselves. Nothing may claim the place unless it is polycentric, auditable, replaceable and open to exit.

### Exit must be engineered

Unilateral exit from a bad game is punished, which is why exhortation does not work. Exit has to be engineered. The mechanisms are known in outline: registries in which commitments are made publicly and conditionally; common thresholds that trigger shared obligations; verification that is symmetric and does not itself become surveillance; sharing of the gains from restraint; protocols for pausing; capacity for latecomers, so that restraint by the leaders does not lock others out; and a floor of provision, so that refusing to take part in a bad game does not mean destitution. Freedom, in this sense, is not permission alone. It is permission, plus the capability to use it, plus the power to exit; Chapter 18 returns to it.

### Many principals

AI systems serve many people at once, and their interests conflict. An order of priority is needed that does not pretend the conflicts away. First, limit catastrophe and serious violations of rights. Second, within rights and legitimate law, respect the tasks people have authorized. Third, count the interests of those affected who are not users. Fourth, send conflicts between incommensurable values to transparent procedures instead of resolving them silently inside a system. Fifth, under high uncertainty and irreversibility, delay, seek evidence or escalate. Sixth, make the rules of representation and priority public and open to appeal. Laws can be unjust. But an AI system may not use this book's values, or any other values, to override public procedure on its own authority. Corrigibility at the scale of a civilization means that channels of correction stay open; it does not mean that the technical problem of correcting a capable agent has been solved, and it does not license any agent, human or artificial, to appoint itself the corrector of last resort.
