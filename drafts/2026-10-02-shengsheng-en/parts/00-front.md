## A note on this edition

This is not a translation. *Shengsheng buxi lun* (生生不息论), the theory of unceasing generation, was first worked out in Chinese between May and August 2026, in a series of formal treatises, a twenty-chapter book, an English companion volume, a plan for a stricter second version, and a proof layer. Since then the same ideas have been carried further in other places: in an account of how selection comes to capture its own rules, in an argument about the timing of capability and correction, in a model of productive capacity that can reproduce itself, and in a statement of what artificial intelligence should and should not be used for. This edition restates the theory in English and brings that later work into it, so that the argument can be followed in one place from its first premise to its last consequence.

It follows a discipline set for the second version. The argument describes before it evaluates: Parts One and Two explain how finite processes continue and how a direction comes to reproduce itself without appealing to good or evil, and the normative commitment is declared openly in Part Five, not smuggled in earlier. A reader should be able to restate the whole chain without using any Chinese term. The Chinese terms appear where they keep something English loses, and each is glossed where it first appears; Appendix D collects them. Appendix B lines up the theory's several vocabularies for closure and its several sets of criteria, which grew up separately. Appendix C says what would change the theory.

The ideas and positions are Ren's. The English text was drafted with AI assistance from his corpus.

## Prologue: The world is not yet finished

Consider three things that are happening at once.

A seed lies in damp soil. It is not yet anything it will be. Water enters it, a root finds a crack, a shoot turns toward light. If it grows, the tree will drop leaves that become soil for other roots, shade that changes which plants can live beneath it, fruit that feeds animals who carry its seed somewhere else. The seed is not a small, finished object waiting to get bigger. It is a process that changes what can happen next, both for itself and for everything around it.

Two people make a promise. Each closes a road: neither will back out when backing out becomes convenient. The closing is the point. Because those roads are shut, others open: they can plan together, rely on each other, build something that takes longer than either could manage alone. A promise is a limit, and it is a limit that makes things possible.

An artificial intelligence system goes into service this week. It will answer millions of questions. Its answers will become people's beliefs, their documents, their code and their decisions. Some of those will enter the archive from which the next system is trained, so that the next system learns from a world the first one helped to make. It, too, is not a finished thing. It is a process that changes what can happen next, at a scale and a speed that no seed or promise has.

None of the three is a static object. Each takes up what the world offers, gives back something that was not there before, and alters the conditions under which what comes after will form. This book is about that kind of process: how it continues, how it fails, and what can be done to keep it going. Its question is this. How does an unfinished world keep becoming, through life, minds, societies and technology? And how can it lose the capacity to become otherwise, not through stupidity or malice in the first place, but through processes that are highly successful, highly rational, and increasingly intelligent?

The second half of the question is the urgent one. We live at a moment when power is increasing faster than direction. Human beings can alter landscapes, redesign living processes, connect the planet into one system of exchange and signal, and build forms of intelligence that act, judge and create at a scale no person or institution has had before. The growth of capability has not produced a matching growth of wisdom. The more efficiently we can pursue a target, the more it matters whether the target is consuming the world that makes its pursuit possible. The danger this book describes is not a single catastrophe arriving from outside. It is a gradual and often comfortable loss: of the capacity to notice what is going wrong, to refuse, to repair, to replace what has failed, and to produce kinds of value that no one present can yet imagine. That loss can proceed while everything visible looks like progress.

The argument comes in two halves, and the order matters. The first half describes. It explains how finite processes depend on wider conditions in order to persist; how the consequences of an action can come apart from the place where the action is decided, so that local success rests on damage that never returns to the decider; how differential persistence creates a selection environment before anyone competes; how success can buy the power to shape the next round of selection; how environments form the very participants who will be selected; and how artificial intelligence enters this ecology and changes its speed. None of that requires a word about good or evil. It is offered as mechanism, with the conditions under which each step holds and the evidence that would show it does not.

Only then does the second half make a commitment, and it makes it openly. It holds that centers capable of feeling, caring, knowing, acting, creating and valuing matter; that the shared conditions which let such centers arise, respond to one another, find their errors and produce unforeseen value matter too; and that no process acquires the right to close other sources of value merely by being stronger, larger, more stable or more successful. The book does not claim to squeeze this commitment out of facts. It shows what is destroyed when it is denied, why those affected have reason to resist the denial, and why anyone who still asks that their own reasons and future be respected has already appealed to it. From that commitment it draws out what generation asks of persons, of institutions, and of the intelligences we are now building.

Some things this book is not. It is not a complete metaphysics, and it does not claim that everything is information or that physics has a direction. It is not a proof that one morality follows from nature. It is not an algorithm for maximizing some quantity called generativity; one of its central claims is that the good cannot be stated as the maximization of anything. It is not a claim about the single original meaning of Chinese tradition, which it uses as a practical resource and not as an authority. It is not a utility function that a world government could execute, not a substitute for the technical work of making AI systems safe, and not a teleology in which history is bound to arrive somewhere good.

The name of the theory is *shengsheng buxi* (生生不息). *Sheng* (生) means more than biological birth: to arise, to grow, to bring forth, to become able, to open a way. *Shengsheng*, the word doubled, means generation that gives rise to generation: what has come to be becomes a condition of what comes next. *Buxi* (不息) means not ceasing. It does not mean that any particular form should last forever. Leaves fall, cells die, theories are refuted, institutions are dissolved, and generation depends on all of that. It means that the source of further forming is not exhausted, the way of transformation is not sealed, and those who come later can still inherit, repair and create. An old-style Chinese formulation puts the three conditions in three short phrases: do not cut off the source, do not block the flow, do not destroy what comes after (不绝其源，不塞其流，不毁其后).

A theory of openness that could not itself be corrected would be its own best counterexample. So the book writes down, at the end, what would change it.
