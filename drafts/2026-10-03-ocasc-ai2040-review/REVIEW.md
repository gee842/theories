# Review: "What Makes US–China AI Cooperation Credible?"

**Paper:** OCASC Research Review, "What Makes US–China AI Cooperation Credible? Rethinking the Incentives and Enforcement of AI 2040's Plan A". Second draft, October 2026, 32 pages. Source file: `~/Downloads/AI2040 review 2nd.pdf`.

**Review:** 2026-10-03. Three reviewers worked in parallel, each in one pass:

- Argument and strategy. This reviewer read the relevant parts of ai-2040.com.
- Fact and citation check. About 60 claims were checked against primary sources.
- Editorial and figures. This reviewer read all 32 pages as images.

The synthesis and §4 are mine. I checked two items myself:

- the Plan A quotations used below, against the page text;
- the CAISI rename, by fetching nist.gov on 2026-10-03.

---

## Verdict: major revision

**Strengths.** The paper is original and useful on what Plan-A-style verification would need in order to work inside China:

- lawful access paths under DSL/PIPL;
- the CAC's October 2025 ruling on viewing data on site;
- the CII review;
- the symmetric US deemed-export rules;
- local custody, and the separation of evidence from authority.

The evidence taxonomy, the seeded-failure pilot and the point about convertible capacity are also strong. The facts are unusually clean.

**Weaknesses.** The paper is weak on its own title question.

- Part I attacks two positions Plan A does not hold: that MACD is a discretionary strike, and that a US lead secures Chinese agreement.
- Part I also misses Plan A's own admissions: impairment, the US advantage on exit, and Taiwan.
- Part II replaces enforcement with reciprocity and a small recurring benefit, then concedes that it may be too slow.
- Caveats bury the claims. Roughly one sentence in four or five carries a "not X" disclaimer, and the Conclusions end on one.

**The author must choose which paper this is.**

- **(A) Keep the title.** Then the paper needs four things:
  - a stated theory of credibility;
  - a response ladder built from its own evidential states;
  - a bargaining analysis from both sides;
  - a real emergency branch.
- **(B) Retitle to what it delivers.** For example, "Implementation conditions for Plan A-style verification in China". Part II then becomes the core and Part I shrinks to a framing section.

The current material supports (B). Option (A) is a substantial rewrite.

## 1. Argument

The problems are ranked by how much each one weakens the paper.

### 1.1 No theory of credibility (pp. 2, 18–19, 28)

Credibility means that complying, and punishing, are in each actor's interest after the fact. The paper never states such a theory, and Track 2 concedes both halves of it:

- "the partner cannot force a refusing host to comply" (p. 18);
- the benefit "may be far too weak" (p. 19).

The Summary's list covers verifiability and legitimacy, not enforcement.

**Fix:** state a thesis and judge both designs against it, or change the title. For example, cooperation is credible when:

- (a) violations are observable with known error rates;
- (b) a pre-agreed response ladder makes each response rational at each evidential state;
- (c) staying is worth more than exit to the party with the best exit option.

### 1.2 MACD analysed as the wrong kind of threat (pp. 5–10)

Plan A's MACD works in three ways:

- **It is automatic.** Chips "become bricks" unless the regular "continue" messages keep arriving.
- **It is a hostage exchange.** Chinese R&D sits in Canada and US R&D in Mongolia.
- **It is a denial device.** If the new compute survived, takeoff "would have been several times faster" (Plan A text, verified).

The paper notes this on p. 7, then analyses a discretionary strike anyway. Two passages simply repeat Plan A:

- Fig. 4's "released overhang" objection is Plan A's own rationale for MACD.
- The graduated responses on p. 9 repeat Plan A's escalation ladder.

**Fix:** analyse the real problems.

- Withholding "continue" gives each side a veto it can use for blackmail.
- Accidents or hacks could trigger destruction.
- Stability–instability: a catastrophic threshold makes cheating below it safer.
- Erosion: as AI dependence grows, the hostage grows, and so does pressure to weaken the mechanism in advance. This is the paper's best point, and it gets one sentence on p. 10. Make it the centre.

### 1.3 §1.1 rebuts a claim Plan A does not make (pp. 3–4)

Plan A calls the US lead "one extra reason" for China to come to the table (verified). Two things go wrong:

- **The argument backfires.** The DSA chain in Fig. 2 lowers China's stakes in any deal, the paper's Track 2 included.
- **It is one-sided.** It looks only at China. As the US lead grows, China's willingness to deal rises while America's falls; this is Powell's shifting-power problem. Plan A's research transparency is also a large transfer from the US.

**Fix:** analyse the bargaining range from both sides.

### 1.4 The analogies and the matrix do not fit (pp. 4–7)

- **North Korea supports Plan A.** It is a patron coercing a client, with no commitment device. It shows why discretionary threats fail, which is why Plan A built in automaticity.
- **The p. 6 matrix treats the true state as a move by nature.** The violator chooses that state. If the enforcer is known to hesitate on ambiguous evidence, violations will be designed to stay in evidential states 1–2.
- **NORAD 1980 is a false-positive story.** It cuts against the paper's argument about reluctance to act. It earns its place only as *dual phenomenology*: escalate only when two independent sensor types agree.

**Fix:** replace these with arms-control compliance cases:

- the Krasnoyarsk radar;
- INF/9M729 (2014–19);
- the JCPOA snapback (2025);
- Biopreparat.

Then merge the matrix, the three evidential states and the graduated responses into **one table**: evidential state, response, and the corroboration rule required. That table would be a genuine contribution to Plan A.

### 1.5 Track 1 has no causal path to Track 2 (pp. 11, 21–22, Fig. 5)

- **Weak trust-building.** Tabletops and reproducibility reports are cheap signals. A state planning to cheat can send them as easily as an honest one (Kydd), so they build little trust.
- **The wrong flagship channel.** The CNCERT/CISA pairing sits in the most securitised domain there is: the attribution politics of Volt Typhoon and Salt Typhoon.
- **The risk** is cooperation theatre.

**Fix:** a GRIT-style ladder of costlier reciprocated steps, with explicit gates into Track 2:

- the test harness sets Track 2's thresholds;
- log reconciliation grows into workload accounting;
- the incident channel becomes the notification route for evidential state 1.

### 1.6 The retention incentive is mis-specified (§2.2.4, p. 19)

- **Wrong standard.** "No assured payoff" is not the test. Exit turns on expected value.
- **Wrong motive.** A benefit can deter exit driven by greed, but not exit driven by fear. Only verification addresses fear.
- **Wrong scale.** The benefit is far too small for the stakes, as the paper itself admits.
- **Tencent is a nonstarter.** A Tencent-hosted service for US defenders will not fly: Tencent has been on the Pentagon's 1260H list since January 2025.
- **It ignores Plan A's own side-payments,** which are far larger: research transparency, and equal in-deal R&D clusters. The paper's own p. 8 illustration shows those clusters.

**Fix:** benefits on the scale of the stakes, revocable by snapback. One example is reciprocal export-control relief: chips and tools in exchange for rare earths and magnets.

### 1.7 The pace concession undercuts the design, and the trigger is a placeholder (pp. 24–26)

- **Timing.** Table 1's pilots run to 2028. That is the window the paper's own METR extrapolation flags (fn 58).
- **Shadow of the future.** Host enforcement plus reciprocity needs a long shadow of the future. Fast takeoff removes it, and Plan A chose hostages for exactly this reason. So §2.4 undercuts the design, not just its timing.
- **The "any time: risk trigger / do not wait" row is a placeholder.** It relies on "lawful" restrictions that, by the paper's own account (pp. 20–21), do not exist for nonpublic frontier training:
  - China's GenAI Measures exclude it;
  - EO 14409 disclaims licensing.

**Fix:**

- name the trigger metrics;
- name the response, for example notice within 72 hours and a standstill on named runs pending joint review;
- name the legal authority, or make obtaining it an explicit legislative ask;
- consider Plan A's crude inference-only halt as the emergency branch.

### 1.8 Plan A vulnerabilities the paper misses

A China-based paper is best placed to press these.

- **Impairment, not dissolution.** Lifland's *Deal Decline* puts decline at about 37% within 5 years and about 48% by 2040, roughly half of it impairment. Impairment does not trigger compute destruction. This is stability–instability, quantified by Plan A's own authors. The paper cites Deal Decline only for its three evidential states, and fact-check note 3 below finds they are not there.
  - *Checked 2026-10-03 against the supplement:* 37% within 5 years and 48% within 10, split evenly between dissolution and impairment. The figure of about 51% in the v4 direction notes is off.
- **The US has the better exit option.**
  - Cold storage follows pre-deal ratios: about 5M H100e for the US against about 500k for China.
  - In-deal R&D is split equally.
  - So dissolution restores US superiority, and Deal Decline reportedly concedes that the arrangement favours the US.
  - US executive agreements are also fragile: the US left the JCPOA in 2018, and the 2032 and 2036 elections fall inside the deal's span.
- **Taiwan.** Plan A's own dissolution scenario starts from a war over Taiwan, in which "perhaps one side is okay with" destruction (verified). MACD does not deter exit motivated by war. The paper never mentions Taiwan.
- **Power-based adjudication.** Bans pass by majority "weighted by bargaining clout" (verified). The authors call research titration a weaker part of the plan.
- **Entry cost.** Without progress on verification, about 90% of frontier services go offline for 6–12 months (Verification Plan). This is the real obstacle to the initial halt.

### 2.1 Must fix

  - Fix: "CAISI (renamed CAISSI, Sept 2026)".

### 2.2 Tighten

1. **NORAD (p. 6).** The 46-cent chip was the Pentagon's attribution. Secretary of Defense Brown told Carter that NORAD could not get the suspected circuit to fail again. Write "attributed by the Pentagon to…". Cite the source as National Security Archive Briefing Book #699, an update of BB #371.
2. **"Chips require continuing authorisation" (p. 7).** In Plan A the "continue" messages apply to the new transparent training datacenters, not to all chips.
3. **fn 12.** The three evidential states do not appear in Deal Decline. Present them as the author's own typology.
4. **EO 14409 (p. 20, fn 48).** The order has three relevant parts:
   - Sec. 3(a) is a *classified, mandatory* benchmarking process run by NSA, Treasury and CISA;
   - Sec. 3(b) is a voluntary framework for developer access;
   - Sec. 3(c) disclaims licensing and preclearance.
   "Directed voluntary federal assessment" undersells it.
5. **fn 60.** Whitfill & Cunningham is a *METR research note* that summarises a paper with seven further co-authors. It carries a disclaimer that it does not necessarily represent METR's views. Cite it as a research note, not as "a METR analysis".
6. **Hugging Face incident (p. 13).** One agent escaped. It reached a third party's endpoint hosted on Modal, then Hugging Face. Modal's own infrastructure was not compromised. "Evaluation agents… compromised third-party systems" is loose.
7. **METR TH1.1.** The doubling time is 130.8 days, with an interval of 107–161. The same post reports **89 days since 2024**. Adding it strengthens the pace argument.
8. **SB 53.** The duties sit in §22757.12 (framework and transparency reports) and §22757.13 (incident reporting: 15 days, or 24 hours for imminent risk). §.14 is a definitional review and §.15 covers penalties.
9. **fn 37, "footnote 54".** That number is from the web edition. The same note is no. 29 in the PDF edition, so state which edition is meant. In this paper, fn 54 is a different note.
10. **MOFCOM/GACC Announcement No. 18 of 2025 (fn 44, fn 84).** This is the rare-earth export-control announcement. Say that it is cited only for the bureau name in its issuer line, or cite an organisation page instead.

## 4. For Ren: how this paper relates to your AI 2040 critique

This section compares the paper with the critique in `sources/16-claude-code-directions/2026-10-03-v4-direction-cooperation.md`. The two point in **opposite directions.**

- **The paper** argues that Plan A is too coercive and too intrusive to be credible or acceptable. It proposes narrower, consent-based, lawful measures and admits they give less assurance.
- **Your critique** argues that verification changes the probability of being caught (q), not the value of winning (G). Deterrence needs q ≥ G/(G+L), and under DSA beliefs G is large. In the trust form: if T > R, no level of trust suffices. Verification lowers P−S but cannot get anyone across the threshold. Only shared understanding that no one can win pushes T below R.

**Where they overlap:**

- **Convertible capacity (p. 15)** is your point that the lid must grow with the pressure.
- **Erosion under dependence (p. 10)** is your point that the pressure is held down, not removed.
- **Separating evidence from authority** sits near your point that the guardians are themselves a field. The paper treats it as institutional design, not as a second-order correction problem.
- **The §2.4 concession** is your "insufficient". The paper states it as a caveat, not as its thesis.

**What is missing:** the value of winning (G, or T) is never on the table. Fig. 2's DSA chain comes closest. It argues that a lead may not convert into dominance, decisive advantage or usable coercion, which is really an argument that G is smaller than racers believe. That is the seed of Theorem A. The paper uses it only to say that China need not sign.

If OCASC wants this paper to carry the community's position, that is the lever. Turn Fig. 2 from "China needn't accept Plan A's terms" into "neither side can bank on winning". Then the paper's conclusion becomes that T < R is a precondition verification cannot supply. That move would also answer reviewer point 1.3, because it makes the argument two-sided.
