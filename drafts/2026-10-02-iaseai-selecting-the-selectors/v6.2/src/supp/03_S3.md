## S3. One system

### Hosts harsher than the forgiving one

The host of Section 3.1 is forgiving: per unit it regenerates fastest when most depleted, and it has no threshold of its own. Real shared conditions, such as trust, institutions, information environments and skills, are closer to hosts that degrade by themselves below a threshold, and they are vectors of coupled components. For a threshold host a load later removed can leave it collapsed, and with two coupled components collapse can begin at half the load at which one would fail while their average reads as healthy. Comparison results transfer in one direction only. A *harsher* host, one whose regeneration per unit is no greater than $\rho(1-V)$ at every level, such as $\rho_A(V/A-1)(1-V)$ with $\rho_A=\rho A/(1-A)$, makes every *crossing* result hold a fortiori. *Viability* results do not transfer, and need a margin even on the forgiving host: {{safeguards}} keeps the trajectory above the floor only with a liability margin set by the host, the floor, the initial composition and the speed of selection; without it the forgiving host dips below its floor and recovers, while a host that degrades by itself below a threshold does not recover (S10). A host normalized to the same peak regeneration is not harsher in this sense, since above about a third of capacity it regenerates faster.

### Proof of Lemma {{#blind}}

(1) A relative rule satisfies $\mathcal F(p,\pi+G\mathbf 1)=\mathcal F(p,\pi)$ at every instant, so shares solve the same equation whatever $G(V(t))$ is; a common term leaves every $\pi_k-\pi_l$, and so the sign of relative growth, unchanged.

(2) An equal additive cost is a common term among those who bear it. Costs $c_k$ differing across carriers change $\pi_k-\pi_l$ by $c_l-c_k$, which a payoff-monotone rule selects on: the carrier the constraint costs least gains share among the compliers, and a carrier that alone bears a cost loses share to every other.

(3) *Within a type.* An evader of type $k$ pays the expected sanction $\bar F_k$ instead of the cost $c_k$, so its payoff exceeds that of a complier of its own type by $c_k-\bar F_k$, and under a payoff-monotone rule the evading variant grows relative to the complying one iff $c_k>\bar F_k$. *Survival and prevailing.* If $\bar F_k\ge c_k$ for every type that can evade, no evading variant grows against its own type's compliers, so the compliant share stays bounded away from zero. If every inequality is strict, then under cumulative selection ({{relsel}}), in which the log-ratio of two variants moves at least in proportion to the integral of their payoff gap, as under replicator dynamics, each evading variant's share relative to its own type's complying variant tends to zero, so the compliant share tends to one whatever the payoffs across types, which only re-sort the compliers. *Not necessary across types.* A type whose evasion pays, $c_k>\bar F_k$, is still eliminated, evaders included, if another type's compliers earn more than its evaders. *Falling sanction.* With one type and $\bar F(x)$ continuous and strictly decreasing in the evading share $x$, $\dot x=x(1-x)\big(c-\bar F(x)\big)$ under replicator dynamics, and the sign of $\dot x$ is that of $c-\bar F(x)$ under any payoff-monotone rule. A root $x^\dagger\in(0,1)$ of $\bar F(x)=c$ exists iff $\bar F(0)>c>\bar F(1)$, by the intermediate value theorem; it is unique, and unstable because $c-\bar F$ is increasing, so full compliance and full evasion are both stable and $x^\dagger$ separates their basins. Otherwise $c-\bar F$ has one sign on $(0,1)$: compliance is globally stable if $\bar F(1)\ge c$, evasion if $\bar F(0)\le c$. $\square$

*Numerics (CI).* With costs $(0.3,0.1)$ relative growth rates are $(-0.1,+0.1)$; with an equal cost 0.2, $(0,0)$; a universal cap at 0.4 on loads $(1,0.6,0.2)$ costs the three carriers $(0.6,0.2,0)$ and reverses their ranking. With $c=0.3$ evaders take over when the expected sanction is 0.25 or 0.27 and vanish when it is 0.35 or 0.4. Detection funded by compliers, $\bar F(x)=q_0(1-x)F$ with $q_0=0.8$ and $F=0.6$, gives the tipping share $1-c/(q_0F)=0.375$: starts at evading shares 0.05 and 0.2 return to full compliance, and 0.4 and 0.6 go to full evasion. The endpoints decide, as stated: with $c=0.3$, $\bar F=0.5+0.5(1-x)$, which never falls below the cost, restores compliance from every start; $0.24(1-x)$, which starts below it, leads to evasion from every start; $0.48(1-x)$ and $0.6-0.4x$ are bistable with tipping shares 0.375 and 0.75, and $1.2(1-x)$, integrated independently, with 0.75. Two types with compliance costs 0.1 and 0.2: with sanctions 1 and 1, compliance goes to one and all of it is the cheaper type, the dearer complier displaced (from equal shares the compliant share is 0.99999997 at $t=20$): incidence re-sorting the compliers; with sanctions 1 and 0.15 the second type's evasion pays, yet its evaders lose to the first type's compliers and compliance still goes to one; with sanctions 1 and 0.05 the second type's evaders earn most and evasion takes over. Equal costs leave the compliers' relative shares where they started. A common liability $\tau=1.5>\nu=1$ drives the high-load share to zero; $\tau=0.5$ drives it to one.

### Proof of Proposition {{#load}}

With $\phi_k=\phi_l$, $\pi_k-\pi_l=(\nu-\tau_{\rm eff})(e_k-e_l)>0$ and $G$ cancels. Under replicator dynamics $\dot E=\operatorname{Cov}_p(e,\pi)=(\nu-\tau_{\rm eff})\operatorname{Var}_p(e)+\operatorname{Cov}_p(e,\phi)$, so the mean load rises unless restraint is correlated strongly enough with other advantages; a rising mean does not imply that the maximal-load carrier gains share (shares $1/3$, loads $(0,0.9,1)$, other advantages $(0,10,0)$, $\nu=1$: the mean load rises at 1.09 while the maximal-load share falls at 0.99). With all $\phi_k$ equal, replicator dynamics give $\frac{d}{dt}\ln(p_k/p_l)=(e_k-e_l)(\nu-\tau_{\rm eff})$, and any cumulative rule gives at least $\varkappa$ times that while $\nu>\tau_{\rm eff}$, so $B(t)\to\infty$ sends a race carrier's share to one and $E\to\bar e$. For $\epsilon>0$, eventually $\dot V\le V[\rho(1-V)-h_V(\bar e-\epsilon)]$, so $\limsup V\le(1-h_V(\bar e-\epsilon)/\rho)^+$; let $\epsilon\to0$, and (reach) puts the bound below $V_{\min}$. Under replicator dynamics, if $B(\infty)<\infty$, each $\ln(p_k/p_l)$ converges. $\square$

*Why cumulative.* Payoff-monotonicity alone fixes the sign of relative growth, not its size, and does not carry the second sentence. Under the relative, payoff-monotone rule $\frac{d}{dt}\operatorname{logit}x=(\Delta\pi)^3$, with a race carrier of load 1 against one of load 0, $\nu=1$ and $\tau_{\rm eff}=\nu-1/(1+t)$, the cumulative advantage $B(t)=\ln(1+t)$ grows without bound, yet the race share $x$, from 0.001, never exceeds 0.0017, and the host of R1's example ($\rho=0.05$, $h_V=0.08$, floor 0.3) stays above 0.99; under replicator dynamics on the same path the share passes one half and the host falls to 0.017 by $t=2000$ [num: VF]. The rule is autonomous and of the form of {{relsel}}; what fails is the rate bound.

What carriers value decides which strategies they try, not which ones grow: this is evolutionary suicide, under stated conditions (Rankin, Bargum and Kokko 2007).

### Proof of Proposition {{#twochan}}, and measuring $\theta$

With $\Delta\bar\pi_{-i}=(n\bar\Delta-\Delta\pi_i)/(n-1)$, $\Delta J_i=\frac{n-1-\theta}{n-1}\Delta\pi_i+\frac{n\theta}{n-1}\bar\Delta$; writing $\Delta\pi_i=\bar\Delta+(\Delta\pi_i-\bar\Delta)$ the coefficient of $\bar\Delta$ is $1+\theta$. (1): each $\pi_j$ appears in $n-1$ averages, so $\sum_iJ_i=(1+\theta)\sum_i\pi_i$. (2): at $\theta=-1$ objectives sum to zero at every profile; at $\theta=n-1$ each objective is the total, so a total-maximizing profile admits no profitable deviation. (3): $\pi_k-\sum_jp_j\pi_j=(1-p_k)[\pi_k-\sum_{j\ne k}\frac{p_j}{1-p_k}\pi_j]$; and $\pi_i+\lambda(\pi_i-\bar\pi)=(1+\lambda\frac{n-1}n)\pi_i-\lambda\frac{n-1}n\bar\pi_{-i}$. (4): a rebated levy is a transfer with $\bar\Delta=0$ and own part $-\tau(n-1)/n$; withholding a promised $\beta\Omega$ from the $n-1$ others gives own part $\beta\Omega$ and $\bar\Delta=0$, hence $\beta\Omega(n-1-\theta)/(n-1)$; if the promised share was to be divided among all $n$, the gain is $\beta\Omega(n-1-\theta)/n$ [num: SM(1)]. (5): $\Delta\pi_i=g-H/n$ and the others' $-H/n$, so $\Delta J_i=g-(1+\theta)H/n$. An act of spite that costs its taker $c_y$ and the others $\Delta_y$ in total pays iff $\theta<-(n-1)c_y/\Delta_y$. $\square$ (TC: 4,000 random games, an identity check.)

**Measuring the weight.** In the contest of Section 3.4 the symmetric intensity depends on the private cost $c$ and the shared loss $\ell$ only through $c+(1+\theta)\ell$, so the ratio of its response to the shared loss to its response to private cost is exactly $1+\theta$, the weight of the common channel; read from numerical equilibria it is 0.50004, 0.99996, 0.56997 and 1.99960 against $1+\theta=0.5$, 1, 0.57 and 2 [num: SP]. This is the observable Test Γ needs (Section 7.4). Because the working $\theta$ of Proposition {{#standprice}} rises when provision lowers the won share of capacity, the observable rises with provision: in the checked case, from 0.58 with the whole of capacity won to 0.81 with half of it provided [num: SP]. That is the sign Test Γ must use. A second identification sets a change in the shared loss, which enters through the common channel, against a rebated levy, which enters through the differential one: the ratio of the two responses is $n(1+\theta)/(n-1-\theta)$ [num: TH]. If actors perceive only a share $\psi$ of the shared loss, both ratios are scaled by $\psi$, so $\theta$ and $\psi$ are confounded: with half perceived, a true $\theta=0$ reads as $-0.5$ by the first ratio and $-0.43$ by the second [num: SM(3)]. Separating $\theta$ from $\psi$ needs a third perturbation that changes the visibility of shared harm without changing its size, or an independent measure of perceived harm. The identification fails exactly where the theory says shared harm goes unseen.

### Full statement and proof of Proposition {{#wow}}

*Let $n$ symmetric developers with $\tau_{\rm eff}=0$ and the host at steady state maximize $u_k=\pi_k+\lambda(\pi_k-\bar\pi)$, with $G(V)=g_VV$, $h_V\bar e<\rho$ and $\Upsilon=g_Vh_V/(\rho\nu)$; write $w_\lambda=1+\lambda(n-1)/n$. (1) Restraint is dominant if $w_\lambda<\Upsilon/n$, depletion if reversed. (2) If $\Upsilon>1$, joint payoff is maximized by full restraint while the equilibrium is full depletion whenever $\lambda>(\Upsilon-n)/(n-1)$, at every $\lambda$ when $n>\Upsilon$. (3) Every developer then loses $\nu\bar e(\Upsilon-1)$. (4) Under (reach) the equilibrium host is below the floor.*

*Proof.* $\pi_k-\bar\pi$ gives $G$ weight zero, so $\partial u_k/\partial e_k=w_\lambda\nu-g_Vh_V/(n\rho)$, independent of others' choices. Joint payoff at a common $e$ has slope $n\nu(1-\Upsilon)$. Payoffs are $\nu\bar e+g_V(1-h_V\bar e/\rho)$ at full depletion and $g_V$ under restraint. $\square$

A classical commons needs each actor's share of the loss to be small; by (2), selection on relative standing depletes this one even among a privileged few, since full depletion is the equilibrium at every $\lambda$ when $n>\Upsilon$ and once $\lambda>(\Upsilon-n)/(n-1)$ otherwise.

**{{!Remark:thrrep}} (Thresholds and repetition need observation).** If crossing the floor costs each a common loss $L$, a developer pivotal for crossing restrains when $L>(w_\lambda-\Upsilon/n)\nu\bar e$, and the game becomes coordination at the threshold (Barrett 2013). Joint restraint is sustained by reversion whenever $\delta\ge(w_\lambda-\Upsilon/n)/(w_\lambda-\Upsilon/n+\Upsilon-1)$: for $\Upsilon=3$, $n=4$ the cutoff is 0.11 at $\lambda=0$, 0.47 at $\lambda=2$ and 0.95 at $\lambda=50$. Uncertainty about the threshold turns coordination back into a dilemma (Barrett and Dannenberg 2012), and noisy monitoring makes reversion costly (Green and Porter 1984).

### Proof of Proposition {{#boundary}}

Write $\pi_i=w_i\Omega_e-ce_i-\ell\mathcal E+w_i\Omega_s$: the first three terms are the divisible part, the last the positional part. (1) An objective with one weight $\theta$ on all payoffs is $J_i=\Omega(1-\frac{\theta}{n-1})w_i-(c+(1+\theta)\ell)e_i+$ terms independent of $e_i$: a contest with prize $\Omega(n-1-\theta)/(n-1)$ and unit cost $c+(1+\theta)\ell$, whose symmetric pure equilibrium for $r\le n/(n-1)$ gives $e(\theta)$ (Pérez-Castrillo and Verdier 1992). At a symmetric profile $\pi^{\rm e}=\Omega_e/n-e(c+n\ell)$ and $\pi^{\rm s}=\Omega_s/n$, so $W=(1+\kappa)\pi^{\rm e}+(1+\kappa_s)\pi^{\rm s}$, which falls with $e$ and therefore rises with $\theta$. (2) As a function of $e_i$ with the others fixed, $W_i$ is a contest objective with prize $\big[\Omega_e(n-1-\kappa)+\Omega_s(n-1-\kappa_s)\big]/(n-1)$ and unit cost $c+(1+\kappa)\ell$, since the others' divisible winnings fall by what $i$ wins and their positional winnings count with weight $\kappa_s$: it is the objective that weighs the others' divisible payoffs by $\kappa$ and their positional payoffs by $\kappa_s$, and its best response maximizes $W_i$. Lowering the divisible weight below $\kappa$ raises the marginal value of intensity by $\Omega_e\,(\partial w_i/\partial e_i)/(n-1)+\ell>0$ per unit of weight, so the chosen intensity moves away from the welfare maximizer whenever it moves. On the positional part, $\kappa_s\le0$ makes the positional prize count at least fully: a rival's positional gain is the holder's loss, and relative concern there is accurate. (3) A finite-population evolutionarily stable intensity maximizes $\pi_i-\bar\pi_{-i}$ (Schaffer 1988), the case $\theta=-1$, in which the shared loss carries weight $1+\theta=0$; at $r=1$ it dissipates the whole prize (Hehenkamp, Leininger and Possajennikov 2004), so with $\ell>0$ every actor's payoff is negative. For $r>1$ asymmetric equilibria with lower total intensity also exist in a band below $n/(n-1)$; the symmetric one is used throughout. $\square$ (RH: the closed forms, and a grid best response.)

**Which preferences evolve.** It depends on the population: with unobserved preferences in large random populations, material self-interest (Ok and Vega-Redondo 2001; Dekel, Ely and Yilankaya 2007); with observed preferences and at least three actors in this contest, a commitment to a narrower objective (Güth and Yaari 1992; Heifetz, Shannon and Spiegel 2007); with high observability and assortative matching, cooperative preferences (Alger and Weibull 2013). Local competition favours spite (Alger, Weibull and Lehmann 2020), and an observable commitment to aggression is itself a race (Dixit 1987; Fershtman and Judd 1987). That competition selects organizations that behave *as if* they maximize (Alchian 1950) is part of this theory, not an objection to it: Proposition {{#boundary}}(3) is the selection that produces the conduct of Ψ whether or not anyone holds the doctrine (Section 3.8).

### Full form and proof of Proposition {{#standprice}}

*Full form.* In the model of Proposition {{#loopgain}} let the prize depend on the total stocks $S^j=\sum_iX^j_i$, with $g_j=\partial\Omega/\partial S^j\ge0$, and take as reference a symmetric steady state or, where the prize is proportional to the stocks, the balanced path. The relative and the common loop gain, evaluated at the reference state, are
$$\Gamma_\delta=(1-\beta)\sum_j\frac{\delta\kappa_jr_j\,\Omega/S^j}{1-\delta(1-d_j-\delta_{f,j})},\qquad \Gamma^{\rm c}_\delta=\sum_j\frac{\delta\kappa_jg_j}{1-\delta(1-d_j)}.$$
At a steady state $d_jS^j=\kappa_j\Omega+nk_{0j}$, so $\kappa_j\Omega/S^j=\omega_jd_j$ and $\Gamma_\delta$ is the discounted loop gain of Proposition {{#loopgain}}(3); with a fixed prize every $g_j=0$ and $\Gamma^{\rm c}_\delta=0$. Diffusion enters the relative gain and not the common one, because it acts only on differences between actors.

*Decomposition.* Split a change in today's net payoffs into its common part $\bar\Delta$ and the relative part $y=\Delta\pi_i-\bar\Delta$, and let every actor convert net payoff into stock $j$ at the rate $\kappa_j$. To first order the two parts evolve separately: the prize depends on the totals, and a relative shift leaves the totals unchanged.

*Relative part.* The actor's stocks rise by $\kappa_jy$ and the others' fall by as much in total. Relative deviations follow the Jacobian $\mathcal J=\mathcal D+\frac1n\mathbf u\mathbf v^\top$ of S2, with $\mathcal D=\operatorname{diag}(1-d_j-\delta_{f,j})$ and $\frac1nu_jv_j=(1-\beta)\kappa_jr_j\Omega/S^j$ at the reference state. By the Sherman–Morrison argument of S2, applied to $\delta\mathcal J$, the actor's discounted future winnings rise by $\lambda_{\rm rel}y$ with $\lambda_{\rm rel}=\Gamma_\delta/(1-\Gamma_\delta)$, finite iff $\Gamma_\delta<1$. Since the totals are unchanged, the others' winnings fall by $\lambda_{\rm rel}y$ in total.

*Common part.* Every actor's stock $j$ rises by $\kappa_j\bar\Delta$, so the total $S^j$ rises by $n\kappa_j\bar\Delta$ and the prize by $n\sum_kg_k\kappa_k\bar\Delta$, of which each actor receives $1/n$ at equal standing. The common deviations $c^j$ per actor therefore follow $c^{j\prime}=(1-d_j)c^j+\kappa_j\sum_kg_kc^k$, with the rank-one Jacobian $\operatorname{diag}(1-d_j)+\boldsymbol\kappa\mathbf g^\top$, on which diffusion does not act, and each actor's winnings rise by $\sum_kg_kc^k_t$ in period $t$. By Sherman–Morrison the discounted sum is $\lambda_{\rm com}\bar\Delta$ with $\lambda_{\rm com}=\Gamma^{\rm c}_\delta/(1-\Gamma^{\rm c}_\delta)$, finite iff $\Gamma^{\rm c}_\delta<1$.

*Matching.* The others' average future winnings change by $\lambda_{\rm com}\bar\Delta-\lambda_{\rm rel}y/(n-1)$, so with today's change from Proposition {{#twochan}} the total change in the objective with weight $\theta_0$ is
$$\Delta J_i=(1+\theta_0)(1+\lambda_{\rm com})\,\bar\Delta+\frac{n-1-\theta_0}{n-1}(1+\lambda_{\rm rel})\,y .$$
Matching it to $(1+\theta)\bar\Delta+\frac{n-1-\theta}{n-1}y$ up to a positive factor gives {{eq:thetagamma}}, and $1+\lambda=1/(1-\Gamma)$ for each gain gives its second form. Explicitly, with $\varrho=\frac{(1+\theta_0)(1-\Gamma_\delta)}{(n-1-\theta_0)(1-\Gamma^{\rm c}_\delta)}$, $\theta=\big(\varrho(n-1)-1\big)/(1+\varrho)$. The map $\theta\mapsto(1+\theta)/(n-1-\theta)$ is increasing on $[-1,n-1)$, so $\theta<\theta_0$ iff $\lambda_{\rm rel}>\lambda_{\rm com}$, and $\theta$ rises with $\theta_0$: no held self-model short of $\theta_0=n-1$ escapes the narrowing.

(1) With a fixed prize, $\lambda_{\rm com}=0$ and $\varrho=(1+\theta_0)(1-\Gamma_\delta)/(n-1-\theta_0)$, so $1+\theta_\Gamma=(1+\theta_0)\,n(1-\Gamma_\delta)/\big(n-(1+\theta_0)\Gamma_\delta\big)$, equivalent to the stated formula; as $\Gamma_\delta\to1^-$, $\varrho\to0$ and $\theta\to-1$. In Proposition {{#twochan}} the host's visibility $(1+\theta)/n$ and the point of enough $(1+\theta)H/n$ fall with $\theta$, while a rebated levy $\tau(n-1-\theta)/n$ and the gain from breaking a promise $\beta\Omega(n-1-\theta)/(n-1)$ rise. (2) With one stock, $\Omega=gS$ and $k_0=\delta_f=0$, $\kappa_c\Omega/S=\kappa_cg$, so $\Gamma_\delta=\delta(1-\beta)r\kappa_cg/\big(1-\delta(1-d_K)\big)$ and $\Gamma^{\rm c}_\delta=\delta\kappa_cg/\big(1-\delta(1-d_K)\big)$, whose ratio is $(1-\beta)r$ at every $\delta$; Proposition {{#growprize}} gives the comparison over every horizon, the instability condition, and the effect of diffusion. $\square$

**{{!Proposition:growprize}} (A prize that grows with capacity) [thm].** Where the prize is proportional to total capacity, looking ahead narrows the working self-model exactly where leads compound, until diffusion opens a band between the two; a part of the prize that does not grow with capacity adds to the relative return only. *In the one-channel model of Proposition {{#compound}} without provision, let a share $\beta$ of the prize be divided equally, and let actors convert net payoff into capacity at the rate $\kappa_c$ and discount at $\delta$.*
1. *Two periods. If first-period net payoffs become second-period capacity and the second-period prize is $\Omega_2=\Omega_{20}+gS$, with $S=\sum_iK_i$, then at equal standing $\lambda_{\rm rel}=\delta\kappa_c(1-\beta)r\,\Omega_2/S$ and $\lambda_{\rm com}=\delta\kappa_cg$, so $\lambda_{\rm rel}/\lambda_{\rm com}=(1-\beta)r\big(1+\Omega_{20}/(gS)\big)$, which is $(1-\beta)r$ when $\Omega_{20}=0$.*
2. *Infinite horizon. If $\Omega=gS$ and capacity depreciates at $d_K$ and diffuses at $\delta_f$, then along the balanced path, on which $S$ grows by the factor $\Lambda_{\rm com}=1-d_K+\kappa_cg$ a period while a relative deviation grows by $\Lambda_{\rm rel}=1-d_K-\delta_f+(1-\beta)r\kappa_cg$, and where $\delta\Lambda_{\rm com}<1$ and $\delta\Lambda_{\rm rel}<1$,*
$$\lambda_{\rm rel}=\frac{\delta(1-\beta)r\kappa_cg}{1-\delta\Lambda_{\rm rel}},\qquad \lambda_{\rm com}=\frac{\delta\kappa_cg}{1-\delta\Lambda_{\rm com}},$$
*that is, $\lambda=\Gamma/(1-\Gamma)$ for $\Gamma_\delta=\delta(1-\beta)r\kappa_cg/\big(1-\delta(1-d_K-\delta_f)\big)$ and $\Gamma^{\rm c}_\delta=\delta\kappa_cg/\big(1-\delta(1-d_K)\big)$. The working self-model narrows iff $(1-\beta)r>1+\delta\delta_f/\big(1-\delta(1-d_K)\big)$, and equal shares are unstable iff $(1-\beta)r>1+\delta_f/(\kappa_cg)$.*
3. *Without diffusion both conditions are $(1-\beta)r>1$, and over every horizon a relative unit is worth more than a common one exactly when $(1-\beta)r>1$. With diffusion the narrowing threshold lies below the instability threshold by $\delta_f(1-\Gamma^{\rm c}_\delta)/(\kappa_cg)>0$: between them looking ahead narrows the self while equal shares are stable.*

*Proof.* (1) Future winnings are $w_i=\Omega_2\big[(1-\beta)p_i+\beta/n\big]$ with $p_i=K_i^r/\sum_jK_j^r$, and each actor reinvests $x_j=\kappa_c\Delta\pi_j$. At equal standing $\partial p_i/\partial K_i=r(n-1)/(nS)$, $\partial p_i/\partial K_j=-r/(nS)$ and $\partial\Omega_2/\partial K_j=g$, so $\Delta w_i=(1-\beta)r\,\Omega_2\,(x_i-\bar x)/S+g\bar x$; discounting gives the two returns, and the matching of the main proof gives {{eq:thetagamma}}. (2) A relative unit raises the actor's capacity by $\kappa_c$ and lowers the others' by as much in total; since $\Delta p_i=ru_i/S$ for a deviation $u$ summing to zero, its own winnings rise by $(1-\beta)rg$ per unit of capacity deviation, and the deviation is multiplied each period by $\Lambda_{\rm rel}$. A common unit raises every capacity by $\kappa_c$; each actor's winnings rise by $g$ per unit of common capacity, and that deviation is multiplied by $\Lambda_{\rm com}$. Summing the discounted geometric series gives the closed forms. Narrowing is $\lambda_{\rm rel}>\lambda_{\rm com}$, that is $(1-\beta)r(1-\delta\Lambda_{\rm com})>1-\delta\Lambda_{\rm rel}$, which rearranges to $(1-\beta)r\big(1-\delta(1-d_K)\big)>1-\delta(1-d_K)+\delta\delta_f$; shares are unstable iff $\Lambda_{\rm rel}>\Lambda_{\rm com}$, that is $\kappa_cg\big((1-\beta)r-1\big)>\delta_f$. (3) Without diffusion the ratio of the relative to the common increment in period $t$ is $(1-\beta)r\,(\Lambda_{\rm rel}/\Lambda_{\rm com})^{t-1}$, and both factors exceed one exactly when $(1-\beta)r>1$, so every partial sum compares the same way. The gap between the thresholds is $\delta_f/(\kappa_cg)-\delta\delta_f/\big(1-\delta(1-d_K)\big)=\delta_f(1-\Gamma^{\rm c}_\delta)/(\kappa_cg)$, positive inside the domain. $\square$

*Numerics for {{growprize}} (SP).* Infinite horizon without depreciation: the simulated ratio of the values of a relative and a common unit equals the closed form in six $(r,\beta)$ pairs (2.53846, 0.68750, 0.62264, 1.00000, 2.08108, 0.92958) and exceeds one exactly when $(1-\beta)r>1$. Without diffusion, narrowing and the instability of equal shares coincide in 24 of 24 simulated cases; with $\delta_f=0.03$ the simulated thresholds are $(1-\beta)r=1.1421$ for narrowing and $1.2000$ for instability, as (2) predicts, so an objective narrows at $r=1.17$ while equal shares are stable.

*The two errors.* For Remark {{#twoerrors}}: $\theta_\Gamma(\kappa)=0$ iff $n\kappa=(n-1)(1+\kappa)\Gamma_\delta$, and $\theta_\Gamma$ falls with $\Gamma_\delta$, so an actor whose held weight is the coupling acts on a weight below the isolated self once $\Gamma_\delta>n\kappa/\big((n-1)(1+\kappa)\big)$, a threshold below one for every $\kappa<n-1$: 0.222, 0.381 and 0.667 for $\kappa=0.2$, 0.4 and 1 at $n=4$, and 0.333 and 0.571 for $\kappa=0.2$ and 0.4 at $n=2$. At $n=4$ and $\kappa=0.4$ the working weight is $+0.15$, $-0.15$, $-0.53$ and $-0.80$ at $\Gamma_\delta=0.25$, 0.5, 0.75 and 0.9 [num: SP].

*Balanced paths.* The closed form uses one Jacobian at the reference state, so it needs linearized coefficients that stay constant along the path: a steady state, or a balanced path along which strength depends on ratios of stocks and the prize is homogeneous of degree one, as in part (2). Where the elasticity changes along the path, as with strength linear in a growing stock, the derivative of future winnings is a sum over products of time-dependent Jacobians, $\sum_t\delta^t\,\mathbf w_t^\top\mathcal J_{t-1}\cdots\mathcal J_0$, which converges only under a condition on that product; a gain below one at the reference state does not certify it (S14) [num: CX].

*Numerics for {{standprice}} (SP, PD, NB, LG).* Algebraic checks, first order by construction at a symmetric point: in a two-period contest in which first-period net payoffs become second-period capacity, the working weight read off the directly computed equilibrium equals {{eq:thetagamma}} to $7.6\times10^{-8}$ in 14 cases ($n\in\{2,4\}$; held weights from $-0.5$ to 1.9; fixed, mixed and proportional prizes; $\beta$ from 0 to 0.6), and the sign rule holds in all 14, with exact ties at $(1-\beta)r=1$; with a growing prize the fixed-prize formula misses the computed intensity by up to 15% while the general form matches it to $3\times10^{-8}$, and with a proportional prize and $\theta_0=0.5$ the working weight is 0.5 at $r=1$, 0.549 at $r=0.7$ and 0.439 at $r=1.4$; with a fixed prize the same identity holds for held weights $-0.5$, 0, 0.5, 1 and 1.9 (LG). Independent checks: in the nonlinear stock model, with sharing, provision and diffusion, both closed-loop prices match simulation to a relative error of $2.5\times10^{-5}$ in five steady states and two balanced paths (2.213115 against 2.213115 with a fixed prize; 4.764587 against 4.764706, and 0.473684 against 0.473684, with a growing one); outside the domain ($\Gamma_\delta=1.066$) the simulated price is 226 against a formal value of $-16.2$, the cusp of Proposition {{#loopgain}}(3) (S2; PD). With a fixed prize and a stationary total ($n=3$, $r=1.5$, $k_0=0.1$), $\Gamma=0.938$: equal standing is stable and the objective narrows. Where only gross winnings convert, the relative part of private cost is not amplified, and the working weight is narrower still: $-0.3125$ against $-0.1649$ with net payoff reinvested ($r=1$, $\theta_0=0$). Provision: with a fixed prize ($n=4$, $\theta_0=0$, $r=1$, $d=0.2$, $\delta_f=0.1$, $\delta=0.9$), lowering the won share from 1 to 0.5 raises $1+\theta_\Gamma$ from 0.5846 to 0.8058. With $n=4$ and a fixed prize, $\theta_\Gamma$ at $\theta_0=0$ is $-0.20$, $-0.43$, $-0.69$, $-0.87$ and $-0.99$ at $\Gamma_\delta=0.25$, 0.5, 0.75, 0.9 and 0.99, and at $\theta_0=2$ it is still $-0.08$ at $\Gamma_\delta=0.9$ (NB).

*Rivals who respond.* With the others' conduct fixed, the exact finite-jump price equals $\Gamma_\delta/(1-\Gamma_\delta)$ even at the paper's jump sizes, so what the strategic case changes is the premise, not the linearization. Where rivals respond, the relative part of the decomposition above takes the price of Proposition {{#loopgain}}(4), read from the elasticity with the actor's own intensity held and its rivals at their equilibrium policies; with a fixed prize the common part is zero and {{eq:thetagamma}} keeps its form. How the common return changes when rivals respond to a growing prize is not computed here. In contests in which a lead discourages the follower, the discouragement effect of dynamic contests (Harris and Vickers 1987; Konrad and Kovenock 2009), the price at parity is amplified: by exactly $2(n-1)/n$, the static factor of {{eqgain}}(2) [thm], which a two-period contest with effort reproduces [num: MP]; and by about 1.05 to 7.9 times in the two-actor Markov-perfect contests computed in {{eqgain}}(3): 1.05 to 3.15 times at $d=0.1$, 1.12 to 4.75 times at the paper's depreciation $d=0.2$, and 7.87 times under high patience ($\delta=0.97$). A laggard's price falls, at a quarter share to 57% and 25% of the structural value at $\Gamma=0.3$ and 0.6, and below zero with a shared loss, and it stops contesting standing ({{eqgain}}(6)); a strong motive to catch up turns the correction the other way ({{eqgain}}(5)). Where rounds are small, a winner-take-all equilibrium coexists with the smooth one, at about 12.5 and 8.8 times the structural price for $\Gamma=0.2$ and 0.3 ({{eqgain}}(4)). All are held for outside review [num: MP].

*Bounds.* First order. At $\Gamma_\delta\ge1$ no finite weight represents the actor (Proposition {{#loopgain}}(3)), so "where the loop gain is high" reads as the limit $\Gamma_\delta\to1^-$. Away from a steady state or a balanced path, as with a prize that has both a fixed and a growing part while capacity grows, the two returns vary over time and are not closed here; the two-period form covers that case. A myopic actor has $\lambda_{\rm rel}=\lambda_{\rm com}=0$ and acts on its held model, but selection still favours $\theta=-1$ (Proposition {{#boundary}}(3)).

### Proof of Lemma {{#onegroup}}

Under any partition into groups $g$, the Price equation gives $\bar w\Delta\bar z=\operatorname{Cov}_g(\bar w_g,\bar z_g)+\operatorname E_g\operatorname{Cov}_{j\in g}(w_j,z_j)+\operatorname E[w\Delta z]$. Let the trait cost its bearer $c>0$ and give every member a benefit $b\bar z$ equal across members, so that $\pi_j=\pi_0+b\bar z-cz_j$ is the same decreasing function of $z_j$ for everyone. A payoff-monotone rule orders growth as payoff, so fitness is $w_j=\varphi(\pi_j)$ with $\varphi$ increasing on the payoffs present, and $w_j$ is a decreasing function of $z_j$ alone. By Chebyshev's sum inequality, $\operatorname{Cov}(w,z)\le0$ in the population and within every group. Where $\varphi$ is affine, $\bar w_g=\varphi(\pi_0+b\bar z-c\bar z_g)$ is decreasing in $\bar z_g$ and the between-group term is $-c\varphi'\operatorname{Var}_g(\bar z_g)\le0$. Where $\varphi$ is not affine, $\bar w_g$ depends on the spread of $z$ within the group as well as on its mean (Jensen), and the between-group term can be positive; the total is unchanged, since the decomposition is an identity, and the within-group terms absorb the difference. The trait can rise only if the transmission term is positive, by biased transmission, or if a rule changes the bearer's relative payoff. If the benefit goes only to the bearer's group, $\bar w_g$ contains $b\bar z_g$, and under the partition that matches its reach the between-group term can be positive. $\square$

*Numerics (PR).* With 600 individuals in 30 units and five partitions (one group, units, five blocs, fifty random groups, individuals), the between-group term under a global benefit is $0$, $-0.0019$, $-0.00002$, $-0.0011$ and $-0.0123$; under a benefit local to units it is $+0.0097$ at the unit partition. With nonlinear increasing fitness, over 3,000 random partitioned populations for each of six maps (affine, $e^{5\pi}$, $\log$, $\pi^3$, logistic, near-step), the total and every within-group term are positive in none; the between-group term is positive in none under affine fitness and in 3 to 346 of 3,000 under the nonlinear maps; in two reviewers' instances it is $+0.017$ with total $-0.364$, and $+0.0092$ with total $-0.115$. Biased transmission $\dot z=-cz(1-z)+\mu_{\rm tr}(1-z)$ settles at $\mu_{\rm tr}/c$. In an illustration with a base hazard of 0.03 a period, learning only from failures suffers an irreversible loss within 400 periods with probability 0, 0.14, 0.46 and 1.0 as the irreversible share of failures is 0, 0.05, 0.2 and 1, against 0, 0.06, 0.22 and 0.70 under adoption in advance: an illustration, not an estimate.

### The five endgames

The corpus names five endgames of the dynamic [corpus: selfishness-spiral]: a freeze of mutual destruction; deadlock, a structure frozen by the consensus of the remaining players; a change of narrative by which the players come to see themselves as one whole and so gain the ability to cooperate; collapse; and winner takes all. Four are results of this paper: deterrence at saturation (Section 7.2), a captured configuration that cannot state what would end it (Section 4.5), the crossing results (Sections 3.2, 4.6 and 5.3) and the decisive winner (Section 5.2). The third is the transmission term of Lemma {{#onegroup}}, and the purpose of this paper (Section 3.7).

### Proof of Proposition {{#club}}

(1) Contestants who weigh one another fully maximize $\Omega(\mathcal E)-c\mathcal E-n\ell\mathcal E$; the system subtracts $m_o\ell_o\mathcal E$ as well. At a positive club optimum $\Omega'(\mathcal E_c)=c+n\ell$; any $\mathcal E\ge\mathcal E_c$ has $\Omega'(\mathcal E)\le c+n\ell<c+n\ell+m_o\ell_o$, so strict concavity puts the system optimum strictly below $\mathcal E_c$. With $\Omega=10\sqrt{\mathcal E}$, $c=1$, $n=4$, $\ell=0.3$, $m_o=20$, $\ell_o=0.3$, the club's intensity is 5.17 against 0.37, and the harm to non-contestants 31.0 against 2.2 [num: CB]. (2) A bloc of $n_b$ equal members acting as one maximizes its members' summed payoff: it receives their summed prize share and bears the shared loss $n_b\ell$ per unit of total intensity, and with equal blocs the factor by which splitting effort among members changes a bloc's strength is common to all blocs and cancels. The game among the $n/n_b$ blocs is therefore the original game with $n/n_b$ players and loss $n_b\ell$. At $r=1$ the bloc intensities are 0.1563, 0.1389 and 0.1316 for two blocs of two, three blocs of two and two blocs of three, as that game predicts and the game with loss $\ell$ alone does not [num: SM(2)]. With unequal sizes and $r\ne1$, equal total effort gives a bloc of three $3^{1-r}$ times a single contestant's strength (1.732 at $r=0.5$, 0.577 at $r=1.5$). $\square$
