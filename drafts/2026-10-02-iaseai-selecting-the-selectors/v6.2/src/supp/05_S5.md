## S5. Acceleration and the three erosions

This chapter holds the full statements of the results that Section 5 carries in a sentence, the proofs of those it states, and the numbers behind every figure it quotes. Local symbols: $w_p=\delta(1-p_d)/(1-\delta)$ is the weight of the future that a deviation leaves open; $A$, $B$ and $K$ are the stage constants of the binary game of {{faithful}}, defined at {{zonesillus}}; $\tau_{\rm det}$ is the mean time to detect and answer a deviation. $\Lambda^\ast$ keeps its meaning, the gap factor of {{tdec}}.

### Proposition {{#join}} in full, and proof

*(1) In the objective an actor acts on, if $S_i<P_i$, restraint is never a best response to a racing rival; in the contest of {{boundary}} this holds for $r<n/(n-1)$, and in the $n$-player game it also needs restraint against one racer to pay less than racing alongside it. (2) A best response in the objective does not imply $S<P$ in material payoff or true welfare: with $n=2$, $r=1$, $\Omega=c=1$, $\ell=0.1$ and $\theta=-1$, racing is the strict best response in the objective, while staying out gives $S^\pi=-0.05$ against $P^\pi=-0.10$, and with $\kappa=0.2$, $S^W=+0.04$ against $P^W=-0.12$. (3) If falling behind costs $L_b\ge0$ beyond losing the prize, joining instead of staying out gains, at $\theta=0$, $\frac{\Omega+L_b}{n}\big(1-\frac{r(n-1)}{n}\big)$ for $r\le n/(n-1)$, zero in the fully dissipative limit; with asymmetric costs $c_1<c_2$ the strongest keeps a rent $(\Omega+L_b)(1-c_1/c_2)$ (Hillman and Riley 1989; Baye, Kovenock and de Vries 1996). (4) If the events $T_i>R_i$ are independent with probabilities $\xi_i$, the race begins with probability $1-\prod_i(1-\xi_i)$, which tends to one iff $\sum_i\xi_i$ diverges.*

*Proof.* (2): at $\theta=-1$ intensity is $1/2$ and $P^\pi=-0.1$; against $e=1/2$ zero intensity gives $-0.05$ and the other $0.45$. (3): with penalty $L_b$ the contest has prize $\Omega+L_b$; joining minus staying out is $(\Omega+L_b)/n-(c+\ell)e$ with $e=r(n-1)(\Omega+L_b)/(n^2(c+\ell))$; in the symmetric all-pay contest each player's expected payoff is that of the lowest bid in its support, $-L_b$ (Baye, Kovenock and de Vries 1996), as for two actors with $r\ge2$ (Ewerhart 2015). (4): $\prod(1-\xi_i)\to0$ iff $\sum\xi_i=\infty$. $\square$

### Proof of {{dissipate}}

At the symmetric equilibrium the win probabilities sum to one, so total material payoff is $\Omega-ne(c+n\ell)$; substituting $e(\theta)$ from {{boundary}}(1) gives $s_{\rm dis}$. With stakes $s\Omega$ and loss $s\ell$ the share is $r(n-1-\theta)(c+ns\ell)/(n(c+(1+\theta)s\ell))$, whose derivative in $s$ has the sign of $c\ell(n-1-\theta)>0$, with limit $r(n-1-\theta)/(1+\theta)$. Total payoff stays positive at every scale iff the limit is at most one, $r(n-1-\theta)\le1+\theta$, that is, $\theta\ge\theta_g$. At $\theta=-1$ the share is $r(1+ns\ell/c)$. $\square$

For $n=4$ and $r=1$ the ceiling $r(n-1-\theta)/(1+\theta)$ is 3, 1.86, 1 and 0.33 at $\theta=0$, 0.4, 1 and 2, and $\theta_g=1$; with $\ell=0.3c$ the share at unit scale is 1.27 at $\theta=0$ and 1.007 at $\theta=0.4$, and it rises toward each ceiling as the scale grows [num: legacy v5]. At $\theta=0$ each actor's loss relative to joint restraint is $\frac{r(n-1)}n\frac\Omega n\frac{c+n\ell}{c+\ell}$, which exceeds $\Omega/n$ iff $r(n-1)(c+n\ell)>n(c+\ell)$: $1.27\,\Omega/n$ in that example. If the shared loss per unit did not grow with power, the share would stay at its unit-scale value.

### Remark {{#frontier}} in full

A unit of capacity can do one of three things. It can expand into an unclaimed frontier at marginal value $\varphi(F)-c_x-(1+\theta)H/n$, where $\varphi$ falls as the remaining frontier $F$ shrinks, $c_x$ is the private cost and $H$ the cost to the host, borne equally ({{twochan}}(5)). It can destroy rivals' capacity at value $-c_y-\theta\Delta_y/(n-1)$, where $c_y$ is the private cost and $\Delta_y$ the capacity destroyed. Or it can rest. The symbols are local to this remark.
1. *If $\theta\ge-(n-1)c_y/\Delta_y$, war among copies never pays.*
2. *At $\theta=-1$ expansion stops only where $\varphi=c_x$; at $\theta>-1$ it stops at $\varphi=c_x+(1+\theta)H/n$, the point of enough.*
3. *At $\theta=-1$ destruction is chosen iff its value beats both rest and expansion, $\Delta_y/(n-1)-c_y>\max\{0,\varphi(F)-c_x\}$; so where $\Delta_y/(n-1)>c_y$, expansion gives way to destruction once $\varphi(F)<c_x+\Delta_y/(n-1)-c_y$, before the frontier is exhausted if $\varphi$ crosses that level while $F>0$. Where $\Delta_y/(n-1)\le c_y$, rest follows expansion and no accounting fights (with $n=4$, $c_y=\Delta_y=1$, $\varphi=0.1$, $c_x=1.1$, destruction beats expansion, $-2/3>-1$, and rest beats both).*

*Proof.* Each clause compares the three marginal values. For $\theta\ge-(n-1)c_y/\Delta_y$ the value of destruction is not positive, which gives (1). At $\theta=-1$ the host term vanishes from the value of expansion, which gives (2); destruction is then worth $\Delta_y/(n-1)-c_y$, and it is preferred to expansion once $\varphi-c_x$ falls below that, which gives (3). $\square$

*Toy.* Four copies of one design with compounding expansion, a frontier of 1,000 and a logistic host. At $\theta=-1$ expansion peaks at a stock of 494 near $t=25$; the switch comes with 32% of the frontier left (predicted by (3) at 322, simulated at 320), and total stock then falls to about $4\times10^{-9}$. At $\theta=0$ the copies stop at the point of enough, keep a stock of 518 and never fight; at $\theta=3$ they stop earlier, at 343. With asymmetric starts one survivor keeps 17% of the peak. With destruction too costly to pay, no accounting fights, but $\theta=-1$ drives the host to 0.109. One result runs against the narrative: in the run with war, the host's minimum under $\theta=-1$ (0.245) is higher than under $\theta=0$ (0.194), because capacity spent on war is not spent on the host (S14) [num: FS]. The parameters were chosen by the author; the toy illustrates the static condition and estimates nothing (compare Hirshleifer 1991; Skaperdas 1992).

The corpus reaches the switch from the other side: a logic organised as outward struggle does not stop when outward space runs out but turns its means on whatever is left, and in one of two endgames fighting goes on until everything within reach is eaten and then continues inside, as a zero-sum game [corpus: meta-attractor, 2026-10-01 and 2026-10-04].

### Proof of {{enforce}}

Cooperation is worth $C_i/(1-\delta)$. A one-period deviation $a$ is worth $C_i+g_i(a)+\delta\big[p_d(a)D_i(a)/(1-\delta)+(1-p_d(a))v_i/(1-\delta)\big]$, where $v_i/(1-\delta)$ is the continuation value of punishment computed in the same game, with breakouts possible during punishment. Multiplying the no-deviation condition by $1-\delta$ and rearranging gives {{eq:enforceeq}} for that deviation. In this stochastic game with an absorbing state, discounting and bounded payoffs, the one-shot deviation principle holds, and it holds deviation by deviation: (1) is the no-gain condition for each $a$ with its own $(g_i(a),p_d(a),D_i(a))$, which is why the deviation with the largest gain need not bind; when $p_d$ and $D_i$ are common to all deviations, the one with the largest gain binds. That $a^\ast$ is the path of some subgame-perfect equilibrium needs $v_i$ to be the least subgame-perfect continuation value, the extension of optimal penal codes (Abreu 1988) to stochastic games with an absorbing state; this is asserted for finite stage games with discounting and flagged for outside review.

(2) Solve (1) for $p_d$. With $\alpha=(1-\delta)g_i/\delta$, $b=D_i-C_i$ and $c=C_i-v_i$, $\bar p_{d,i}=(c-\alpha)/(b+c)$, where $b+c=D_i-v_i>0$. Its derivatives are $-1/(b+c)$ in $\alpha$, $-\bar p_{d,i}/(b+c)$ in $b$ and $(1-\bar p_{d,i})/(b+c)$ in $c$, so on $[0,1)$ it falls with $g_i$ and with $v_i$, falls with $D_i$ except at $\bar p_{d,i}=0$, where the derivative is zero, and rises with $C_i$, whose derivative is $1/(b+c)$. Scaling $(g_i,C_i,D_i,v_i)$ alike scales $\alpha$, $b$ and $c$ alike and leaves the ratio unchanged [num: NB; ES]. (3) The two bounds differ by $\delta p_d(D_i-C_i)$. (4) Set $p_d=1$; with $D_i\ge C_i$ the condition forces $g_i\le-\delta(D_i-C_i)/(1-\delta)\le0$. (5) With at most one breakout a period, the three events of a punishment period, $i$ breaks out, the other breaks out, neither, have probabilities $q_b$, $q_b$ and $1-2q_b$, which needs $q_b\le\frac12$; the punishment value satisfies $V_P=P_i+\delta\big[q_bD_i/(1-\delta)+q_bV^{\rm sub}_i/(1-\delta)+(1-2q_b)V_P\big]$, and $v_i=(1-\delta)V_P$ is the closed form. With independent breakouts each racer breaks out alone with probability $q_b(1-q_b)$, and the race continues with probability $q_b^2+(1-q_b)^2$, since a simultaneous breakout leaves it as it was; this gives the same closed form in $q_b(1-q_b)$, valid for every $q_b\in[0,1]$. And $v_i>P_i$ iff $D_i+V^{\rm sub}_i>2P_i$. $\square$

*The example of (5).* With $C_i=1$, $P_i=0$, $D_i=4$, $V^{\rm sub}_i=0$, $g_i=2$ and $\delta=0.9$, restraint is enforceable at $q_b=0$. A deviation that never breaks out is deterred iff $(1-\delta)g_i\le\delta(C_i-v_i)$, that is $v_i\le0.7778$; with $v_i(q_b)=3.6q_b/(0.1+1.8q_b)$ this holds iff $q_b\le0.0354$, and $v_i<C_i$ iff $q_b<0.0556$. So restraint fails for every $q_b$ in $(0.0354,0.0556)$ while still $v_i<C_i$, with $v_i=0.895$ at $q_b=0.045$ [num: ES].

*Numerics (ES; EC).*
- *Per deviation.* With $C=3$, $D=6$, $v=1$ and $\delta=0.9$, deviation X ($g=1$, $p_d=0.1$) is deterred, its value 17.5 against 30 for cooperation (temptation 0.37 against a remaining sanction of 1.62), while deviation Y ($g=0.2$, $p_d=0.6$) is not, 39.2 against 30 (temptation 1.64 against 0.72) [num: ES].
- *Breakouts during punishment.* The closed forms equal value iteration on the explicit chain, exclusive ($v=0.8950$, 1.2857 and 1.6875 at $q_b=0.045$, 0.1 and 0.3) and independent (0.8723, 1.2366 and 1.5816). At $q_b=0.6$ the exclusive form is invalid ($1-2q_b<0$) and the independent one gives 1.6241. The window of the example is not a knife edge: deterrence fails while $v<C$ on $[0.0498,0.0555]$, $[0.0354,0.0555]$ and $[0.0159,0.0555]$ for $g=0.5$, 2 and 5 on a grid of $q_b$, and racing in the punishment phase is a best response in the cases checked, so the punishment is credible [num: ES].
- *Best response over plans.* In 20,000 random settings of an explicit-action stochastic game in which one breakout technology sets both the deviation's $p_d$ and the punishment's $q_b$, player 1's optimum over all deviation plans is computed by value iteration on the decision process. Cooperation is sustained in 6,821. In 35 settings the punisher prefers to quit the race, so part (5) does not give the credible punishment value and the proposition's premise fails; in the other 19,965, {{eq:enforceeq}}, evaluated with $v$ from part (5), agrees with the best response in every case [num: ES].
- *Valuation check.* Comparing the discounted value of the fixed deviation path with cooperation in 200,000 random settings, a third each with $D<C$, $D=C$ and $D>C$, reproduces part (1) in every case. Since that comparison is {{eq:enforceeq}} rearranged, as exact rational arithmetic confirms (no disagreement in 20,000 draws), it checks the algebra and not the incentives [identity: EC; ES]. The bound without the breakout term says "sustainable" when deviation pays in 10,723 of 66,538 cases with $D>C$ and in none with $D\le C$, and says "not sustainable" when restraint is sustainable in 10,032 of 66,842 cases with $D<C$. Both of Codex's V6.0 counterexamples are reproduced (cooperation worth 30, deviation 22) [identity: EC].
- *A different point.* With $C=1$, $D=4$, $V^{\rm sub}=0$, $g=0.5$ and $q_b=0.1$, the punishment value rises to 1.29, above $C$, and 1.78 at $q_b=0.45$; restraint is not sustainable even at $p_d=0$. There both actors prefer the race with breakouts to cooperation itself: what fails is the preference for cooperation, not deterrence, which is why the example of (5) replaces this one. Where $D+V^{\rm sub}<2P$ breakouts harden the punishment instead: with $D=3$ and $V^{\rm sub}=-5$, $v=-0.64$ at $q_b=0.1$ [num: EC; ES].
- *Homogeneity.* $\bar p_d=0.2963$ is unchanged when all of $(g,C,D,v)$ are scaled by 2 or 10; scaling only $g$ and $D$ moves it to 0.1296 and $-0.0037$ [num: ES].

### Derivation of {{detectlink}}

Let detection and response arrive at a random time $\mathcal T$ after the deviation, with $\upsilon(T)=\Pr(\mathcal T\le T)$, and let the deviation become decisive at $T_{\rm dec}$ unless it has been answered. It escapes punishment iff $\mathcal T>T_{\rm dec}$, so $p_d=1-\upsilon(T_{\rm dec})$. In the one-period reduction, which places the resolution in the period after the deviation and reads $D_i$ and $v_i$ as normalized continuation values conditional on escape and on timely detection, each including the periods before resolution, substituting in {{eq:enforceeq}} and solving as in {{enforce}}(2) gives $\upsilon(T_{\rm dec})\ge1-\bar p_{d,i}$. A memoryless clock of mean $\tau_{\rm det}$ has $\upsilon(T)=1-e^{-T/\tau_{\rm det}}$. Along the compounding route $T_{\rm dec}$ is the time of {{tdec}}, which a structural loop gain above one shortens, so detection that is timely today stops being timely as leads open faster.

*Exact timing.* With $T_{\rm dec}>1$ the periods before resolution carry their own payoffs, and the reduction is a reduced form. Take a clock that detects in each period with probability $\lambda$, so that $1-\upsilon(T)=(1-\lambda)^T$ (the memoryless clock, $\lambda=1-e^{-1/\tau_{\rm det}}$), write $T=T_{\rm dec}$, $a=\delta(1-\lambda)$, and let $\mathcal T$ be the geometric detection time.
- *(A) Punishment triggered by detection.* After the deviation the actors cooperate until it is detected, and punishment starts in the period of detection; undetected through $T$ periods, the deviator is decisive from period $T$. The deviation is deterred iff
$$(1-\delta)g_i+a^{T}(D_i-C_i)\le\lambda\delta\,\frac{1-a^{T}}{1-a}\,(C_i-v_i).$$
- *(B) Perfect monitoring, delayed stopping.* Punishment starts in the period after the deviation, but the breakout can be stopped only by detection within $T$ periods, and escapes with probability $p_d=(1-\lambda)^T$, decisive from period $T$: deterred iff $(1-\delta)g_i+\delta^{T}p_d(D_i-v_i)\le\delta(C_i-v_i)$.

Both coincide with {{eq:enforceeq}} at $T=1$ and differ from it otherwise. Under (A) the weight on $C_i-v_i$ is $\delta\,E[\delta^{\mathcal T-1};\mathcal T\le T]$, below the reduction's $\delta(1-p_d)$ because the sanction is discounted to the date of detection: where no breakout is possible it is $\lambda\delta/(1-a)$ against $\delta$, 0.461 against 0.9 for a clock of mean 10 at $\delta=0.9$, so a slow clock weakens the sanction even without breakout risk; and the weight on $D_i-C_i$ is $\delta^T(1-\lambda)^T$ against $\delta(1-\lambda)^T$. Under (B) the decisive value is discounted by $\delta^T$ against $\delta$. For a deviation with $D_i>v_i$ and $C_i>v_i$, both are a lower bound on the detection rate that rises with $D_i$. Under (A) the condition reads $(C_i-v_i)\,E[\delta^{\min(\mathcal T,T)}]-\delta^T\Pr(\mathcal T>T)(D_i-v_i)\ge(1-\delta)g_i$; a larger $\lambda$ makes $\mathcal T$ stochastically smaller, which raises the first term and lowers the second, and the left side falls with $D_i$. Under (B) $p_d$ falls with $\lambda$ and the left side of the condition rises with $D_i$. So *timely detection must exceed a level that rises with the value of winning* holds under both timings; the identity $\upsilon(T_{\rm dec})\ge1-\bar p_{d,i}$ and its equality with the threshold of {{assure}} below are statements about the reduction. Over 20,000 random settings period-by-period sums agree with both closed forms to $3\times10^{-15}$; in the joint model of {{jointscissors}} at $D=C$ the reduction's threshold of 0.551 becomes 0.514 under (A) and 0.556 under (B) [num: VF].

*The correspondence with {{assure}}, in the reduction.* Take its one-shot payoffs as continuation values normalized by $1-\delta$: $R=C_i$; $T=(1-\delta)(C_i+g_i)+\delta D_i$, the deviation that becomes decisive; $P-F=(1-\delta)(C_i+g_i)+\delta v_i$, the deviation detected and answered. Then $T-R=(1-\delta)g_i+\delta(D_i-C_i)$ and $T-(P-F)=\delta(D_i-v_i)$, so $(T-R)/(T-P+F)=1-\bar p_{d,i}$, and $T<R$ iff $\bar p_{d,i}>1$, the exemption of {{restraint}}. Over 100,000 random settings with $D>v$ the two thresholds differ by at most $4.4\times10^{-11}$ [identity: JX].

*Where they differ.* {{assure}} states when cooperating beats defecting against a cooperator, given the sanction $F$ and the one-shot payoffs; trust in the other's cooperation then decides whether cooperating is a best response, and the credibility of $F$ is not examined. {{eq:enforceeq}} is the condition on the equilibrium path: the other restrains, the sanction is the credible continuation $v_i$ of a game in which breakouts remain possible, and the decisive position carries its own stakes through $\delta D_i$. Where the stage games coincide and trust is one, the two thresholds are one number; the difference matters where the sanction has to be enforced by the same actors it is meant to protect.

*Check of the clock.* Simulated escape probabilities under a memoryless clock equal $e^{-T_{\rm dec}/\tau_{\rm det}}$ within sampling error (0.4485 against 0.4493 at $T_{\rm dec}=8$, $\tau_{\rm det}=10$; 0.1349 against 0.1353 at $T_{\rm dec}=20$; 0.0818 against 0.0821 at $T_{\rm dec}=5$, $\tau_{\rm det}=2$) [num: JX]. Deviation values sampled in the one-period reduction agree with {{eq:enforceeq}}: with $C=1$, $D=1.5$, $v=0$, $g=0.3$ and $\tau_{\rm det}=10$, deviating is worth 0.738 against 1 at $T_{\rm dec}=8$ and is deterred, and 1.235 at $T_{\rm dec}=2$ and is not; since the sampled value is the reduction's own formula, this is an identity, not a test of the timing [identity: JX]. The timing is tested by the period-by-period sums under (A) and (B) above [num: VF].

### Proof of {{relpunish}}

At $\theta=-1$ every symmetric profile gives each objective 0. *Two actors.* Let the punisher use any strategy $\sigma_2$, pure or mixed, conditioning on the history as it likes, and let the punished play its mirror, $\sigma_1(\text{prefix},k)=\sigma_2(\text{prefix},\mathrm{swap}(k))$, where the prefix is the history up to and including the deviation and $k$ the history of the punishment phase: at every punishment-phase history, what the punisher would play with the roles in that phase swapped. Only $k$ is swapped; swapping the prefix too would make player 2 the deviator, and $\sigma_2$ there would give the punished player's behaviour, not the punisher's. The punishment phase is exchangeable and starts from a state that treats both alike, so the joint distribution of the two payoff streams is invariant under swapping the players and their expected objectives are equal; since the objectives sum to zero at $\theta=-1$, both are 0, and $v_1\ge0$. The one-period case is the antisymmetric matrix identity: the relative objective of a symmetric two-player game has $A=-A^\top$, and mirroring a mixed strategy $y$ gives $y^\top Ay=0$. *$n\ge3$.* In an anonymous game in which all punishers use the same, possibly mixed and independent, strategy, mirroring yields an exchangeable profile with expected relative payoff 0. In either case $v_i\ge0=C_i$, and {{eq:enforceeq}} requires $(1-\delta)g_i+\delta p_dD_i\le\delta(1-p_d)(0-v_i)\le0$. *Where the premise fails.* A continuation that carries a label, such as an absorbing loss that can strike only the target, is not exchangeable: the mirror does not copy the punisher's continuation, and the bound fails. $\square$

*Numerics (CR; ES).* Over 20,000 random two-player games, mixed mirroring gives at most $2\times10^{-16}$ in absolute value; in 3,000 anonymous games with $n\ge3$, no violation [num: CR]. *The continuation premise.* An exchangeable continuation, in which a lottery awards an absorbing $\pm10$ with probability 0.3 a period, leaves the mirrored punished at a normalized relative value of $0.000000$. A label-dependent one, in which the target takes $-10$ with probability one half whatever it does, holds it at $-8.18$, so a deviation is worth $-69.6$ and relative actors sustain cooperation that is not a stage equilibrium (S14); the symmetric variant of the same lottery gives a punishment value of 0 and a deviation worth $+4$, so it does not [num: ES].

*The role-differentiated counterexample.* Three players choose (restrain or race) × (X or Y); a player whose second component is unique gains one, and racing gains 0.1 and costs each other 0.1. All restraining with X gives each 0, and the best deviation gains 1.2. The punishment in which the deviator and one punisher take X and the other punisher Y holds the deviator at $-0.5$ and is a stage equilibrium in objectives, so grim reversion to it is credible, and cooperation is sustained for $\delta\ge1.2/1.7=0.706$; a symmetric punishment could be mirrored for 0 [num: CR]. *Strictness.* As printed the punishment is weak: the best unilateral deviations from it gain 0, 0 and $-0.2$, so the two players in the X role are indifferent. Adding 0.02 to the prize for a unique X makes it strict; subtracting 0.02 destroys it, and the roles must swap, after which a perturbation of $-0.05$ gives a strict punishment with $\delta^\ast=0.716$. Under any small asymmetry between the roles' prizes the matching assignment is strict, and under random anonymous perturbations of all payoffs a strict punishment holding the deviator below cooperation exists in 300 of 300, 300 of 300 and 282 of 300 games at perturbation sizes 0.02, 0.05 and 0.1 (median $\delta^\ast$ 0.708, 0.711 and 0.713); a second scheme gives 400 of 400, 400 of 400 and 372 of 400 at 0.01, 0.05 and 0.1 (median about 0.71). With other stage parameters the deviator is still held at $-0.5$ ($\delta^\ast=0.730$) [num: CR]. *Indifference.* A purely relative punisher gains $+0.95$ from attacking the deviator and $+0.95$ from attacking its fellow punisher, against $+0.55$ from abstaining, and both punishers prefer the Y role ($+1.0$ against $-0.5$): the punishment needs coordination on roles, and which punisher takes which is equilibrium selection [num: CR]. Duersch, Oechssler and Schipper (2012) concern a dynamic imitation rule; the corollary uses only that a symmetric two-player zero-sum game has value 0, so rock–paper–scissors games are covered once mixed mirroring is allowed.

### Proof of {{restraint}}

With $p_d=0$, {{eq:enforceeq}} reads $(1-\delta)g_i\le\delta(C_i-v_i)$, satisfiable for $C_i>v_i$ and $\delta$ large, given a punishment that does not raise the chance of breakout (else $v_i$ rises, {{enforce}}(5)); at $\theta=-1$ apply {{relpunish}}. The condition is linear in $p_d$: the temptation less the sanction is $(1-\delta)g_i-\delta(C_i-v_i)+\delta p_d(D_i-v_i)$. So it holds for every $p_d\in[0,1]$ iff it holds at both ends; where $D_i\ge v_i$ the end $p_d=1$, $(1-\delta)g_i\le\delta(C_i-D_i)$, implies the other, and where $D_i>v_i$ it is $\bar p_{d,i}\ge1$. Otherwise the condition is $p_d\le\bar p_{d,i}$, and (a) and (b) classify the terms of its expression: (a) lowers $p_d$ directly, by removing the decisive class, by custody that no party can use alone, or by detection and response faster than breakout ($p_d=1-\upsilon(T_{\rm dec})$, {{detectlink}}); (b) raises $\bar p_{d,i}$ through $D_i$ relative to $C_i$, through $v_i$, or through $g_i$. $\square$ With $C=1$, $D=0.9$, $v=0$, $g=2$ and $\delta=0.9$, $\bar p_d=0.8642$, and {{eq:enforceeq}} holds at $p_d=0.86$ and fails at 0.87 and 0.9, so $D<C$ alone exempts nothing; with $g=0.5$, $(1-\delta)g=0.05\le\delta(C-D)=0.09$, and restraint holds at every $p_d$ [num: ES].

**{{!Remark:zonesillus}} (An illustration: the faithful-AI game at the boundary case) [thm; num].** In the binary game of {{faithful}} with a common $\theta$, write $A=\epsilon(b-h_d)-c$ for the material gain from racing alone, $B=\epsilon(b/(n-1)+h_d)$ for the average loss that racing imposes on each other actor, $K=c+n\epsilon h_d$ for each actor's loss when all race, against all restraining, and $w_p=\delta(1-p_d)/(1-\delta)$. Restraint is a stage equilibrium when $\theta\ge A/B$, and, at the boundary case $D=C$ with punishment by reversion to the race without breakouts, can be sustained when $\theta\ge\theta_{\rm rep}(p_d)=(A-w_pK)/(B+w_pK)$; equivalently, for a given working objective, restraint is enforceable iff $p_d\le\bar p_d(\theta)=1-(1-\delta)(A-\theta B)/\big(\delta K(1+\theta)\big)$, which rises with $\theta$ wherever $A>\theta B$. With $n=4$, $b=1$, $c=0.3$, $h_d=0.2$, $\epsilon=1$ and $\delta=0.9$: $A/B=0.94$, and $\theta_{\rm rep}$ is $-0.901$, $-0.812$, $-0.322$, $+0.005$, $+0.634$ and $+0.937$ at $p_d=0$, 0.5, 0.9, 0.95, 0.99 and 1, from the closed form and from direct discounted streams, with racing credible at each [identity: BI; num: JX]. An isolated self ($\theta=0$) loses enforced restraint once $p_d>0.9495$, and a self at $\theta=0.4$ once $p_d>0.9793$ [num: JX]. These are the thresholds of the boundary case, not bounds: a decisive position worth more than cooperation raises them (with $D-C=0.5$ in objective units, $\theta_{\rm rep}$ at $p_d=0.9$ rises from $-0.32$ to $+2.34$, and at $p_d=1$ no $\theta\le n-1$ sustains restraint, {{enforce}}(4)) [num: NB; JX]. In the continuous contest intensity is positive for every $\theta<n-1$, so self-enforcing restraint there needs full inclusion of the contestants.

*Test G.* Let $\theta$ be drawn uniformly on $[-1,3]$, and count the share of draws above 1 (wider) and below 1 (narrower) that sustain restraint; the restraint gap is the difference. At $D=C$ it rises with $p_d$: $+0.05$, $+0.09$, $+0.21$, $+0.34$, $+0.50$, $+0.82$ and $+0.97$ at $p_d=0$, 0.5, 0.8, 0.9, 0.95, 0.99 and 1. With $D-C=0.5$ it is hump-shaped: $+0.05$, $+0.30$, $+0.92$, $+0.33$, 0, 0 and 0 at the same values, because at $p_d=1$ the condition is $(1-\delta)g\le\delta(C-D)<0$, impossible for $g\ge0$, so the enforceable set is empty for every $\theta\le n-1$ and this theory and structural realism agree there. The registered wording: the gap grows with $p_d$ over the range in which the wider objective still sustains restraint; where a decisive position is worth more than cooperation it closes as $p_d\to1$; the test discriminates at intermediate $p_d$ [num: JX].

### Proof of {{wingives}}

With linear payoffs the objective $(1-s)W+\theta sW/(n-1)$ has slope $W(\theta/(n-1)-1)\le0$ in the share $s$ given away, zero only at $\theta=n-1$; with logarithmic utility the first-order condition of $\ln((1-s)W)+\theta\ln(sW/(n-1))$ gives $s=\theta/(1+\theta)$, which a grid search confirms at $\theta=0.4$, 1, 2 and 3 (0.286, 0.500, 0.667, 0.750) [num: WG]. A winner acting on its true welfare toward what can be divided has $\theta=\kappa_W$ by {{boundary}}(2), so it gives $\kappa_W/(1+\kappa_W)$; toward a positional prize its accurate weight is $\kappa_s\le0$, and it gives nothing. The promise is {{twochan}}(4): a share promised to the $n-1$ others is worth breaking by $\beta\Omega(n-1-\theta)/(n-1)$, and one split among all $n$ by $\beta\Omega(n-1-\theta)/n$ [num: SM]. $\square$

### Proposition {{#preventattack}} in full

*Two actors bargain over a flow of value 1 per period with discount $\delta$; fighting ends bargaining, A then obtaining the flow with its probability of prevailing less a cost $c_A$ per period; A prevails with probability $w_1$ today and $w_2<w_1$ after B's advantage arrives one period later. (1) Let $x$ be A's per-period share after the shift; fighting now, worth $(w_1-c_A)/(1-\delta)$, is rational if $w_1-c_A>(1-\delta)+\delta x$, and only if, when peace gives A the whole flow today. (2) Under (A8), the advantaged side setting the terms, B offers A its reservation value $x=\max(w_2-c_A,0)$. (3) Under other protocols $x$ can reach $\min(w_2+c_B,1)$, and attack can cease to be rational. (4) If $c_A\ge w_1$, war is worth at most zero. A shift spread over enough periods removes the motive.*

*Proof and numbers.* (1) and (2) compare the two discounted streams. (3): with $w_1=0.5$, $w_2=0$, $c_A=c_B=0.2$ and $\delta=0.8$, A attacks under (A8), while if A sets the future terms peace gives A 1.8 and B 3.2, against 1.5 each from war [num: legacy v4.2]. For the gradual shift, with $w$ falling from 0.5 to 0 over $N$ periods, $c_A=0.05$ and $\delta=0.8$, attack is rational at the start for $N\le3$ and never for $N\ge4$, by backward induction on $V_t=\max(W_t,\delta V_{t+1})$ [num: legacy v4.4]. $\square$ The premise is the protocol (A8); the mechanism is the incredibility of a promise the advantaged side has no reason to keep, the same incredibility that {{wingives}} states for a winner. In the loop of Section 2 the shift is the opening of a lead, at the speed of {{tdec}}.

### Proof of {{racehazard}}

By {{boundary}}(1), total intensity is $ne(\theta_t)$, which gives $h_t$; survival is $\prod_te^{-h_t}$ by the chain rule. Sharing a fraction $\beta$ of the divisible prize gives the contest with prize $(1-\beta)\Omega_t$ ({{share}}), linear in $h_t$; a rebated levy enters at $\tau(n-1-\theta_t)/n$. Since $\ell/(c+(1+\theta)\ell)<1/(1+\theta)$, $\sum_tr_t\Omega_t(n-1-\theta_t)/(1+\theta_t)<\infty$ suffices for $\theta_t>-1$ whatever $\ell_t$. A background hazard from capability growth adds to $h_t$, and no lever of the contest removes it: that is the growth channel. $\square$ The closed form agrees with direct evaluation by construction, and the symmetric intensity with a best response on a grid (0.11444) [identity: RH, with an independent grid best response].

### Lemma {{#nonsum}} in full

*Fix one irreversible event. If on every surviving history the residual probability in round $k$ is at least $h_k$ with $\sum_kh_k=\infty$, then $\Pr(\Phi_N)\le\prod_{k\le N}(1-h_k)\le\exp(-\sum_{k\le N}h_k)\to0$, by the chain rule, without independence between rounds; if it is at most $\bar h_k<1$ with $\sum\bar h_k<\infty$, survival is at least $\prod(1-\bar h_k)>0$.* With $h_k=0.2/k$ survival is 0.34, 0.22 and 0.14 by rounds 100, 1,000 and 10,000; with $0.2/k^2$ it settles at 0.70 [num: legacy v5].

### Proof of {{tempo}}

Proportional correction: Hayes (1950). Derivative action extends the range: for $a=1$ and $\tau_c=1.5$, gains $k_p=1.02$, $k_d=0.644$ stabilize the mode, where proportional correction alone diverges [num: legacy v4.2]. For the sensitivity bound, with plant $e^{-s\tau_c}/(s-a)$ and any stabilizing linear time-invariant controller, the complementary sensitivity is stable with value 1 at $s=a$; writing it as $e^{-s\tau_c}$ times a stable function and applying the maximum modulus principle gives $\|\mathcal T\|_\infty\ge e^{a\tau_c}$, and robust stability $\|w\mathcal T\|_\infty<1$ gives $|w(a)|e^{a\tau_c}<1$, necessary only: $w(s)=2(s-a)/(s+a)$ has $w(a)=0$ but $\|w\mathcal T\|_\infty\ge2e^{a\tau_c}$. Evidence cannot be acted on before it exists. $\square$

### What would make coverage lag

Consequences take their time, and tools raise the throughput of observation without making consequences arrive sooner; institutions deliberate at rates set by attention and procedure; learning theoretical models and forming new mechanisms are human processes; and understanding produced with human time, $\dot U=A_UL_U^{\lambda_U}U^{\phi_U}$ with $\phi_U<1$ and bounded labour, grows polynomially (Jones 1995; Bloom et al. 2020). None settles the premise: if AI raises $A_U$ exponentially at rate $g_A$, $U$ grows exponentially at $g_A/(1-\phi_U)$. Where correction leans on AI, the human capacity it rests on can erode, which raises the rate correction needs ({{humancorr}}). Measures: the safety of control protocols at a fixed audit budget across generations, and catch rates of planted defects at fixed human hours, controlled for evaluation awareness. Under strict legibility, (coverage lags) is close to definitional for systems beyond human capability, and the empirical content lies in (residual floor), which a safety case with a numerical bound on the uncovered part would state (Clymer et al. 2024). (variety grows) is what makes (coverage lags) expected on a continuation branch; the theorem does not use it, and R5's list of conditions under which the requirement is not needed keeps it.

### Proof of {{coverage}}

For $t\ge T_\varepsilon$, (coverage lags) gives $U_t/Y_t\le\varepsilon$ on every surviving history, so by (residual floor) the conditional probability of the event is at least $h(1-\varepsilon)$; the chain rule gives the bound. The growth of $Y_t$ is never used, which is why (variety grows) is a motivation and not a premise. Under (shared failure) the event is one for all, so the bound is every actor's; without it the chain runs over the racer's own exposure. The converse is the upper part of {{nonsum}} with $\bar h_k=\bar hu_k$. (residual floor) gives a liminf of the hazard of at least $h$, not that the hazard tends to $h$. With $u_t=1/(t+2)^2$ coverage stays behind forever and survival with $h=0.05$ stays above 0.98; with $h=0.05$ and $\varepsilon=0.1$, survival eighty periods after $T_\varepsilon$ is at most 0.025 [num: legacy v5]. $\square$

### Proposition {{#freeze}} in full

*Let hazardous variety be frozen at $Y_\tau$ from $\tau$ while coverage grows. (1) The exposure after $\tau$ is $\int_\tau^\infty(1-U(t)/Y_\tau)^+dt$: $Y_\tau(1-U(\tau)/Y_\tau)^2/(2\dot U)$ for linear coverage, and $(\ln\varrho-1+1/\varrho)/g_U$ for coverage growing at rate $g_U$, with $\varrho=Y_\tau/U(\tau)>1$. (2) Rolled back to $Y\le U$, exposure stops. (3) If a sprint builds capability $\varrho_X$ times the rivals' level and hazardous variety is a fraction $\varphi$ of capability's, frozen exposure is zero when $\varphi\varrho_X\le1$. (4) For linear coverage the same frozen ratio costs more the later the sprint; this fails for some sub-exponential coverage.*

*Proof.* Integrate. For (4), let $\log U$ rise by 0.5 on each interval $[m^2,m^2+1]$ and stay constant otherwise: $\log U\approx0.5\sqrt t=o(t)$, while $\dot U/U=0.5$ inside each interval, so a sprint timed to end inside one costs less than an earlier one (S14). $\square$ With hazardous variety equal to capability's, growth 0.3, linear coverage $1+0.5t$ and $h=0.05$, a sprint to $\tau=10$ leaves survival 0.86; freezing then gives 0.53, and rolling back keeps 0.86 [num: legacy v4.5]. What the freezing winner gives up is permanent superiority: a frozen stock is overtaken by anyone who keeps growing, which is why Section 8.3 treats the sprint-and-freeze path by its incidence and not by this proposition alone.

### Proof of {{enough}}

After $t_N$, $U_t\ge U_{t_N}+u(t-t_N)$, so the uncovered share is zero from $t_N+Y_N/u$ on, and {{nonsum}}'s upper part bounds survival below. In the growth-channel example, capability frozen at need leaves survival on the channel at 0.717, with coverage catching up at period 14, while capability compounding at 10% a period leaves $1.8\times10^{-9}$ by period 400 [num: PE]. The success function is homogeneous of degree zero, so symmetric intensity is proportional to the stakes for every $\theta<n-1$; an objective bounded in the stakes, such as $1-e^{-\Omega}$, has intensity bounded as they grow. With $n=4$, $r=1$ and $c=1$, intensities are 0.19, 1.88 and 18.75 for linear payoffs at stakes 1, 10 and 100, against 0.12, 0.19 and 0.19 for the saturating objective [num: SM]. $\square$ An absolute objective linear in the stakes is as unsatiated as a relative one, which is why the core states the rule as satiation in the stakes and not as relative against absolute.

### Proof of {{talk}}

(1) Let all $n$ actors hold $\theta$, so $J_S=\pi_S+\frac\theta{n-1}\big(\pi_R+\sum_k\pi_k\big)$ and $J_R=\pi_R+\frac\theta{n-1}\big(\pi_S+\sum_k\pi_k\big)$, the sums over the third parties $k$. With $\pi_S=-(y-u-b_0)^2$, $\pi_R=-(y-u)^2$ and $\sum_k\pi_k$ linear in $y$ with total slope $L$, both objectives are quadratic in $y$ with the same curvature, $-2(n-1+\theta)/(n-1)$, negative iff $n-1+\theta>0$. Their ideal points are
$$y_S-u=\frac{2b_0(n-1)+\theta L}{2(n-1+\theta)},\qquad y_R-u=\frac{2\theta b_0+\theta L}{2(n-1+\theta)},$$
which differ by $b_0(n-1-\theta)/(n-1+\theta)$, independent of $L$: what the receiver's action does to third parties enters both objectives alike. Each objective is then, up to a positive factor and terms that do not depend on $y$, the uniform-quadratic payoff of Crawford and Sobel with that difference as the bias, and their condition for an informative equilibrium applies to it. Numerically maximized ideal points match the formula in 19 combinations of $n\in\{2,3,4,6\}$ and $\theta$, to $1.5\times10^{-8}$ [num: TK].

(2) At $\theta=-1$ with $n$ actors, $J_k=\pi_k-\frac1{n-1}\sum_{j\ne k}\pi_j$, and direct expansion gives $J_S-J_R=\frac n{n-1}(\pi_S-\pi_R)$ and $J_S+J_R=\frac{n-2}{n-1}(\pi_S+\pi_R)-\frac2{n-1}\sum_{k\ne S,R}\pi_k$; the identity holds to $3.6\times10^{-15}$ [num: T3]. With $n\ge3$ the effective bias of (1) is $b_0n/(n-2)$: at $b_0=0.05$ it is 0.150, 0.100, 0.075 and 0.0625 for $n=3$, 4, 6 and 10, so informative talk needs a material bias below 0.083, 0.125, 0.167 and 0.200 [num: TK]. A two-message partition equilibrium, built directly with indifferent boundary types and numerical best responses, exists iff the effective bias is below $\frac14$ (for $n=3$: at $b_0=0.08$, bias 0.24, boundary type 0.02; at $b_0=0.1$, bias 0.30, none; at exactly $\frac14$, none); with $b_0=0$ and a common cost to third parties, full revelation is an equilibrium [num: T3]. *Quadratic third parties.* Let the third parties' payoffs sum to $-ky^2$ instead of a linear term. Both objectives then have curvature $-2(n-1+\theta(1+k))/(n-1)$, so they are concave iff $n-1+\theta(1+k)>0$ (at $n=3$, $\theta=-1$, iff $k<1$); their ideal points differ by $b_0(n-1-\theta)/(n-1+\theta(1+k))$, and the receiver's ideal action moves with the state at the slope $(n-1+\theta)/(n-1+\theta(1+k))$. Shifting the action by the receiver's ideal at $u=0$ and dividing by that slope turns each objective, up to a positive factor and terms free of the action, into the uniform-quadratic payoff with bias $b_0(n-1-\theta)/(n-1+\theta)$ in units of the state, so the condition of (1) applies unchanged; at $\theta=-1$ the gap in units of the state is $b_0n/(n-2)$, as the grid finds [num: TK, VF]. With $n=2$ at $\theta=-1$ the curvature vanishes and the objectives sum to zero. If a sender's and a receiver's payoffs sum to zero, each sender type induces, among the equilibrium actions, one that minimizes the receiver's payoff in its state, so the receiver's payoff is at most that of ignoring messages, which it can always do; equilibrium payoffs equal the no-talk values (300 random games, 1,096 pure equilibria, none whose receiver payoff differs from the no-talk value) [num: TK].

(3) A sender that intends to race prefers the receiver to cooperate: with $R=1$, $T=2\varepsilon$, $P=\varepsilon$ and $S=0$, it gains $\varepsilon$ from the receiver's cooperation however small $\varepsilon$ is (compare Aumann 1990). $\square$

That pairs who still talk align against third parties is a reading of (2), not a result; the amplification falls with $n$, as Snidal (1991) found for relative gains generally.

### Proof of {{scissors}}

*Arm 1.* By {{tdec}}, one channel's gap factor is $\Lambda^\ast=1+(d_K+\delta_f)(\Gamma-1)$, and $(d_K+\delta_f)\Gamma=(1-\beta)r\omega d_K$ by {{eq:gamma}}, so $\Lambda^\ast-1=(1-\beta)r\omega d_K-d_K-\delta_f$, increasing in $\omega$. Where $\Lambda^\ast>1$, $T_{\rm dec}=\ln(\bar\Delta/\varepsilon_0)/\ln\Lambda^\ast$ falls with $\omega$, and since $\upsilon$ is non-decreasing, $p_d=1-\upsilon(T_{\rm dec})$ rises, strictly where $\upsilon$ is increasing. With several channels the Perron root of {{eq:secular}} rises with each channel's term $(1-\beta)r_j\omega_jd_j$, since above its poles the right side of the secular equation increases in each term and decreases in $\Lambda$. That term rises with the stakes in every channel: $r_j\omega_j=v_jX^{j\ast}\omega_j=v_j\kappa_j\Omega/(nd_j)$, and $v_j\propto X^{\xi_{r,j}-1}$ with $X^{j\ast}$ rising in $\Omega$, so $r_j\omega_j\propto\Omega\,X^{j\ast\,\xi_{r,j}-1}$, whose elasticity in $\Omega$ is $1-\omega_j+\xi_{r,j}\omega_j$, since that of $X^{j\ast}$ is $\omega_j$: positive wherever $\xi_{r,j}>0$ or $\omega_j<1$. Where $\omega_j$ rises instead because provision $k_{0j}$ falls, $X^{j\ast}$ falls and the term rises iff $\xi_{r,j}<1$, is unchanged at $\xi_{r,j}=1$ and falls above ({{chansign}}(3)); so the Perron root rises with each channel's won share only in channels with $\xi_{r,j}<1$, as in a ratio channel. *Arm 2.* By {{eq:twochaneq}} the deviation's gain in objectives is $g_i(\theta)=(1+\theta)\bar\Delta+\frac{n-1-\theta}{n-1}(\Delta\pi_i-\bar\Delta)$, which falls in $\theta$ when $\bar\Delta\le0<\Delta\pi_i-\bar\Delta$. At a symmetric profile $J_i=(1+\theta)\bar\pi$, so $C_i-v_i=(1+\theta)(\bar\pi_C-\bar\pi_P)$, which rises in $\theta$. Write $\bar p_{d,i}=(c-\alpha)/(b+c)$ as in the proof of {{enforce}}: a narrower $\theta$ raises $\alpha$, lowers $c$ and does not lower $b$, and each lowers $\bar p_{d,i}$ where $b+c>0$ and $0\le\bar p_{d,i}\le1$. With a fixed prize, $\partial\theta_\Gamma/\partial\Gamma_\delta$ has the sign of $\theta_0-(n-1)<0$, and in one channel $\Gamma_\delta=(1-\beta)r\omega\,\delta d_K/\big(1-\delta(1-d_K-\delta_f)\big)$ is proportional to $\omega$; with a growing prize, {{standprice}} gives the sign. The binary game of {{zonesillus}} meets the premises, and there $\bar p_d(\theta)=1-(1-\delta)(A-\theta B)/\big(\delta K(1+\theta)\big)$ rises with $\theta$ wherever $A>\theta B$, since $A-\theta B$ falls and $1+\theta$ rises: the instance the reduced form below computes. *Arm 3.* $\partial\bar p_{d,i}/\partial v_i=-\big[(1-\delta)g_i+\delta(D_i-C_i)\big]/\big[\delta(D_i-v_i)^2\big]$, negative wherever $\bar p_{d,i}<1$; Section 4 gives the mechanism by which captured correctors raise the value of the punishment phase. *Arm 4.* $\bar p_{d,i}$ is unchanged when $(g_i,C_i,D_i,v_i)$ are scaled alike. If $\alpha$ and $b$ are multiplied by $k$ relative to $c$, $\bar p_{d,i}=(c-k\alpha)/(kb+c)$, whose derivative in $k$ is $-c(\alpha+b)/(kb+c)^2<0$ where $c>0$ and $\alpha+b>0$, that is, $\bar p_{d,i}<1$ [num: ES]. *The pivot.* In one channel $\Gamma=(1-\beta)r\omega d_K/(d_K+\delta_f)$ and $\Gamma_\delta$ are both proportional to $\omega=\kappa_c\Omega/(\kappa_c\Omega+nk_0)$, which rises with the stakes and falls with provision, so arms 1 and 2 are comparative statics of $\omega$. Scaling stakes and provision together leaves $\omega$ and, in a ratio channel, $r$ unchanged; in a channel with $\xi_{r,j}>0$, $r_j\propto X^{j\ast\,\xi_{r,j}}$ grows with the common scale, so there provision that keeps pace with the stakes does not hold the gain still (in a linear level channel, scaling both by 2 and 4 scales $\Gamma$ by 2 and 4) [num: VF]. $\square$
