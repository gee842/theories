## S6. AI: instrument and incarnation

This chapter gives the proofs and numerics of Section 6, the full statements of the three results the core states in a sentence (Propositions {{#homeostasis}} and {{#opaquecorr}}, Remark {{#lyapunov}}), and three supplementary results: how certain a lock must be for narrow E1 to need no trust (Remark {{#nearlock}}), the formal part of the masked class, and the bounded optimizer (Remark {{#boundedopt}}). ### Proof of Proposition {{#faithful}}

The harm coefficient, $d$ in V6.1, is written $h_d$, since $d$ is the correction rate; its exponent in (3) is $\beta_h$ (the log of SM(5) writes $\beta_d$).

Changing $a_i$ from 0 to 1 changes $u_i$ by $\epsilon b-c-\epsilon h_d$, independently of the others' choices, and each other $u_j$ by $-\epsilon b/(n-1)-\epsilon h_d$. Summed over all principals the relative terms cancel: at all-racing each principal has $-c-n\epsilon h_d$, which gives (1). In objectives, racing changes $J_i$ by $\epsilon b\big(1-\frac{\theta}{n-1}\big)-c-\epsilon h_d(1+\theta)$, which is positive iff $\theta<\theta^\ast$; this gives (2). For (3), write $\theta^\ast=(T-H-c)/(T/(n-1)+H)$ with $T=\epsilon b$ and $H=\epsilon h_d$ growing with exponents $1+\beta_b$ and $1+\beta_h$, and $c$ with $\beta_c$. If $\beta_c<1+\max(\beta_b,\beta_h)$, $c$ is negligible against the larger of $T$ and $H$, and the limit is set by $T/H$, which is constant, unbounded or vanishing as $\beta_b$ equals, exceeds or falls short of $\beta_h$. If $\beta_c>1+\max(\beta_b,\beta_h)$, the numerator tends to $-\infty$ faster than the denominator grows. $\square$

*Numerics* ($n=4$, $b=1$, $h_d=0.2$, $c=0.3$). Exponents $(\beta_b,\beta_h,\beta_c)=(0,0,0)$ and $(1,1,0)$ give the limit 1.50; $(0,0.5,0)$ gives $-0.99$ at $\epsilon=10^6$; $(0.5,0,0)$ gives 2.998; $(1,0,3)$ and $(0,0,1.5)$ diverge to $-\infty$; at the boundary $\beta_c=1+\max(\beta_b,\beta_h)$, $(0,0,1)$ gives 0.9375 and $(1,0,2)$ gives 2.1 [SM(5)]. With $b$, $h_d$ and $c$ fixed, $\theta^\ast$ is 0.9375 at $\epsilon=1$ and 1.44 at $\epsilon=10$; with $c$ also scaling with $\epsilon$ it stays 0.94 [AL(6.1)]. The double edge: at $\epsilon=1$, all-racing gives each principal $-1.1$; lowering $h_d$ to 0.1 and 0.05 cuts the loss to $-0.70$ and $-0.50$ while raising $\theta^\ast$ to 1.39 and 1.70, and $\Delta J_i$ changes sign at $\theta^\ast$ [AL(6.1)]. The condition once written as $\beta_c<\beta_b=\beta_d$ was neither necessary nor sufficient; the correct one is $\beta_c<1+\max(\beta_b,\beta_h)$.

### Proof of Proposition {{#delegate}}

Actor $i$'s payoff is $-L_e(1-w_i)-L_c\big[1-\prod_j(1-\psi_j)\big]$, with $w_i=(1+s\iota_i)/\sum_j(1+s\iota_j)$. (1) With $k$ others delegating, $w_i(1,k)=(1+s)/(n+(k+1)s)$ and $w_i(0,k)=1/(n+ks)$. The first is non-decreasing in $s$ and the second decreasing, so the gain $L_e[w_i(1,k)-w_i(0,k)]$ increases in $s$, toward $L_e(1-1/n)$ for $k=0$ and $L_e/(k+1)$ for $k\ge1$. The cost is the change in shared survival, $L_c\Delta\psi(1-\psi_0)^{n-1-k}(1-\psi_1)^k$, independent of $s$. If every limit exceeds its cost, there is a speed beyond which delegating is strictly better at every $k$; if one limit falls short, delegating is worse at that $k$ for every $s$. The smallest limit is $L_e/n$ and the largest cost $L_c\Delta\psi$, so $L_e/n>L_c\Delta\psi$ suffices. (2) With all delegating $w_i=1/n$, as with none, and shared survival is $(1-\psi_1)^n<(1-\psi_0)^n$. (3) Under relative objectives the shared term is common to all and cancels; the gain in $w_i$ remains, and is positive for every $s>0$. $\square$

*Numerics.* With $n=3$, $L_e=L_c$, $\psi_0=0.01$ and $\psi_1=0.2$, delegation is dominant from $s^\ast=1.1628$, where a lone delegator wins with probability 0.5196 against $1/3$ without delegation [AL(6.2)].

### Proof of Lemma {{#lockin}}, and where $q_L$ comes from

With $\dot{\mathcal M}=-\delta_h\mathcal M$ under delegation and $\dot{\mathcal M}=\varrho_h(\bar{\mathcal M}-\mathcal M)$ otherwise, capacity reaches $\mathcal M_{\min}$ after $\ln(\mathcal M_0/\mathcal M_{\min})/\delta_h$ of delegation, and from $\mathcal M<\mathcal M_{\min}$ it regains $\mathcal M_{\min}$ after $\varrho_h^{-1}\ln[(\bar{\mathcal M}-\mathcal M)/(\bar{\mathcal M}-\mathcal M_{\min})]$. Capacity maintained in parallel does not decay; the resources it uses are withdrawn from strength. $\square$

The lock probability $q_L$ of Proposition {{#delegatelock}} is therefore not free. It is the probability that the delegated decision is irreversible within the window for deliberation, or that a delegation lasts longer than $T_{\rm lock}$ in a contest that does not allow the recovery time. Drilled parallel capacity keeps the second term near zero at a cost in strength, which is why it has to be held in common. The same capacity carries correction: where the effective correction rate is $d(\mathcal M/\mathcal M_0)^w$ and $\mathcal M$ decays at $\delta_h$, the rate at which independent correction must grow rises by $w\delta_h$ ({{humancorr}}; S4, block QS).

### Proposition {{#delegatelock}} in full, and proof

*Let locked delegation end the delegator's ability to overrule with probability $q_L$, and losing the contest mean subordination with probability $p$. With $Q=\prod_j(1-\psi_j)$ and winning probabilities as in Proposition {{#delegate}}, actor $i$'s survival is $\Phi_i=(1-q_L\iota_i)\,Q\,[w_i+(1-w_i)(1-p)]$. Write $a=(1-\psi_1)/(1-\psi_0)$ and, with $k$ others delegating, $w_D=(1+s)/(n+(k+1)s)$ and $w_N=1/(n+ks)$, so that*
$$\frac{\Phi_D}{\Phi_N}=(1-q_L)\,a\,\frac{1-p+p\,w_D}{1-p+p\,w_N}.$$
*(1) If $q_L=1$ and $Q>0$, not delegating gives strictly higher survival for every finite $s$, every $n$, every $p$ and every profile of the others. (2) If $p=1$ and $A=(1-q_L)a$, all delegating is an equilibrium iff $A(n+(n-1)s)/n\ge1$, and no one delegating iff $An(1+s)/(n+s)\le1$. At least one always holds, since $A(n+(n-1)s)/n-An(1+s)/(n+s)=A(n-1)s^2/[n(n+s)]\ge0$, and both can. (3) If $p<1$, the ratio can fall as $k$ rises; where it decreases in $k$, from above one at $k=m-1$ to below one at $k=m$ for some $1\le m\le n-1$, the profiles with $m$ delegators are the only pure equilibria, and neither corner is one. (4) At bounded speed one delegator does not drive another's survival to zero: with $p=1$ a non-delegator facing $k$ delegators survives with probability at least $Q_N/(n+ks)>0$.*

*Proof.* (1) $\Phi_D=0$ while $\Phi_N\ge Q_Nw_N>0$. (2) At $p=1$ the ratio is $A(1+s)(n+ks)/(n+(k+1)s)$; take $k=n-1$ for all delegating and $k=0$ for none, and the identity gives "at least one". (3) A profile with $m$ delegators is an equilibrium iff the ratio is at least one at $k=m-1$, so that no delegator gains by stopping, and at most one at $k=m$, so that no other actor gains by starting. (4) Immediate. $\square$

*Numerics.* Codex's case, $n=3$, $s=1$, $p=0.1$, $q_L=0$, $\psi_0=0$, $\psi_1=0.015$: the ratios for $k=0,1,2$ are 1.00259, 1.00097 and 0.99928, so the only pure equilibrium has two delegators; with $p=1$ the ratios rise with $k$, 1.48, 1.58 and 1.64, and all delegate [DL(a), (b)]. Over 20,000 random draws with $q_L<1$, neither corner is an equilibrium in 0 draws at $p=1$, 1,586 at $p=0.5$ and 60 at $p=0.1$ [DL(c)]. Part (1) holds in 20,000 of 20,000 random settings [DL(d)]. At $p=1$, over 100,000 draws "neither" never occurs and "both" occurs 4,068 times [SM(6)]; an independent recomputation with another sampling distribution finds 0 and 21,922, with the closed forms equal to the direct survival comparison in every draw [AL(6.4)]. With $n=2$, $q_L=0.99$, $\psi_0=0.01$ and $\psi_1=0.05$, all delegating is an equilibrium only for $s\ge206.4$, with survival 0.0045 there [AL(6.4)].

**{{!Remark:nearlock}} (How certain the lock must be) [thm].** *In the model of Proposition {{#delegatelock}}, not delegating gives strictly higher survival for every $n$, every $p$ and every profile of the others whenever $(1-q_L)(1+s)(1-\psi_1)<1-\psi_0$.* Since $w_N\le w_D\le(1+s)w_N$, the ratio $(1-p+pw_D)/(1-p+pw_N)$ lies between 1 and $1+s$, so $\Phi_D/\Phi_N\le(1-q_L)a(1+s)<1$. The bound is approached as $n\to\infty$ with $k=0$ and $p=1$, where the ratio is $(1-q_L)a(1+s)\,n/(n+s)$. A lock is near certain in the sense of narrow E1 when its failure probability is below $(1-\psi_0)/\big((1-\psi_1)(1+s)\big)$: the larger the speed advantage, the nearer one the lock must be for keeping AI out to need no trust.

### Proofs of Lemma {{#retain}} and Remark {{#transmit}}

*Lemma {{#retain}}.* Variants acting on common terms exist and have relative performance lower by at least a positive margin, so under cumulative retention their share tends to zero, as in {{load}}; the retained systems' decisions are the actor's. If constraints are an enforced admission condition, no admissible variant violates them. That an actor which cannot overrule decisions taken in its name has lost O2 in that domain is a definition. For a reader who counts survival only as preservation of the unit, delegation acts through the shared hazard instead: delegated systems interacting at machine speed without a human veto raise the probability of shared loss by reciprocal fear of surprise attack (Schelling 1960), inadvertent escalation (Posen 1991) and flash war (Scharre 2018), an argument with cited mechanisms, not a derivation.

*Remark {{#transmit}}.* A faithful system maximizes its operator's objective. Under a relative payoff that objective puts a weight on winning, equivalent to a self-model $\theta<0$ ({{twochan}}), and where reinvestment's relative return exceeds its common return the working objective narrows further ({{standprice}}). A more effective system raises the value the objective attains, not its content.

### Proposition {{#homeostasis}}: risk homeostasis in delegation

**{{!Proposition:homeostasis}} (Risk homeostasis in delegation) [thm].** Inside the contest, gains in the safety of delegation are partly absorbed by more delegation. *In the model of Proposition {{#delegate}}, let each actor choose $\iota_i\in[0,1]$, with strength $1+s\iota_i$ and hazard $\psi_0+\Delta\psi\,\iota_i$; write $x=\Delta\psi\,\iota$, $b_s=\Delta\psi/s$, $q=1-\psi_0$ and $K_h=(n-1)L_e/(n^2L_c)$. An interior symmetric equilibrium solves $(x+b_s)(q-x)^{n-1}=K_h$. (1) Where $x\ll q$, the added hazard is $nx\approx(n-1)L_e/(nL_cq^{n-1})-n\Delta\psi/s$, nearly independent of $\Delta\psi$, until delegation saturates. (2) There can be several interior equilibria, and along a branch $\partial x/\partial\Delta\psi=-(q-x)/\big(s[q-nx-(n-1)b_s]\big)$, whose sign changes between branches. (3) After saturation, realized risk falls with $\Delta\psi$ and can be summable under full delegation and growing speed: with $n=3$, $L_e=L_c=1$, $\psi_0=0$, $s_t=2^t$ and $\Delta\psi_t=2^{-t}$, full delegation is an equilibrium at every $t$, the realized hazard at $t=0$ is 1, and from $t=5$ the realized risk is at most $3/16$. (4) With all-or-nothing delegation, any equilibrium with $k\ge1$ delegators has realized hazard above the baseline, so an improvement that carries $\Delta\psi$ across the threshold at which delegation becomes an equilibrium raises realized hazard.*

*Proof.* At the symmetric point $\partial w_i/\partial\iota_i=s(n-1)/(n^2(1+s\iota))$, and the marginal cost is $L_c\Delta\psi(1-\psi)^{n-1}$ with $1-\psi=q-x$; equate them and divide by $s$. (1) approximates for $x\ll q$. (2) differentiates implicitly. (3) The own marginal gain at full delegation, $2s_t/(9(1+s_t))-\Delta\psi_t(1-\Delta\psi_t)^2$, is positive at every $t$, and the objective is concave in own delegation; the union bound gives $\sum_{t\ge5}3\cdot2^{-t}=3/16$. (4) $(1-\psi_0-\Delta\psi)^k<(1-\psi_0)^k$. $\square$

*Numerics.* (2): with $n=2$, $s=100$, $L_e=0.5$, $L_c=1$, $\psi_0=0.01$ and $\Delta\psi=0.9$ there are two interior roots, $\iota=0.153$ and $0.937$, and the corner is also an equilibrium [v5 HO, run by `checks_v62.py --legacy`]. (3): the own marginal gain at full delegation is $+0.111$, $+0.023$, $+0.037$, $+0.102$ and $+0.154$ for $t=0,\dots,4$ and rises after, full delegation is a best response on a grid at $t=0,\dots,7$, and the exact realized risk from $t=5$ is 0.1836 [SM(12); AL(6.11)]. (1): with $n=3$, $s=100$, $L_e=1$, $L_c=100$ and $\psi_0=0.01$, the added hazard is 0.0011, 0.0053, 0.0062, 0.0065 and 0.0067 as $\Delta\psi$ falls from 0.19 through 0.05, 0.02 and 0.01 to 0.005, and falls to 0.0030 only at complete delegation, $\Delta\psi=0.001$ [AL(6.11)]. Compare Stafford, Trager and Dafoe (2022) on safety–performance trade-offs in races, and the risk compensation of Peltzman (1975) and Wilde (1982).

### Proof of Proposition {{#nested}}

(1) Level 2 is relative and payoff-monotone, so {{blind}}(1) removes every term common to all developers.

(2) Level 0 maximizes its reward by definition. Level 1 chooses rewards and constraints to maximize the developer's objective, which counts privately visible divergences through customers, liability and reputation. Under $(A_\tau)$, countering lowers the developer's payoff relative to its matched variant by a positive margin net of every private benefit, so under cumulative selection its share relative to that variant tends to zero ({{load}}, with developers as carriers and the matched variant as the carrier otherwise identical). Without a matched variant the comparison is between different developers: one whose other advantages exceed the tax grows against unsafe rivals that lack them, for as long as those advantages last.

(3) A constraint borne by one developer lowers its relative payoff, which (2) selects against. A constraint enforced on all as a condition of admission is a common term among compliers when its incidence is equal ({{blind}}(2)); when unequal, its cost differences are payoff differences among compliers only, and they re-sort the compliers. Each type $k$ of developer can comply at cost $c_k$ or evade against an expected sanction $\bar F_k$. Within a type, evasion is selected out iff $\bar F_k\ge c_k$ ({{blind}}(3)). Across types, $\bar F_k\ge c_k$ for every type suffices, but is not necessary: a type whose evasion pays within its type can still lose to another type's compliers. $\square$

*Numerics.*
- *The premise.* A unilateral safeguard that cuts the error rate from 0.20 to 0.01 at cost 0.01 is selected for (its share tends to one), which is the case $(A_\tau)$ excludes; a safeguard whose benefit is common is selected away [CI(e)].
- *Several types* (compliance costs 0.1 and 0.2). With sanctions $(1,1)$ or $(0.15,0.25)$ compliance takes over; with $(1,0.15)$ it also takes over although the second type's evasion pays within its type; with $(1,0.05)$ evasion takes over [CI(h)].
- *Incidence.* With unequal compliance costs and sanction 1, the compliant share is 0.999999975 at $t=20$ and the compliers sort 0.88 to 0.12; with equal incidence their relative shares are preserved exactly [CI(i)].
- *The matched variant.* A safeguarded developer with baseline 2 and tax 0.1 outgrows an unsafe one with baseline 1 (its share tends to one); against its matched twin its share falls to $2\times10^{-9}$ by $t=200$ [CI(i)], and to $1.4\times10^{-87}$ in a second implementation [CI(j)]. With zero tax shares are unchanged [CI(i)].
- *A tax paid from a lead.* A tax of 0.01 paid from an independent advantage of 0.02 survives; from an advantage of 0.005 it does not [CI(j); AL(6.7)].

*Interface.* What level 2 selects on is the developers' relative payoff, of which the trained systems' contribution to relative standing is part. Under a public mandate, or a national laboratory that only moves relative selection to the level of states, the result applies at that level or not at all.

### Remark {{#masked}}: the formal part

Let admission detect a divergence, or the absence of the countering that removes it, with probability $\pi$, and impose a sanction $F$; the variant that does not counter it saves the tax $\tau$. Within a type, Proposition {{#nested}}(3) gives: the countering variant survives cumulative selection iff $\pi F\ge\tau$. With $\tau=0.3$, an expected sanction of 0.25 leaves the share of the non-countering variant at one, and an expected sanction of 0.35 removes it [CI(b)].

Masking enters through $\pi$. Where an evaluation reads behaviour that the system controls, and the system behaves differently when evaluated, the evaluation detects the divergence only as often as it shows under evaluation; a developer's own tests face the same bound, so the countering it pays for is unverifiable to it too, and $(A_\tau)$'s premise that countering is a choice with a price fails for this class. If $\pi$ falls toward zero as capability rises while $F$ is bounded by exclusion, the largest sanction admission holds, there is a capability beyond which no admissible sanction keeps the divergence out. Evidence that the system's behaviour does not control (weights, activations, training data, elicitation by fine-tuning) raises $\pi$; a control protocol that does not assume the system's cooperation bounds harm directly instead of through detection, which is why the admission condition for this class is stated in capability. How $\pi$ scales with capability at fixed auditor access is crux C12; audit games with planted hidden objectives (Marks et al. 2025) measure it.

### Relative selection inside training (Section 6.4)

In a Bradley–Terry model the probability that response $y_1$ is preferred to $y_2$ is $\sigma(r(y_1)-r(y_2))$. Adding the same harm to third parties to both responses leaves it unchanged: 0.7110 with shared harm 0 and with shared harm $-5$ [AL(BT)]. If abstaining is among the responses compared and the rater sees the harm, complying (quality 1.3, harm $-5$) is preferred to abstaining (0, 0) with probability 0.024 [AL(BT)]. Since only differences enter, a reward fitted to comparisons is identified up to an additive constant for each context, and whether to act at all is not in it.

### The bounded optimizer (Section 6.4)

**{{!Remark:boundedopt}} (Bounded objectives, cheap resources) [thm].** *An optimizer that maximizes $1-e^{-R}-cR$ over resources $R$ acquires $R^\ast=\ln(1/c)$, without bound as $c\to0$; a satisficer that stops at success probability $1-\varepsilon_s$ acquires $\ln(1/\varepsilon_s)$, whatever $c$.* The first-order condition is $e^{-R}=c$. With $c=0.1$, 0.01, 0.001 and $10^{-6}$ the optimizer acquires 2.30, 4.61, 6.91 and 13.8, while the satisficer with $\varepsilon_s=0.05$ stays at 3.0 [AL(bounded)]. In the contest the same falling cost is harmless to a mind that counts the shared loss: at the symmetric equilibrium of {{boundary}}(1) with $n=4$ and a shared loss of 0.3 per unit, intensity under $\theta=-1$ rises from 0.25 to 2.5, 25 and 250 as the private cost falls from 1 to 0.1, 0.01 and 0.001, under $\theta=-0.5$ it reaches 1.449, and under $\theta=0$ it rises only from 0.144 to 0.623 [AL(contest)]. A bounded goal plays the part of a bounded prize, not of satiation: what bounds acquisition is a term that counts its cost to others, the analogue of $\theta>-1$, or a rule that stops, the analogue of satiation.

### Correction that people cannot understand (Section 6.5)

**{{!Proposition:opaquecorr}} (Correction that people cannot understand) [thm].** A corrector that people cannot follow lowers residual risk wherever it blocks more than it adds, and keeps survival bounded away from zero only if its blocking becomes complete. *Let a correction architecture block a share $c_{b,t}$ of failures at base hazard $q_0>0$ and add a residual hazard $q_{c,t}$ of its own in period $t$. It lowers residual risk in period $t$ iff $q_0c_{b,t}>q_{c,t}$, and makes residual risk summable iff $\sum_t\big[q_0(1-c_{b,t})+q_{c,t}\big]<\infty$, which requires $\sum_t(1-c_{b,t})<\infty$; a fixed $c_b<1$ never does.*

*Scope.* Summable residual risk is what keeps survival bounded away from zero ({{nonsum}}). To show that such correction fails, one must show that its own residual hazard meets the premises of {{coverage}}: coverage that lags and a residual floor. Whether the loss of legibility is a loss of O2 is a separate question, answered by overrulability (Section 6.5).

*Proof.* The residual hazard in period $t$ is $q_0(1-c_{b,t})+q_{c,t}$; compare it with $q_0$. Survival $\prod_t(1-h_t)$ with $h_t<1$ is bounded away from zero iff $\sum_th_t<\infty$ ({{nonsum}}); with $q_0>0$ fixed, $\sum_tq_0(1-c_{b,t})<\infty$ needs $\sum_t(1-c_{b,t})<\infty$, which no fixed $c_b<1$ gives. $\square$

*Example.* With $q_0=0.1$ a period, $c_{b,t}=1-2^{-(t+1)}$ and $q_{c,t}=2^{-(t+2)}$ from $t=0$, survival is 0.5101 at period 200 and at period 20,000, against $7.1\times10^{-10}$ by period 200 without the corrector [SM(11)]. A second summable example, $c_{b,t}=1-2^{-t}$ from $t=1$, gives 0.6888 at both horizons [SM(11)]. The example printed in V6.1, a fixed $c_b=0.99$ with the same $q_{c,t}$, lowers risk but is not summable: survival to period 200 is 0.473 counting from $t=0$ (0.630 counting from $t=1$, the figure V6.1 printed) and of order $10^{-9}$ by period 20,000 [SM(11); AL(6.8)]. V6.1's claim that it made residual risk summable is withdrawn.

### Proof of Proposition {{#topology}}

Each layer covers $m_\ell$ times the attention of the layer above, so $k=\ln(\mathsf A/\mathsf B)/\ln m_\ell$ layers are needed, where $\mathsf A$ is the attention the overseen conduct requires and $\mathsf B$ the attention of the top layer. A serial chain catches a problem only if every link succeeds: $f^k=(\mathsf A/\mathsf B)^{\ln f/\ln m_\ell}$. Independent checks across the gap fail together with probability $(1-f^k)^{k_c}$; a common cause defeating all with probability $q_{\rm cc}$ leaves at most $1-q_{\rm cc}$. Layered redundancy needs every layer to succeed, each with probability $1-(1-f)^{k_c}$. $\square$ (The number of checks, $m$ in V6.1, is written $k_c$, since $m$ is masking.)

*Numerics* ($f=0.9$, $k=6$). The serial chain catches 0.531; six checks across the gap, 36 layer-checks, catch 0.989, or 0.940 under a common cause with $q_{\rm cc}=0.05$; layered redundancy catches 0.941 with two checks per layer (12 layer-checks) and 0.994 with three (18) [SM(13); AL(6.9)]. With a blind spot common to all checks covering a share $\rho_{\rm cc}$ of hazards, catch rates are 1.000, 0.950 and 0.700 at $\rho_{\rm cc}=0$, 0.05 and 0.3 [SM(7)]. Against an attacker that strikes in the blind spot when it knows it and at random otherwise, with $\rho_{\rm cc}=0.05$, catch rates are 0.950, 0.475, 0.095 and 0 as the probability that it knows rises through 0, 0.5, 0.9 and 1 [AL(6.9)]. Common causes include shared training sources, shared models and shared funders; evaluators' preference for their own lineage's outputs (Panickssery et al. 2024); and collusion between untrusted monitors and the policy they monitor, which the control literature treats as a design variable (Greenblatt et al. 2024; Gardner-Challis et al. 2026).

### Remark {{#lyapunov}}: prediction horizons

**{{!Remark:lyapunov}} (Prediction horizons under chaos) [thm].** *In a system with largest Lyapunov exponent $\lambda_L>0$, an initial error $\epsilon_{\rm obs}$ reaches a tolerance $\Delta_{\rm tol}$ after about $\lambda_L^{-1}\ln(\Delta_{\rm tol}/\epsilon_{\rm obs})$, so a gain in precision by a factor $g_p$ extends the horizon only by $\lambda_L^{-1}\ln g_p$.* In the logistic map at full chaos, errors of $10^{-3}$ and $10^{-12}$ reach 0.1 after 7.4 and 37.3 steps on average [AL(6.10)]. This limits safety cases that predict trajectories, such as "the margin we bought will suffice", not certification of invariants, reachable sets or probabilistic bounds. That AI raises the sensitivity of the systems it acts in is a conjecture [conj].
