## S6. AI: instrument and incarnation

Numbers below cite the block of `checks_v62.py` that reproduces them: SM(5)–(6) and SM(11)–(13), DL, CI for the selection among developers, and AL, the alignment review's independent recomputation of Section 6 (`../v6.1/reviews/scripts-v6.1-alignment/al_checks.py`, standard library only), ported whole with its parts named as the reviewer named them.

### Proof of {{faithful}}

Changing $a_i$ from 0 to 1 changes $u_i$ by $\epsilon b-c-\epsilon h_d$, independent of the others' choices, and each other $u_j$ by $-\epsilon b/(n-1)-\epsilon h_d$. Summing, the relative terms cancel, which gives (1); in objectives racing changes $J_i$ by $\epsilon b(1-\frac{\theta}{n-1})-c-\epsilon h_d(1+\theta)$, which gives (2). For (3), write $\theta^\ast=(T-H-c)/(T/(n-1)+H)$ with $T=\epsilon b$, $H=\epsilon h_d$ growing with exponents $1+\beta_b$, $1+\beta_h$ and $c$ with $\beta_c$, and take limits. $\square$ *Numerics [num: SM(5), AL(6.1)]:* exponents $(\beta_b,\beta_h,\beta_c)=(0,0,0)$ and $(1,1,0)$ give the limit 1.50; $(0,0.5,0)$ gives $-0.99$; $(0.5,0,0)$ gives 2.998; $(1,0,3)$ and $(0,0,1.5)$ give $-\infty$; at equality, $(0,0,1)$ gives 0.9375 and $(1,0,2)$ gives 2.1. With $b,h_d,c$ fixed, $\theta^\ast$ is 1.44 at $\epsilon=10$; with $c$ also scaling with $\epsilon$ it stays 0.94. With $n=4$, $b=1$, $c=0.3$, $h_d=0.2$, $\epsilon=1$, all racing gives each $-1.1$; lowering $h_d$ to 0.1 and 0.05 cuts the loss to 0.70 and 0.50 while raising $\theta^\ast$ to 1.39 and 1.70: the double edge of Section 6.2, and the second fate of safety progress in Section 6.4. The condition earlier written as $\beta_c<\beta_b=\beta_d$ was neither necessary nor sufficient; the correct one is $\beta_c<1+\max(\beta_b,\beta_h)$. The harm coefficient was $d$ in V6.1 and is $h_d$ here, so that $d$ keeps its one meaning as the correction rate.

### {{delegate}} in full, and {{lockin}}

*Each actor chooses $\iota_i\in\{0,1\}$; strength is $1+s\iota_i$ and win probabilities are the contest success function with $r=1$; defeat costs $L_e$; a loss of control by any actor costs every actor $L_c$; the hazard of losing control is $\psi_0$ without delegation and $\psi_1>\psi_0$ with it, $\Delta\psi=\psi_1-\psi_0$; payoffs are $-L_e(1-w_i)-L_c[1-\prod_j(1-\psi_j)]$. (1) If $k$ others delegate, delegating gains $L_e[w_i(1,k)-w_i(0,k)]$, increasing in $s$ and tending to $L_e(1-1/n)$ for $k=0$ and $L_e/(k+1)$ for $k\ge1$, and costs $L_c\Delta\psi(1-\psi_0)^{n-1-k}(1-\psi_1)^k$. (2) Delegation becomes strictly dominant as $s$ grows iff the limiting gain exceeds the cost for every $k$; $L_e/n>L_c\Delta\psi$ suffices. (3) Universal delegation is worse for every actor than none, by $L_c[(1-\psi_0)^n-(1-\psi_1)^n]$. (4) Under relative objectives the shared loss cancels and delegation is chosen at every $s>0$.* *Proof:* direct computation; with all delegating, $w_i=1/n$ as with none and the hazard is higher. $\square$ *Numerics [num: AL(6.2)]:* with $n=3$, $L_e=L_c$, $\psi_0=0.01$, $\psi_1=0.2$, the dominance threshold is $s^\ast=1.1628$, where a lone delegator wins with probability 0.5196. A speed advantage of this size is far from decisive, which is the sense of "delegation pressure arrives well below decisive capability" in Section 6.3.

*{{lockin}}.* With $\dot{\mathcal M}=-\delta_h\mathcal M$ under delegation and $\dot{\mathcal M}=\varrho_h(\bar{\mathcal M}-\mathcal M)$ otherwise, the lock time is $T_L=\ln(\mathcal M_0/\mathcal M_{\min})/\delta_h$ and the recovery time from a level $\mathcal M<\mathcal M_{\min}$ is $T_R(\mathcal M)=\varrho_h^{-1}\ln[(\bar{\mathcal M}-\mathcal M)/(\bar{\mathcal M}-\mathcal M_{\min})]$. $\square$ *The "is" joint of Section 6.3.* Let delegation last a time $T_{\rm del}$ and let the contest allow an actor that reverts a window $T_W$ before the next consequential decision. The lock counts for that actor iff $T_{\rm del}>T_L$ and $T_W<T_R(\mathcal M(T_{\rm del}))$; so $q_L=\Pr(T_{\rm del}>T_L,\ T_W<T_R)$, a probability set by the contest's tempo and by $\delta_h$, not a free parameter. Where correction itself rests on $\mathcal M$, the same decay enters the rate law as $+w\delta_h$ ({{humancorr}}, S4).

### {{delegatelock}} in full, and proof

*Let locked delegation end the delegator's ability to overrule with probability $q_L$; let losing the contest mean subordination with probability $p$; let $Q=\prod_j(1-\psi_j)$; and, with $r=1$, $\Phi_i=(1-q_L\iota_i)Q[w_i+(1-w_i)(1-p)]$. (1) If $q_L=1$ and $Q>0$, not delegating gives strictly higher survival for every finite $s$, every $n$, every $p$ and every profile of the others. (2) With $k$ others delegating and $p=1$, write $a=(1-\psi_1)/(1-\psi_0)$ and $A=(1-q_L)a$; then $\Phi_D/\Phi_N=A(1+s)\frac{n+ks}{n+(k+1)s}$, which rises with $k$; all delegating is an equilibrium iff $A(n+(n-1)s)/n\ge1$ and no one delegating iff $An(1+s)/(n+s)\le1$. At least one always holds, since $A(n+(n-1)s)/n-An(1+s)/(n+s)=A(n-1)s^2/[n(n+s)]\ge0$, and both can. (3) With $p<1$, $\Phi_D/\Phi_N=A\,[1-p+p\,w_D(k)]/[1-p+p\,w_N(k)]$ with $w_D=(1+s)/(n+(k+1)s)$ and $w_N=1/(n+ks)$; both win probabilities fall in $k$ and the ratio can cross one from above, which excludes both corners and leaves an interior number of delegators as the only pure equilibrium. (4) At bounded speed one delegator does not drive another's survival to zero: a non-delegator facing $k$ delegators has $\Phi_N\ge Q_N/(n+ks)>0$.* *Proof.* (1) $\Phi_D=0$ while $\Phi_N\ge Q_Nw_N>0$. (2) By the strengths $1$, $1+s$ and the formula; the identity gives the "at least one". (3) By the formula; with $p=1$ the ratio is (2)'s and rises in $k$ (delegations are complements), with $p<1$ the subordination term $1-p$ dilutes the win probabilities and the ratio can fall in $k$ (substitutes). (4) Immediate. $\square$ *Numerics:* part (1) holds in 20,000 of 20,000 random settings [num: DL]; at $p=1$, over 100,000 draws, "neither" never occurs and "both" occurs 4,068 times [num: SM(6)], and the alignment review's independent draw from a different sampling distribution gives 21,922 "both" and again no "neither", with the closed forms equal to the direct survival comparison in every draw [num: AL(6.4)]. With $n=2$, $q_L=0.99$, $\psi_0=0.01$, $\psi_1=0.05$, all delegating is an equilibrium only for $s\ge206.4$, with survival 0.0045 there [num: AL(6.4)]. The outside reviewer's case for (3), $n=3$, $s=1$, $q_L=0$, $\psi_0=0$, $\psi_1=0.015$: at $p=0.1$ the ratios $\Phi_D/\Phi_N$ are 1.00259, 1.00097 and 0.99928 for $k=0,1,2$ others delegating, so exactly two delegators is the only pure equilibrium; at $p=1$ they are 1.48, 1.58 and 1.64 and all delegating is the equilibrium. Over 20,000 random draws with $q_L<1$, "neither corner is an equilibrium" occurs 0 times at $p=1$, 1,586 times at $p=0.5$ and 60 times at $p=0.1$ [num: DL]. V6.1's core statement dropped $p=1$ from part (2); it is restored.

### Proofs of {{retain}}, {{transmit}} and {{nested}}

*{{retain}}.* Variants acting on common terms exist and have relative performance lower by at least a positive margin, so under cumulative retention their share tends to zero, as in {{load}}; the retained systems' decisions are the actor's. If constraints are an enforced admission condition, no admissible variant violates them. That an actor which cannot overrule decisions taken in its name has lost O2 in that domain is a definition. For a reader who counts survival only as preservation of the unit, delegation acts through the shared hazard instead: delegated systems interacting at machine speed without a human veto raise the probability of shared loss by reciprocal fear of surprise attack (Schelling 1960), inadvertent escalation (Posen 1991) and flash war (Scharre 2018), an argument with cited mechanisms, not a derivation. $\square$

*{{transmit}}.* By {{standprice}} the working objective of an operator in a compounding contest is weighted toward standing; a faithful system maximizes that objective; retention by contest performance selects among systems on the same relative payoff. So the system's effectiveness multiplies the weight already placed on standing, as $\epsilon$ does in {{faithful}}. $\square$

*{{nested}}.* (1) Level 2 is relative and payoff-monotone, so {{blind}}(1) removes every term common to all developers. (2) Level 0 maximizes its reward by definition; level 1 chooses rewards and constraints to maximize the developer's objective, which counts privately visible divergences through customers, liability and reputation. Under $(A_\tau)$ countering lowers the developer's payoff by a positive margin relative to its matched variant, net of every private benefit; the matched variant is in the support of selection; so with cumulative payoff-monotone selection the share of countering developers relative to their matched variants tends to zero ({{load}}(2), developers as carriers). A developer whose payoff exceeds its matched variant's by an independent advantage larger than the tax keeps growing relative to it: the lead pays the tax, and does so only while the lead lasts ({{lead}}). (3) A constraint borne by one developer lowers its relative payoff. One enforced on all as a condition of admission is, among compliers, a common term when its incidence is equal ({{blind}}(2)), so it leaves their relative shares unchanged; with unequal incidence it is a payoff difference among compliers and re-sorts them. Within a type that can evade, the evading variant is selected out iff its expected sanction $s_k$ is at least its compliance cost $c_k$ ({{blind}}(3)); so the condition for every type is sufficient for keeping the divergence out, and within a type necessary. $\square$ *Numerics [num: CI]:* a unilateral safeguard that cuts error from 0.20 to 0.01 at cost 0.01 is selected for, its share tending to one, which is the case $(A_\tau)$ excludes; a safeguard whose benefit is common is selected away. A safeguarding developer with baseline 2 and tax 0.1 against an unsafe developer with baseline 1 takes the whole market, so a positive tax alone does not eliminate safeguarding; add its matched unsafe variant, baseline 2 without the tax, and the safeguarding share is $2\times10^{-9}$ by $t=200$. A tax of 0.01 paid out of an independent advantage of 0.02 survives, with the safeguarding share tending to one; out of an advantage of 0.005 it is eliminated. With zero tax the shares are unchanged: the safeguard neither spreads nor is removed. Two developer types with compliance costs 0.1 and 0.2 under an expected sanction of 1 for evasion: the compliant share is $0.999\,999\,975$ at $t=20$ and the compliers' shares are 0.88 and 0.12, so compliance survives and incidence re-sorts the compliers; with equal costs the compliers' ratio is preserved exactly. Across types the condition is sufficient, not necessary: with sanctions 1 and 0.15 against costs 0.1 and 0.2, the second type's evaders earn less than the first type's compliers and vanish with the rest, while with a sanction of 0.05 they take over; the outside reviewer's script and both memos' scripts agree, and the alignment review's own replicator reproduces the private-benefit, common-benefit and lead cases [num: CI, AL(6.7)].

*Interface.* What level 2 selects on is the developers' relative payoff, of which the trained systems' contribution to relative standing is part. Under a public mandate, or a national laboratory that only moves relative selection to the level of states, the result applies at that level or not at all (S13).

### Proof of {{masked}}, and the direction of the bound

(1) The expected sanction attached by admission is $s_k=p_{\rm det}F$. For a masked divergence, $p_{\rm det}$ is the probability that a protocol of fixed access detects behaviour that differs under evaluation or appears only in some contexts; it is driven toward zero by training against the evaluation, by context, or by the system's own recognition of the evaluation, each shown in constructed settings (EV6). Then $s_k<c_k$ for every finite $F$ once $p_{\rm det}<c_k/F$, and {{blind}}(3) says compliance does not survive cumulative selection: the constraint is enforced in form and evaded in substance. An evaluation of propensity under these conditions can show a tendency present; it cannot show one absent, since absence under evaluation is what the divergence produces. (2) A capability evaluation gives a lower bound, a capability shown; a threshold rule "admit while shown capability is below $\bar\kappa$" would be fooled by sandbagging, so the bound on capability has to come from channels the behaviour does not control, compute, training data and elicitation by fine-tuning ({{alarm}}, S4), none of which falls with the system's conduct under evaluation. The expected sanction attached to such a condition is then $F$ times a detection probability the system does not set, so (1) does not apply to it. Given a capability bound $\bar\kappa$ so obtained, the hazard the system can realize is bounded by a control protocol that is safe against a system of capability up to $\bar\kappa$ that may be adversarial (Greenblatt, Shlegeris, Sachan and Roger 2024); white-box evidence, weights and activations, is a second channel the behaviour under evaluation does not control, which an audit can read (Marks et al. 2025). (3) Beyond $\bar\kappa$ no protocol is certified safe, so the uncovered share of the class is not lowered by admission; on a continuation branch (coverage lags) holds for that class and {{coverage}} applies. E3 bounds the pace of the class to what control covers and E2 does not build it. $\square$ Whether independent auditors' detection rates at fixed access hold up as capability grows is the measurement of crux C12.

### Two numerical illustrations for Section 6.4

*Pairwise comparison inside training [num: AL(BT)].* In a Bradley–Terry reward, $\Pr(y_1\succ y_2)=\sigma(R(y_1)-R(y_2))$. Adding a harm $-5$ borne by third parties to both responses leaves the preference unchanged at 0.7110; adding "do nothing" to the comparison set, with no benefit and no harm, the complying response is preferred with probability 0.024. A learned reward is identified up to an additive constant per context, so a disposition shared by every compared response is invisible unless restraint is among the options compared.

*A bounded objective is not enough [num: AL(bounded), AL(contest)].* A maximizer of a bounded goal with success probability $p(R)=1-e^{-R}$ in the resources $R$ it acquires, at unit resource cost $c$, acquires $R^\ast=\ln(1/c)$: 2.30, 4.61, 6.91 and 13.8 as $c$ falls from $0.1$ through $10^{-2}$ and $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-6}$, without limit; a satisficer that stops at $p\ge0.95$ acquires 3.0 at every cost. In the contest of Section 3.4 with $n=4$ and shared loss $\ell=0.3$, as the private cost falls from 1 to 0.001 the equilibrium intensity rises from 0.25 to 250 under the relative objective $\theta=-1$, and from 0.144 to 0.623 under $\theta=0$, where the actor's own share of the shared loss bounds it; any $\theta>-1$ keeps it bounded. Counting side effects corresponds to $\theta>-1$; mild optimization to satiation ({{enough}}).

### Proofs for Section 6.5

*{{opaquecorr}}.* The residual hazard in period $t$ is $h_0(1-c_{b,t})+h_{c,t}$; compare with $h_0$ for the first claim. Survival to $N$ is $\prod_{t\le N}(1-h_0(1-c_{b,t})-h_{c,t})$, which stays bounded away from zero iff the residuals are summable; with $c_{b,t}=c_b<1$ fixed the residual is at least $h_0(1-c_b)>0$ each period and is not summable, and with $c_{b,t}\to1$ fast enough it is. $\square$ *Example [num: SM(11), AL(6.8)]:* at $h_0=0.1$ a corrector with $c_{b,t}=1-2^{-(t+1)}$ and $h_{c,t}=2^{-(t+2)}$ gives survival 0.510 at period 200 and still 0.510 at period 20,000, against $7\times10^{-10}$ without the corrector at 200. V6.1's example, $c_b=0.99$ fixed with $h_{c,t}=2^{-(t+2)}$, is not summable: survival to period 200 is 0.473 counting from $t=0$ (0.630 counting from $t=1$, the figure quoted in V6.1), and of order $10^{-9}$ by period 20,000. The counterexample row of S14 cites the new example.

*{{topology}}.* Each layer covers $\varkappa_\ell$ times the attention of the layer above, so $k=\ln(Y/\mathsf B)/\ln\varkappa_\ell$ layers are needed to span a gap $Y/\mathsf B$; a serial chain catches a problem only if every link succeeds, $p_\ell^k=(Y/\mathsf B)^{\ln p_\ell/\ln\varkappa_\ell}$. Independent checks across the gap fail together with probability $(1-p_\ell^k)^k$; a common cause defeating all with probability $p_{\rm cc}$ leaves at most $1-p_{\rm cc}$. Layered redundancy needs every layer to succeed. A blind spot common to every check and covering a share $\rho_{\rm cc}$ of hazards is missed whatever the topology, so for hazards that strike at random the catch rate is at most $1-\rho_{\rm cc}$, and an adversary that knows the blind spot and places every attempt in it is never caught. $\square$ *Numerics [num: SM(13), AL(6.9)]:* with $p_\ell=0.9$, $k=6$: the serial chain catches 0.53; six checks across the gap 0.989, or 0.940 under a common cause with $p_{\rm cc}=0.05$; layered redundancy 0.941 with two checks per layer and 0.994 with three. With a common blind spot, catch rates for random hazards are 1.000, 0.950 and 0.700 at $\rho_{\rm cc}=0$, 0.05, 0.3; at $\rho_{\rm cc}=0.05$ an adversary that knows the blind spot with probability 0, 0.5, 0.9 or 1 is caught with probability 0.950, 0.475, 0.095 or 0. *Budgets:* six checks across six layers are 36 layer-checks; two or three per layer are 12 or 18, so per unit of budget layered redundancy is the better design. Common causes include shared training sources, shared models, shared funders and collusion among untrusted monitors, which the control literature treats as a design variable (Greenblatt, Shlegeris, Sachan and Roger 2024); checks of the checked system's own lineage add self-preference (Panickssery et al. 2024) and collusion through self-recognition or coordination signals (Gardner-Challis et al. 2026). Beside the obfuscated-arguments failure of debate (Barnes and Christiano 2020) stands a protocol designed against it, prover–estimator debate (Brown-Cohen et al. 2025); whether it scales is part of crux C3a.

**{{!Remark:lyapunov}} (Chaos and the horizon of trajectory prediction).** In a system with largest Lyapunov exponent $\lambda_L>0$, an initial error $\epsilon_{\rm obs}$ reaches a tolerance $\Delta_{\rm tol}$ after about $\lambda_L^{-1}\ln(\Delta_{\rm tol}/\epsilon_{\rm obs})$, so a $k$-fold gain in precision extends the horizon by $\lambda_L^{-1}\ln k$; in the logistic map at full chaos, errors of $10^{-3}$ and $10^{-12}$ reach 0.1 after 7.39 and 37.31 steps on average [num: AL(6.10)]. This limits safety cases that predict trajectories, such as "the margin we bought will suffice", not certification of invariants, reachable sets or probabilistic bounds (Dalrymple et al. 2024). That AI raises the sensitivity of the systems it acts in is a conjecture.

**{{!Proposition:homeostasis}} (Risk homeostasis under delegation) [thm].** *In the delegation model with additive stakes and $r=1$, let each actor choose $\iota_i\in[0,1]$, with strength $1+s\iota_i$ and hazard $\psi_0+\Delta\psi\,\iota_i$; write $x=\Delta\psi\,\iota$, $b_s=\Delta\psi/s$, $K_h=(n-1)L_e/(n^2L_c)$. An interior symmetric equilibrium solves $(x+b_s)(1-\psi_0-x)^{n-1}=K_h$. (1) Where $x\ll1-\psi_0$, the added hazard is $nx\approx(n-1)L_e/(nL_c(1-\psi_0)^{n-1})-n\Delta\psi/s$, nearly independent of $\Delta\psi$, until delegation saturates. (2) There can be several interior equilibria, and along a branch $\partial x/\partial\Delta\psi=-(1-\psi_0-x)/(s[1-\psi_0-nx-(n-1)b_s])$, whose sign changes between branches. (3) After saturation, realized risk falls with $\Delta\psi$ and can be summable under full delegation and growing speed: with $n=3$, $L_e=L_c=1$, $\psi_0=0$, $s_t=2^t$ and $\Delta\psi_t=2^{-t}$, full delegation is an equilibrium at every $t$, the realized hazard at $t=0$ is 1, and the realized risk summed from $t=5$ is at most $3/16$. (4) With all-or-nothing delegation, any equilibrium with $k\ge1$ delegators has realized hazard above the baseline, so an improvement that carries $\Delta\psi$ across the threshold at which delegation becomes an equilibrium raises realized hazard.* *Proof.* At the symmetric point $\partial w_i/\partial\iota_i=s(n-1)/(n^2(1+s\iota))$ and the marginal cost is $L_c\Delta\psi(1-\psi)^{n-1}$; equate and divide by $s$. (1) approximates; (2) differentiates implicitly; (3) the own marginal gain at full delegation, $2s_t/(9(1+s_t))-\Delta\psi_t(1-\Delta\psi_t)^2$, is $+0.111$, $+0.023$, $+0.037$, $+0.102$ and $+0.154$ for $t=0$ to 4 and rises thereafter, and the objective is concave in own delegation, so full delegation is a best response at every $t$; the realized hazard $1-(1-2^{-t})^3$ is 1 at $t=0$ and sums over $t\ge5$ to $0.1836\le\sum_{t\ge5}3\cdot2^{-t}=3/16$; (4) $(1-\psi_0-\Delta\psi)^k<(1-\psi_0)^k$. $\square$ *Numerics [num: SM(12), AL(6.11)]:* with $n=3$, $s=100$, $L_e=1$, $L_c=100$, $\psi_0=0.01$, the added hazard is 0.0011, 0.0053, 0.0062, 0.0065 and 0.0067 as $\Delta\psi$ falls from 0.19 to 0.005, and falls to 0.0030 only at complete delegation (compare Stafford, Trager and Dafoe 2022; Peltzman 1975; Wilde 1982). Two interior roots, at $\iota=0.153$ and $0.937$ for $n=2$, $s=100$, $L_e=0.5$, $L_c=1$, $\psi_0=0.01$, $\Delta\psi=0.9$, with the corner also an equilibrium, illustrate (2) [num: `../v5/checks_v5.py`, HO; not yet ported to a V6.2 block]. V6.1 stated (3) as "an equilibrium for $t\ge5$"; the equilibrium holds at every $t$, and $t\ge5$ is where the tail bound begins.
