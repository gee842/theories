### Narrow E1 and the value of commitment

Deterrence theory holds that removing human discretion from retaliation makes a threat credible: a commitment that cannot be revoked is worth more than one that can (Schelling 1960). Its stronger form is that semi-automated retaliation could relieve the pressure to launch on warning. Five answers follow from the core.
1. The commitment value comes from removing the possibility of human overrule, which is the lock of {{lockin}} and, in its domain, a loss of O2.
2. Survivable, human-controlled forces relieve the pressure to launch on warning without removing judgment.
3. An automated commitment cannot tell a false alarm from an attack. Several false alarms of early-warning systems are documented, and in 1983 an officer judged an alarm false and did not report an attack (Podvig 2002; Hoffman 2009); the register records the event and does not claim that a report would have launched retaliation (EV18).
4. Automation adds a common risk, escalation at machine speed on a false alarm, that relative selection does not see ({{blind}}), so the contest under-prices it.
5. Among actors who price in the lock, keeping AI out of these loops is a strict equilibrium, and doing so verifiably is a costly signal ({{signal}}).

The strict equilibrium of {{delegatelock}}(1) needs a certain lock, and it survives while the lock fails with a probability below $(1-\psi_0)/\big((1-\psi_1)(1+s)\big)$ ({{nearlock}}). The narrow domain meets that bound by construction: a decision irreversible within the time for deliberation cannot be overruled in time once it is delegated. In tactical domains, where the speed advantage is large and the lock uncertain, delegation can be an equilibrium ({{delegatelock}}(2), (3)), and bounded, reversible defensive automation falls under the test of O2 of Section 6.6.

### Feasibility

Each actor can take part of E1 alone, and taken verifiably it is the signal of {{signal}}. E2 and E3 are the most verifiable among rivals, because chips, fabs, datacentres and compute can be observed. Algorithmic progress erodes thresholds stated in compute: with efficiency doubling about every eight months (90% interval about two to twenty-two months, for pre-training on data to 2023), a nominal cap must tighten roughly two and a half to three times a year at the central estimate, and anywhere from about 1.5 to about 64 times across the interval (EV9), a rate condition verification must meet. Hardware-enabled mechanisms for verifying compute use are proposed but not deployed (EV17). E1 is the hardest to verify, since it concerns software and internal procedure, and the most self-enforcing among actors who price in the lock, which is why it can begin as a norm: declared doctrine with inspected exercises.

Staying out of the contest alone while others race can be worse than joining. In the survival accounting of {{survassure}}, take two actors, no hazard but the loss of the ability to overrule and the rival's decisive advantage, with $q_{\rm I}=p_{\rm dec}=1-\varepsilon$, and a decisive winner that extends its dominance, $p_{\rm ext}=1$. Joining gives $\Phi^P=\varepsilon\big(1-(1-\varepsilon)/2\big)=\varepsilon(1+\varepsilon)/2$. Staying out gives $\Phi^S=1-p^S_{\rm dec}$: that is $\varepsilon$, better than joining, if staying out leaves the rival's decisive chance where it was, and $\varepsilon^2$, worse than joining, if it raises that chance to $1-\varepsilon^2$ [num: TR; legacy v4.5, P24]. That is why E1 must be held in common and begins with the decision loops in which the lock makes it an equilibrium.

### Precedents, corrected against the sources

The last column names the condition under which each precedent worked or failed that the AI case does not share, since those conditions are where the analogy is weakest. Like every reading here, they need review by historians outside the drafting family.

| Precedent | What made it work or fail | What it shows | Where the analogy to AI is weakest |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Acheson–Lilienthal report (1946) | International ownership of the dangerous activity was proposed | Custody of the dangerous activity as the object of agreement | It was proposed while one holder had a monopoly; frontier capability in AI is held by several, with short leads (EV5) |
| The Baruch plan (1946) | It added that sanctions on violators would not be subject to veto; the other side opposed this and on-site inspection, and the leader would keep its monopoly until control was in place (Bundy 1988) | Custody must precede decisiveness, and enforcement against violators is part of the bargain | A sequence in which the leader keeps its capability until control is in place is the sequence the others refuse |
| The first nuclear monopoly and the period of superiority (1945–54) | Preventive war was argued, above all in the period of superiority after the monopoly ended, and not carried out; the advantage was small in stockpile and balanced by conventional forces (Trachtenberg 1988/89; Rosenberg 1983 for the early stockpile) | Restraint where the advantage was not decisive | It shows restraint below decisiveness only; whether advantage in AI becomes decisive is the crux (C5) |
| The ABM Treaty (1972) | Reciprocal limits on defences removed one driver of the offensive race; warheads still multiplied through multiple warheads, which were not limited; the treaty was abandoned in 2002, for reasons that are a matter of interpretation | Reciprocal constraint can change the value of extra advantage; if the withdrawal reflected a renewed value of winning, durability fails when that value rises again | It limited a single, countable class of systems; capability in AI is general-purpose, and the verification of its compute is not deployed (EV17) |
| Test bans | The partial ban forbade atmospheric, space and underwater tests while underground testing continued | Not a stop of the decisive class | Tests are observable events; training is observed through compute, whose thresholds erode (EV9) |
| The Outer Space and Seabed treaties | Weapons of mass destruction were excluded from orbit and the seabed; military use of space continued | A class of weapons kept out of a domain, close to E1 | The weapons were kept out before anyone had deployed them there; AI is already in use across the domains at stake |
| The Biological Weapons Convention (1972) | A weapon hazardous in development and dangerous to its holder; one party renounced it unilaterally on grounds of uncontrollability and low military value, while another kept a large covert programme under the convention (Leitenberg and Zilinskas 2012) | A weapon that endangers its holder can be renounced unilaterally; without verification the hard case persists | The renouncer's military value was low and it held another deterrent; on the racers' premise the value of AI is high and it has no substitute |
| Non-proliferation and counter-proliferation | Great powers cooperated to limit others (Coe and Vaynman 2015); strikes on others' programmes were considered (Burr and Richelson 2000/01), and several were carried out, mostly not by the cooperating powers (Fuhrmann and Kreps 2010) | Partial coalitions enforcing a stop on outsiders; the hard case answered by coercion | The cooperating powers were not each other's targets; in AI the leaders are the rivals, and a coalition that stops outsiders need not end their O2 (X8) |
| The stability–instability paradox | Stability among decisive weapons can intensify competition below them (Snyder 1965) | A stop of the decisive class can push competition into cyber operations and influence, the domain of broad E1 | The analogy holds directly, which is why a stop of the decisive class is not the whole of E1 |
| Nuclear power after a serious accident (1979 onward) | An industry institution of mutual inspection and ranking produced durable improvements with profit motives unchanged, because one more accident would harm every operator (Rees 1994); later lapses show the durability is not automatic | Durable restraint with unchanged motives where the shared loss returns and no prize is decisive | No operator could win the industry through the hazard; in AI the prize may be decisive |
| The Montreal Protocol (1987) and its 1990 amendment | Phase-out with trade measures against non-parties, and a fund for developing countries created by the amendment; agreement became possible as substitutes became available (Barrett 2003) | Coverage made attractive by provision and enforced by conditions of access; a low value of winning | Substitutes made restraint cheap; capability in AI has no substitute, and provision beneath the game is the nearest analogue |

The Cold War ended largely by the internal collapse of one side, accompanied by negotiated reductions in intermediate forces, conventional forces and strategic arms, and its hazards fell with learning (Nye 1987), the case in which residual risk becomes summable. Rivals survived where nature or balancing supplied saturation, and gave up capabilities by agreement when reciprocal limits made the extra advantage worth less. Whether advantage in AI saturates is not known (Section 7.3), so Section 11.5 states the consequence conditionally.

### Crisis windows and standing

Arrangements of this kind are often adopted when a crisis opens a window, not through negotiation among the strong at a time of their choosing (Gunderson and Holling 2002; Olsson et al. 2006). A design prepared in advance, with its functions and protections worked out and its activation committed, is what a crisis can adopt; unprepared responses tend to strengthen the contest, through emergency powers without sunsets and measures aimed at rivals. Smaller jurisdictions, users and people who depend on services controlled by others need standing on every track, because they cannot leave the shared exposure; the chamber of X10 and the service guarantees are how they get it.
