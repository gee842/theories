## S10. The generative alternative

This chapter states in full the results that Section 10 carries in a sentence each, {{commonfront}}, {{twoorders}}, {{rivalverify}} and {{participate}}; gives {{commonfrontn}} in full with its variations; proves {{share}} under both sharing conventions, with the numerics; and proves {{safeguards}} and computes its margin on both hosts, the figures the core's Scope paragraphs point to.

### A common frontier

**{{!Proposition:commonfront}} (A common value: too little speed, not too much) [thm].** *If the value of capability is common, $\pi_i=\Omega(\mathcal E)/n-ce_i-\ell\mathcal E$ with $\Omega$ increasing and strictly concave in total intensity $\mathcal E$, private incentives give $\Omega'(\mathcal E)=n(c+\ell)$ against the joint optimum's $\Omega'(\mathcal E)=c+n\ell$, so total intensity is at or below the joint optimum: the residual failure is too little speed, not too much, and the joint optimum must also respect the host's floor ({{viab}}).*

*Proof.* Each actor's first-order condition is $\Omega'(\mathcal E)/n=c+\ell$; the sum of payoffs, $\Omega(\mathcal E)-c\mathcal E-n\ell\mathcal E$, has the first-order condition $\Omega'(\mathcal E)=c+n\ell$. Since $n(c+\ell)-(c+n\ell)=(n-1)c\ge0$, with equality iff $n=1$ or $c=0$, and $\Omega'$ is decreasing, private total intensity is at most the joint one (interior solutions). $\square$

**Proposition {{#commonfrontn}} in full.** *Of $n$ actors, cooperators hold effort $e_c$ and racers choose theirs; whoever wins the decisive contest gains $\Omega$, and every loser bears $L_b$. Under separate frontiers every effort enters the contest separately; under a common frontier the $n_c$ cooperators pool effort into one entity of strength $(n_ce_c)^r$, which yields a common value $\Omega_c$ split equally and confers no dominance among them. With $n=4$, $\Omega=\Omega_c=L_b=c=1$, $\ell=0.3$, $e_c=0.05$, $r=1.3$: (1) under separate frontiers the unique pure equilibrium is all racing; (2) under a common frontier all cooperating ($R=0.140>T=0.002$) and all racing are both strict equilibria, cooperating being a best response iff trust in each rival exceeds about 0.61, or 0.82 if a deviator best-responds to fixed intensities; (3) treating the others as a coalition maximizing their summed payoff, the coalition's cooperation is dominant; (4) a smaller pooled effort ($e_c=0.03$) leaves only all racing, and with $n=2$ all cooperating is an equilibrium for $r$ up to about 1.2; (5) holding $\Omega_c$ and $L_b$ fixed, the pooled effort needed to keep $T\le R$ is 0.0054, 0.035, 0.141 and 0.49 for $\Omega=0.5,1,2,4$; scaling $\Omega_c$ and $L_b$ with $\Omega$, it is 0.0175, 0.035, 0.070 and 0.140.* Racers' intensities are the symmetric fixed point of best responses, each verified as a global best response; cases without a symmetric pure equilibrium are excluded (`../v4/checks_v45.py`, N22, run in the legacy suite of `checks_v62.py`) [num: legacy v4.5, N22]. Variations in the same block keep both strict equilibria with $n=3$, with $L_b=0.5$, with $\ell=0$, with $\Omega_c=2$ and with $n=6$ at $r\in\{0.8,1\}$, the aggregate trust threshold ranging from 0.14 to 0.90; at $n=6$, $r=1.3$ some racer subgames have no symmetric pure equilibrium, and results there are not equilibria.

A common frontier turns a dilemma into a coordination problem while the value of winning is moderate; when it is large, keeping ahead of defectors is the race moved up a level. *Within the pool.* The model fixes $e_c$. Each cooperator's effort raises the pool's strength, and with it the chance that the pool and not a racer wins; the gain, $\Omega_c$ split equally and the loss $L_b$ avoided, goes to every cooperator alike, while the cost falls on the contributor. Contributions chosen one by one are therefore a good common to the cooperators, which selection among them does not favour ({{onegroup}}), and parts (4) and (5) put a floor under the pooled effort that rises steeply with $\Omega$: a pool whose contributions drift below the floor loses its cooperative equilibrium. That is a free-rider result, and it is why Discussion X10 collects frontier contributions by formula from the same base as the levy rather than by subscription. Their size in an equilibrium with chosen contributions is not computed here.

### Proof of Proposition {{#share}}

(1) Pooled payoffs $\pi'_j=(1-\beta)\pi_j+\beta\bar\pi$ give $J_i=\big[(1-\beta)+\beta(1+\theta_0)/n\big]\pi_i+\big[\theta_0(1-\beta)+\beta(1+\theta_0)(n-1)/n\big]\bar\pi_{-i}$; dividing by the first coefficient gives $\theta_{\rm eff}(\beta)$, strictly increasing in $\beta$ for $-1<\theta_0<n-1$, with value $n-1$ at $\beta=1$. At $\theta_0=-1$, $J_i=(1-\beta)(\pi_i-\bar\pi_{-i})$, and under relative selection $\pi'_k-\pi'_l=(1-\beta)(\pi_k-\pi_l)$, so no choice and no ordering of carriers changes.

(2) Write $A_w=(1-\beta\varrho)\Omega_e+\Omega_s$ for what the winner keeps. *Shared among all $n$:* every actor receives $\varrho\beta\Omega_e/n$ whoever wins, so $\pi_i=w_iA_w+\varrho\beta\Omega_e/n-ce_i-\ell\mathcal E$; the shared term is common to all and drops out of every objective $J_i=\pi_i+\theta\bar\pi_{-i}$ and of relative selection, leaving the contest with prize $\Omega_{\rm eff}=A_w$. *Shared among the $n-1$ others:* actor $j$ receives $\varrho\beta\Omega_e/(n-1)$ whenever another wins, so $\pi_j=w_jA_w+\varrho\beta\Omega_e(1-w_j)/(n-1)-ce_j-\ell\mathcal E=w_j\Omega_{\rm eff}+\varrho\beta\Omega_e/(n-1)-ce_j-\ell\mathcal E$ with $\Omega_{\rm eff}=A_w-\varrho\beta\Omega_e/(n-1)=\big(1-\beta\varrho\,n/(n-1)\big)\Omega_e+\Omega_s$; since $\sum_{j\ne i}w_j=1-w_i$, $\bar\pi_{-i}=-w_i\Omega_{\rm eff}/(n-1)$ plus terms independent of $w_i$, and $J_i=w_i\Omega_{\rm eff}(n-1-\theta)/(n-1)-ce_i-(1+\theta)\ell\mathcal E$ plus a constant: again the contest with prize $\Omega_{\rm eff}$. This is the convention under which {{twochan}}(4) and {{wingives}} count what a winner gives. The symmetric equilibrium $e(\theta)$ of {{boundary}}(1), the dissipation of {{dissipate}} and the hazard of {{racehazard}} are each proportional to the prize. Under relative selection a mutant at $y$ among residents at $x$ has relative payoff $\Omega_{\rm eff}(y^r-x^r)/(y^r+(n-1)x^r)-c(y-x)$ under either convention, with zero slope at $x^\ast=r\Omega_{\rm eff}/(nc)$, concave for $r=1$, and no profitable mutant on a grid for $r<1$ [num: SH].

*Where the uninvadable intensity is positive.* Under the first convention $\Omega_{\rm eff}>0$ for every $\beta\varrho<1$, and with $\Omega_s>0$, $\Omega_{\rm eff}\ge\Omega_s>0$ for every $\beta\le1$, so the uninvadable intensity is at least $r\Omega_s/(nc)$ however much of the divisible prize is shared. Under the second, $\Omega_{\rm eff}\ge\Omega_s$ while $\beta\varrho\le(n-1)/n$; beyond it a winner that has given the $n-1$ others more than $(n-1)/n$ of the divisible prize gains less from it than a loser, and $\Omega_{\rm eff}$ stays positive for every $\beta$ iff $\Omega_s>\Omega_e/(n-1)$, the value at which the positional prize outweighs the divisible loss of a winner who has given everything away. V6.1 stated "positive for every $\beta$ when $\Omega_s>0$" without the convention; it holds under the first.

(3) is {{compound}}(1) with {{chansign}}(4): with $k_0=0$, $S^\ast=\kappa_c\Omega/d_K$, and the condition of {{compound}}(1) becomes $(1-\beta)r-1<\delta_f/d_K$, in which $\kappa_c$ does not appear, with or without diffusion. With $\chi=0$ the reflexive channels of {{loopgain}} are removed; then the return to influence is zero ({{influence}}) and $d^\ast=A/c_{\rm ev}$ ({{corrrate}}(1)). $\square$

*Numerics* [num: SH]. With $n=4$, $r=1$, $c=1$, $\Omega_e=1$, the uninvadable intensity is 0.25 without a positional part and 0.125 with half the divisible prize shared among all; with $\Omega_s=1$, sharing all of $\Omega_e$ only halves it, from 0.5 to 0.25. In a common field of intensities $(0.2,0.05,0.1,0.1)$ the higher-intensity carrier out-earns the lower by 0.017 with half shared, and the difference turns negative only beyond 55%: sharing lowers the advantage of intensity at a given profile without moving the uninvadable intensity to zero. With $\Omega_e=1$ and $\Omega_s=0.5$, the uninvadable intensity is 0.300 (shared among all) and 0.275 (among the others) at $\beta=0.3$, and 0.225 and 0.175 at $\beta=0.6$, equal to $r\Omega_{\rm eff}/(nc)$ in each case with no mutant gaining on a grid. With $\Omega_s=0$, actors that value their absolute payoff and choose intensities as best responses follow the same two prizes: their Nash intensities, $r\Omega_{\rm eff}(n-1)/(n^2c)$, are 0.13125 and 0.11250 at $\beta=0.3$, and 0.07500 and 0.03750 at $\beta=0.6$. Earlier versions said that only changing what winning converts into reverses the direction of selection; in this model none of the three levers makes the selected intensity zero while a positional prize remains.

### Concentration at two orders

**{{!Remark:twoorders}} (Concentration at two orders).** At the first order, more contenders make the race fiercer ({{join}}(4)), and in a model of developers racing under a shared existential hazard, fragmentation raises speed and conditional risk, while a developer able to commit first to a slightly slower speed gains because its rivals slow too (Bueno de Mesquita, Dziuda and Polborn 2026; EV11). At the second order, cultivated influence over correctors rises with a high moment of shares, $\sum_kp_k^{1+(2-\xi_v)/\eta}$ ({{corrrate}}(4)), which calls for removing common control: merging the frontier into one carrier maximizes the return to capturing its correctors, and the gain of the reflexive channel is higher at the present concentration of standing than at parity (S2). A merged frontier also raises connectivity and with it common-mode failure, so it keeps modules of independent lineage inside it ({{topology}}), and its correction outside it. The design that serves both orders therefore merges the frontier and disperses and insulates correction, keeping several lineages inside the merged frontier; neither "centralize" nor "decentralize" is the answer, because the two orders call for opposite forms (Section 10.5, the frontier row).

### Proof of Theorem {{#safeguards}}, and the margin on both hosts

(1) (I2) with a margin in rule-making gives a full inbound row sum below one, so $x_{\rm rule}\to0$ ({{certificate}}(2)); once (I1) is enacted, $\tau_{\rm eff}=\tau(1-x_{\rm rule})$ exceeds $\nu$ after a finite time, load has a negative private payoff, the load falls to $e_{\min}$, and $\liminf V\ge1-h_Ve_{\min}/\rho>V_{\min}$ on the host {{eq:host}}, the last inequality being the premise $h_Ve_{\min}<\rho(1-V_{\min})$, which V6.1 left implicit in "a carrier of low enough load". For unanticipated harms, (I2) in evaluation gives $x_{\rm ev}\to0$, so masking falls below its threshold, or the conservative rule of {{alarm}} applies, and the alarm fires above the floor.

*The margin.* Fix the state, above the floor, at which the margin $\Delta=\tau_{\rm eff}-\nu>0$ takes force, and measure time from it. Relative fitness is then $-\Delta(e-E)$, so at selection speed $s$ the distribution of load at $t$ is the distribution at that state tilted by $e^{-s\Delta et}$ and normalized; its mean falls as the tilt $s\Delta t$ rises, its derivative in the tilt being minus the variance, so at every $t>0$ the load $E(t)$ is lower the larger $\Delta$. With two loads the race share solves $\dot x=-s\Delta(e_{\max}-e_{\min})\,x(1-x)$. Since $\dot V$ is decreasing in $E$ on the host {{eq:host}} and on the threshold host below, the comparison theorem makes $V(t)$ nondecreasing in $\Delta$ at every $t$, so the minimum of $V$ along the path is nondecreasing in $\Delta$, and by continuity the margins that keep it at or above the floor form an interval $[\Delta_{\min},\infty)$. It is not empty on {{eq:host}}: as $\Delta\to\infty$ the load falls to $e_{\min}$ at once, and $V$ then moves monotonically toward $1-h_Ve_{\min}/\rho>V_{\min}$. On a host whose per-capita regeneration is lower at every level and negative below a threshold $V_u$, a path that falls below $V_u$ does not return; the interval is then not empty only if the state at which the margin takes force lies above $V_u$ at that load, and a normative floor below $V_u$ would make crossing the floor a loss that is not the first.

(2) Without (I1), {{load}} applies; without (I2) in rule-making, $x_{\rm rule}\to1$ and $\tau_{\rm eff}\to0$; without (I3), silent crossing follows ({{slowroute}}); with rule-making captured first, the floor can be crossed when rule-making adapts slowly.

(3) If the cumulative advantage $B(t)=\int_0^t(\nu-\tau_{\rm eff})\,ds$ tends to $+\infty$, the race share tends to one, the load to $\bar e$, and under (reach) {{load}} puts the host below its floor; so viability at every horizon keeps $B$ from tending to $+\infty$. If the lim sup of the time average of $\tau_{\rm eff}$ were below $\nu$, there would be $\epsilon>0$ and $T_0$ with average at most $\nu-\epsilon$ beyond $T_0$, so $B(t)\ge\epsilon t-C\to\infty$. The converse fails, as the numerics show. $\square$

*The margin, computed* [num: HO]. On the forgiving host ($\rho=0.05$, $h_V=0.08$, $V_0=1$, the race strategy holding 99% of activity, $e_{\max}=1$): with $e_{\min}=0$, a margin of 0.08 from the start dips to 0.1397 before recovering to 1.000, and $\Delta_{\min}$ is 0.108, 0.165 and 0.355 for floors 0.2, 0.3 and 0.5; with $e_{\min}=0.1$ the long-run level is 0.840, a margin of 0.08 dips to 0.1136, and $\Delta_{\min}$ is 0.125, 0.192 and 0.414. Three independent integrations, a closed-form share with its own stopping rule, a replicator integrated jointly with the host, and the memo's own code, agree on these margins to 0.002. Below the margin this host recovers: with $\Delta=0.02$ it falls to 0.0025 and returns to 0.840, since on the logistic host any $\Delta>0$ recovers eventually. On the pointwise harsher threshold host $\rho_A(V/A-1)(1-V)$, which degrades by itself below its threshold $V_u(e_{\min})$, with $e_{\min}=0.1$ and the same initial composition, the margin that keeps the transient above $V_u$ is 0.260, 0.306, 0.359, 0.423 and 0.505 for $A=0.05,0.10,0.15,0.20,0.25$, with $V_u$ equal to 0.254, 0.308, 0.364, 0.421 and 0.481; below it the collapse is permanent. For $A\ge0.10$, $V_u$ lies above a normative floor of 0.3, so the floor must be set no lower than the host's threshold for crossing it to be the first loss. *Part (3) is not sufficient.* With $\tau_{\rm eff}=\nu-1/(2\sqrt{t+1})$ the average tends to $\nu$, but $B(t)=\sqrt{t+1}-1$ grows without bound, and from a race share of 0.001 the host crosses its floor at $t=89$; with $\tau_{\rm eff}=\nu$ it does not. The averaged condition of R1 is necessary and not sufficient; the cumulative one is the condition the proof uses.

*What the computation is.* Earlier versions called the threshold-host margin "the first computable instance of the correctable kernel", and stated viability under a clause, "viable if, in addition, the host stays above its floor while the load falls", that assumed what was to be shown. Both are withdrawn: the clause was circular, and the margin condition replaces it on both hosts. The computation is an instance of the boundary of viable parameters for one fixed strategy of correction; the correctable kernel of {{kernel}} takes a union over admissible strategies and an intersection over disturbances in the justified range, and is not computed here.

### Rival verification

**{{!Proposition:rivalverify}} (Rival verification) [thm].** *A rival's verifier whose full inbound row sum is below one, counting conversion from the verified party, from its own side and from shared sources such as models, supply chains and professional paradigms, is captured by none of them. Rivalry keeps the verified party's contribution near zero; it does not by itself bound the rest of the row.*

*Proof.* Apply {{certificate}}(2) to the verifier's full row. A verifier taking 0.10 from the verified party and 0.95 from shared sources is captured once those sources are, whatever the rivalry. $\square$

*Scope.* Rival verifiers raise false alarms, can collude against third parties, and check compliance rather than judgment. A verifier biased toward finding violations is, for claims the other side cannot check, a sender with a material bias, and talk survives only while the effective bias is below $\frac14$ ({{talk}}); so its findings must rest on evidence the other side can check. An accusation under rivalry can trigger prevention ({{preventown}}), so joint resolution precedes any response.

### Participation

**{{!Proposition:participate}} (Participation) [thm].** Under complete information an arrangement is efficient for a pair of types iff its survival-weighted joint value exceeds the sum of their survival-weighted race payoffs, and an overwhelmingly strong actor takes part only for a share near its race payoff. Under private types an arrangement without verification must give each type an interim share independent of its report, and is feasible iff the joint value covers the sum of the types' best race payoffs; verification that reveals types removes the information rents, and the constraint relaxes as survival in the race falls below survival under the arrangement.

*Two actors have private types, strong or weak, with costs $c_s<c_w$, each strong with probability $p_s$ independently. Outside the arrangement types are revealed in a complete-information all-pay contest for $\Omega+L_b$: a strong actor against a weak one gets $U^{\rm race}=(\Omega+L_b)(1-c_s/c_w)-L_b$, and otherwise $-L_b$; survival in the race is $\Phi^{\rm race}$. The arrangement yields a common value net of costs $S_a$, divided in shares $\mathsf s_i$, with survival $\Phi^R$; an actor's utility is $\Phi_i(\mathsf v_i+\bar{\mathsf v})$, with $\mathsf v_i$ its payoff and $\bar{\mathsf v}$ a value it keeps either way. (1) Under complete information and transferable utility, a pair gains iff $\Phi^R(S_a+2\bar{\mathsf v})\ge\sum_i\Phi^{\rm race}_i(U^{\rm race}_i+\bar{\mathsf v})$. (2) With private independent types and participation decided before types are revealed, Bayesian incentive compatibility makes each type's interim expected share independent of its report, budget balance makes the interim shares constants summing to one, and the arrangement is feasible without verification iff $\Phi^R(S_a+2\bar{\mathsf v})\ge\sum_i\sup_{c}E\big[\Phi^{\rm race}(c,t_j)(U^{\rm race}_i+\bar{\mathsf v})\mid c\big]$; ex-post shares may still vary with reports. (3) Verification revealing types removes the information rents; it is distinct from the timely detection of defection. (4) The information condition relaxes as $\Phi^{\rm race}/\Phi^R$ falls. (5) Under complete information an overwhelmingly strong actor takes part only for a share near its race payoff; raising the joint value, lowering the value of winning, compressing survival or changing the asymmetry of costs can still make equal shares feasible.*

*Scope.* Rare strong types expect much from the race: mutual optimism, a failure of bargaining under private information (Fearon 1995). Which mechanism is second-best depends on the mechanisms allowed and on commitment, and is not solved here. Participation benefits, voting rights and protection against domination are three different quantities in this model: (1) and (2) price the first, say nothing about the second, and the third is a constraint fixed before the shares are set (Section 10.3).

*Proof.* (1) is the comparison of joint survival-weighted values with transfers. (2) Interim incentive compatibility with independent types equalizes each type's interim expected share across reports; budget balance then fixes the interim shares as constants summing to one, and participation of every type needs the joint value to cover each type's best expected race payoff. (3) Revealed types allow shares conditioned on types, removing the rents. (4) and (5) follow from the form of the inequalities. $\square$

With $\Phi^R=0.95$, $\bar{\mathsf v}=1$ and $p_s=0.2$, equal shares become feasible once $\Phi^{\rm race}/\Phi^R\le0.872$.
