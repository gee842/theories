## X8. The alternatives in full

Each alternative is given its best case on its merits, how it would be installed, what remains, what it changes, and what evidence would show that it suffices. Section 8.3 of the core states each in one paragraph; the formal results are in the core and S8. The chapter ends with the tables Section 12.1 compares on: the policies now in force, profiled in advance; the durability of every arrangement by its own documents; and the failure modes of every path, with whether each can be reversed. One evidence standard holds in every row, the paper's own design included.

### Technical alignment and AI-assisted correction

**Best case.** The strongest form is not "technical alignment alone". Control is scoped to early transformative systems and used, under control, to accelerate alignment and oversight research while the window lasts (Greenblatt, Shlegeris, Sachan and Roger 2024): control protocols tested against red teams raise safety at a given level of usefulness in the settings studied. Weak supervisors can elicit part of a stronger model's capability (Burns et al. 2023); in auditing games, teams with access to training data found a planted hidden objective that a team with black-box access alone did not, which supports mandated access (Marks et al. 2025). Oversight can be built redundantly or adversarially by debate, and formal methods certify invariants without predicting trajectories. AI may cheapen independent correction as fast as control, so that AI-assisted correction keeps pace ({{corrrate}}), and correction people cannot follow can still lower residual risk ({{opaquecorr}}). Research can drive the alignment tax to zero or below, class by class, until the safe variant is also the winning one; once published it is common to all, so the safeguards it makes cheap spread without coordination, and the common constraint loses its reason to exist in that class (Section 6.4; {{nested}}). Its proponents' own plans rank a strong international agreement with a long slowdown first, and treat technical work as what one does when that is unavailable.

**What remains.** Principal alignment does not change the game among principals ({{faithful}}). The window lasts only as long as the race allows, and the race shortens it. Systems are retained for performance ({{retain}}), and progress that lowers the hazard per unit of racing or delegation is partly absorbed by more of both ({{homeostasis}}). Redundant oversight is capped by common causes, which selection produces, and certified row sums are a sufficient certificate, not a proof of independent errors ({{topology}}). AI-assisted correction is only as independent as its funding, access, appointment and lineage. One part of the tax no technique removes: where countering a divergence means waiting for evidence that arrives after a manifestation window, its cost is time, which relative selection with decisive advantage always prices ({{tempo}}). For masked divergences, which change under evaluation or appear only in some contexts, admission removes only what it detects; admission for that class has to be stated in capability, enforced in common, with access to weights, activations and training data for auditors, and beyond the threshold E2 or E3 for the class ({{masked}}; EV6). Which divergences are tolerated is set at the outermost loop ({{nested}}).

**What it changes.** Tempo, observation and residual harm per unit, and the alignment tax where the research that lowers it is published. It becomes part of a change of selection, G3 and G7, when held by correctors separated from competitive success, under constraints common to all. **What would show it suffices:** the safety of control protocols at a fixed audit budget not falling across generations; catch rates of planted defects at fixed human hours not falling as capability rises, controlled for evaluation awareness (C3a); auditors with fixed access still finding planted and emergent hidden objectives as capability rises (C12); independent evaluators keeping certificates while depending on the evaluated for access; the tax measured at zero or below, class by class (C9); residual risk per hazard class falling over generations (C1).

### Regulated competition with strong correction

**Best case.** Competition in products and services continues; liability and licensing make consequences return; evaluation access is mandated and thresholds fixed in advance; and where the effective hazard is high and capability transparent, pacing can be the unique equilibrium of a capability race without any agreement (Fudenberg and Koh 2026). There are precedents of durable safety with unchanged commercial motives: an industry that knows one accident can end it for all, as civil nuclear power came to after a major accident; and aviation, with independent accident investigation, liability and insurance pricing. This is the bounded competition a cooperative order can contain (V5).

**Installation.** Each jurisdiction regulates its own developers; coverage must reach every capable actor, since the race is set by the most race-inclined actor ({{join}}); the rule-makers are themselves in a contest of standing, so no one of them imposes the liability alone ({{nounilateral}}); rule-making is subject to Section 4.

**What remains.** Liability cannot exceed the assets of the liable (Shavell 1986): for catastrophic and common harms that is the point at which a common cost cannot be turned into a private one, which is why liability alone does not settle the alignment tax. Regulation in one market leaves the load elsewhere. Two developers' frameworks condition commitments on rivals, one of them stating that it cannot commit to mitigations unilaterally because of a collective action problem while keeping a discretion to pause; one unreviewed study codes 77% of the changes it traced, in twelve pairs of consecutive framework versions, as weakening (EV2). Public evaluation bodies have shifted their mandates toward industry contact under voluntary access (EV3). The precedents had no decisive prize.

**The pacing equilibrium, compared with E3.** The strongest formal result for this path deserves more than a clause. As the register reads it, from the abstract only (EV11), Fudenberg and Koh (2026) study a capability race against a linearly advancing safety frontier and find regions of racing only, of coexistence and of pacing only; with a high effective hazard and transparent capability, pacing can be the unique Markov perfect equilibrium, with no agreement among the developers. Where that holds, it is E3 reached as an equilibrium of bounded competition: capability paced to what can be made safe, because the hazard of outrunning the frontier is seen and borne by whoever would outrun it. This paper does not oppose it, and a cooperative order would contain it. The correspondence that follows is this paper's reading, and it turns on the result's two conditions, which are where the mechanisms of Part I bear.
- *The effective hazard* is the part of a hazard that the objective an actor acts on counts, and that it sees. Relative accounting weighs a shared hazard by $1+\theta$ and drops it at $\theta=-1$ ({{twochan}}), and compounding narrows the working objective as the discounted loop gain rises ({{standprice}}), so the effective hazard falls exactly where the gain rises. Where harm manifests late, or is masked, the hazard seen is below the real one by the gap on which the silent route runs ({{slowroute}}): a path can lie in the racing region of the real parameters while its participants compute the pacing region.
- *Transparency* fails where the conditions of winning predict that it fails: the quiet deviation is the one that binds enforcement ({{enforce}}), captured evaluation masks the harm ({{masking}}), masked divergences hide under evaluation ({{masked}}), and internal deployment and the open–closed gap hide capability (EV5, EV6).
- *What the comparison adds.* Where outrunning the frontier can yield a decisive position, the prize ends the game and the enforcement condition, not the hazard, decides ({{enforce}}). Speed can also be bought outside the frontier by delegation (Section 6.3), and the frontier's position is measured by someone whom success can buy ({{influence}}).

So the pacing equilibrium is not an alternative to the marks of Section 8.2 but an argument for two of them: G1, consequences return to their source, keeps the effective hazard high; G6, measurement and language are shared, supplies the transparency. Where both hold for every capable actor, regulated competition and the generative direction coincide. The question this result puts is therefore not whether pacing equilibria exist, which in that model they do, but whether the present path lies in their region, class by class. The measurements that answer it are those of Section 7.3 and the cruxes of Section 12.3: late-manifesting hazard classes and their evidence lags (C1), the open–closed gap and internal deployment (C5), and the conversion data of T4 (C2). Where they place a class in the pacing region, E3 for that class needs no agreement, and the paper says so; where they do not, pacing has to be made an equilibrium by the arrangements of Section 11.3. The correspondence should be checked against the full paper in outside review.

**What it changes.** This depends on content. Regulation that returns consequences and separates evaluators from success, across every capable actor, is G1 and G3 for all, changes the basin, and is the change this paper argues for, whatever it is called. Regulation that constrains conduct while success can still buy the rule-makers is predicted to erode as stakes and speed grow. **What would show it suffices:** competitor clauses not invoked; thresholds held when rivals advance; the mandates of evaluation bodies widening as the stakes rise; findings executed against leaders (C2); effective liability above the private advantage of load for every capable carrier.

### Leadership and a safety margin

**Best case.** A leader spends part of its lead on safety: it slows, evaluates and hardens before the follower catches up. A large lead can discourage the follower (Harris and Vickers 1987; Konrad and Kovenock 2009); a laggard far behind under moderate hostility does not gamble on risky technology (Stafford, Trager and Dafoe 2022); and a developer able to commit first to a slightly slower speed gains, because rivals slow too, a local result that does not depend on holding a lead (Bueno de Mesquita, Dziuda and Polborn 2026). One developer's framework includes such a "leading" scenario (EV2).

**What remains.** {{lead}}: leads diffuse even when the follower does not accelerate; the margin must buy coverage, and buys nothing for hazard classes whose evidence lags exceed it; it is judged inside the contest; and a leader seeing its window close has a motive to strike first. Spending the lead returns the contest toward parity, where effort is highest in contests of step-by-step innovation (Aghion, Harris, Howitt and Vickers 2001) and, where a lead discourages the follower, the price of standing peaks in the contests computed (Section 7.2). **What it changes.** Tempo for a time; it keeps the objective of winning. Its best case, a first commitment to slow that rivals answer by slowing, is a step toward reciprocal restraint, the first stage of a transition. **What would show it suffices:** leads not spent on speed; rivals slowing in response; margins long relative to the evidence lags of the hazard classes.

### The benign winner: win, bind, share

**Best case.** The strongest form is that a winner wins, binds itself and shares. After great wars, victors have bound themselves through institutions to lower others' fear and their own costs of enforcement (Ikenberry 2001); a dominant power can supply public goods a field of equals would not (Kindleberger 1973; Gilpin 1981), and unipolarity can be stable (Wohlforth 1999); an encompassing ruler internalizes the shared conditions (Olson 1993). In the model of Section 3.5 a single developer restrains whenever restraint is worth more to the field than it costs, so the failure lies in the race, not in the existence of a winner. The holder of the first nuclear monopoly, through the period of superiority to 1954, did not extend its dominance to everything it could reach (X11). In AI the strategy has been proposed, in its authors' terms, as humanitarian and defensive: a coalition seeks a clear advantage, including military superiority and the denial of key inputs such as chips and semiconductor equipment to adversaries, and then offers its benefits to others in exchange for their support, so that adversaries give up competing (EV1). It persuades by promising both security and benefits; both rest on the winner's restraint after the fact, which is what the commitment tests examine. By this paper's own results the form is strongest where the winner still needs the governed: a winner whose self-model is accurate toward what can be divided, $\theta=\kappa_W$, gives them $\kappa_W/(1+\kappa_W)$ under logarithmic utility ({{wingives}}; {{boundary}}(2)). Most strong international institutions were built by victors; that is the record this path appeals to, and the record a transition among rivals near parity has to answer (X11).

**What remains.** The commitment tests. After a decisive win what the others receive is set by the winner's objective alone, and a promise to share is worth breaking ({{wingives}}). What the race cannot verify beforehand is $\kappa_W$ after victory: a winner, like any institution, stays answerable because it depends on the labour, consent and judgment of the people it governs, and AI can substitute for each (Kulveit et al. 2025). The historical cases had advantages that were not decisive, and their restraint rested on institutions others could check and on that dependence (Acemoglu and Robinson 2012); whether AI removes it is crux C13. Where conduct before victory and after it go together, the race shifts winners toward those that extend dominance ({{raceselects}}). And the winner is itself a field of agencies, firms, laboratories and systems, whose restraint after victory depends on whether its own correctors can be bought by its successes; an AI aligned to the winner is not thereby aligned to the people it governs (Section 6.3).

**What it changes.** Nothing in selection before victory; after victory, everything depends on the winner's internal field. **What would show it suffices**, before victory: binding limits on what victory would confer; benefits shared as they arise rather than promised; the coalition's own correctors, constitutional correctors included, certified with row sums below one, with succession rules that survive changes of leadership (Test I); and a measured dependence of the coalition on the people it would govern that AI does not erode (C13). A winner that adopts these has changed what it selects for.

### A finite sprint, then restraint

**Best case.** An actor sprints to a decisive lead and then stops compounding: it freezes or rolls back, and governs at the tempo of understanding. Decisive contests are won with a modest edge when $r$ is large, so the sprint need not run far beyond understanding. A winner that freezes or rolls back keeps positive survival ({{freeze}}), and after a decisive win no contest remains to price standing, so the pressure of Section 3.5 no longer acts on the winner.

**What remains, by kind of objection.**
- *Exposure*, an empirical objection: the path passes through its greatest exposure before any restraint begins, and through the rivals' strongest motive for preventive attack ({{preventattack}}); whether that exposure is survivable is crux C1 for the hazards of development and C5 for decisiveness.
- *Maintenance*, a normative objection: a decisive advantage not used to stop others' growth does not stay decisive, and using it so ends their O2, which V2 and V3 protect ({{sprint}}). Under a survival-first comparison a sprint can preserve the sprinter's survival; what the freeze gives up is permanent superiority, which survival does not need.
- *Mechanism*, the paper's own answer, which needs neither of the others. The freeze is a stop imposed by one actor on the rest, a constraint of the most unequal incidence a stop can have; those it binds are re-sorted by it and evade it wherever evasion pays their type ({{blind}}(2), (3)), so it holds only by a sanction the sprinter supplies, and it reads to every rival as a lead being kept. And it is held by the largest conversion target there is: the return to influence over correctors scales with the square of share ({{influence}}), and cultivated conversion with a high moment of shares ({{corrrate}}(4)), so whatever is to check the freeze is what the sprinter's success can most cheaply buy.

**Against common custody.** E2 and common custody also stop every actor from building the decisive class, and a coalition that stops outsiders need not end their O2 (the non-proliferation row of X11). So the stop is not the difference. Incidence and holder are, and the durability tests show them, read by the same standard on both sides:

| | Sprint, then freeze | Common custody (Section 10.5; X10) |
|---|---|---|
| Who bears the stop | Every actor except the sprinter, which keeps the frozen class; selected to be evaded ({{blind}}) | Everyone, the custodians' own capability included, by design; release by $k$ of $n$ with non-aligned members among the $k$; whether incidence is equal in effect is to be measured |
| D4 Who corrects the holder | The sprinter's own evaluators, held by the largest carrier and the largest conversion target ({{influence}}) | Functions held separately, funded by formula and appointed insulated from success, with evaluators of different lineage and funding certified outside the holder, a chamber of the affected and an appeal, by design; the arrangement is itself a large conversion target, and its certificates are not yet measured (C4) |

The comparison does not make custody easy. It makes exact what the sprint lacks: incidence that includes the holder, and a corrector the holder did not appoint.

**What it changes.** Nothing during the sprint; after it, a stop of the most unequal incidence held by the largest conversion target.

### Deterrence and prevention

Prevention can be survival-preferred for the preventer ({{preventown}}). The objections are three, and none is a change of criterion. *Normative:* it ends or endangers others' O1 and O2. *Epistemic:* aimed at a rival believed certain to race, it destroys, when the rival is in fact survival-first, a game in which restraint was possible, and can lead the rival to act as the type it was feared to be (the spiral model; Jervis 1976; Kydd 1997), while shifts that cannot be observed raise the chance of mistaken prevention (Debs and Monteiro 2014). *Technical:* detecting unobservable progress and striking at machine speed brings the delegation of Section 6.3.

Deterrence by threatened sabotage aims to make a decisive sprint not worth attempting. *Best case:* large datacentres can be observed and compute thresholds made salient (Hendrycks, Schmidt and Wang 2025). *What remains:* red lines that are unclear and hard to monitor, and doubtful credibility of the threat of sabotage, make deterrence of this kind unstable unless they are refined (Abecassis 2025); while the value of winning stays large, timely detection and credibility approach their limits ({{verify}}); among nuclear-armed rivals credibility is the first problem, and under survival-first comparisons it can reverse; pushing AI into military capability strengthens the pressure to delegate ({{delegate}}); verification that supports deterrence can also supply targets (Coe and Vaynman 2020); and algorithmic progress erodes thresholds stated in compute (EV9). *What it changes:* it acts on $p_d$ by threat, as common custody does by holding the decisive class, through a different means and with a different fallback, war or race.

### The hard case

Let a survival-first actor hold the prior $\mu$ that a capable rival holds an unbounded relative objective. When $\mu$ is low, its best course is to propose verifiable limits, and to take narrow E1 unilaterally and verifiably in its own strategic loops, the costly signal of {{signal}}. When $\mu$ is high, it compares denial, a coalition that stops the decisive class, and prevention. Coverage of every capable actor then comes from consent or from coercion. Denying the inputs to the decisive class is the coercive form of a stop; it raises the weight on winning and the trust threshold, and if it escalates it falls under {{preventown}}. The theory does not derive that its net effect is positive and does not endorse it. Section 12.1 admits the coercive restriction of frontier inputs to its comparison only under three conditions of its own, and even there compares it rather than recommends it. If neither consent nor a coercion of positive net effect is available, no actor has a secure position: that is the hard case, stated as such. Insuring against falling behind is not an exit ({{insure}}): it accepts the frame of the contest, and against a winner of the logic Section 3.8 describes, falling behind cannot be made survivable.

### A verified agreement over a contest that remains

**Best case.** Rivals agree to slow and to delay decisive capability, with compute declarations, supply-chain accounting, recomputation of workloads, research transparency, and mutually assured destruction of compute if the agreement dissolves; it works without trust and bounds the largest covert project that could escape detection. One detailed plan of this kind, a deal among leading powers to delay superintelligence to about 2040 (Larsen et al. 2026; EV10), adds inference-only retrofits of datacentres and hardened reserves of about two per cent of pre-deal compute, and aims to end winner-take-all dynamics through many frontier developers, transparency, broad diffusion and a citizens' dividend. Its own scenario estimates do not show near-certain dissolution: one author's judgment puts decline in years five to ten at about 4% a year, half dissolution and half impairment, and cumulative decline over ten years at about 48% (EV10). Its handoff to AI systems is a conditional decision that weighs further alignment research against other risks, and it keeps an option to extend the pause. The plan is one instance of a position, keep the contest, slow it and stay safe; it is cited so that the tests can be applied to documents rather than to a type, and the answer below is to the position, not to the plan's figures.

**What remains.** Verification is required exactly to the degree that the value of winning exceeds the value of cooperating; its threshold is the deterrence condition of the inspection game (Avenhaus, von Stengel and Zamir 2002), the enforcement condition seen from verification ({{detectlink}}); and the net gain from exit can grow with the capability held in check ({{verify}}; {{overhang}}). The bound on covert projects holds only while the coalition's compute advantage keeps pace with the diffusion of algorithmic efficiency, which is not derived (EV9).

**What it changes.** It slows and caps, acting on tempo and decisiveness, and keeps relative standing in its enforcement, through proportional reserves and a coalition that must stay ahead of covert projects; the conversion of success into control over correction is addressed only through transparency. An agreement that preserves relative positions preserves the relative term of every payoff, and with it the motive to exit ({{overhang}}(3)). That makes it a bridge. Whether it can be the end depends on the durability tests and on whether it lowers the value of winning, as G2 and G5 do, or only bounds defection. Common custody faces the same verification limits as any agreement; what differs is its fallback, and whether it lowers the value of winning, a claim with the same standing as any agreement's.

Read across paths, the table has one failure in common: one party comes to hold decisive capability. On continuation it is a decisive win; for the benign winner, its restraint failing; for the sprint, the freeze broken; for the agreement, a covert project escaping; for the coercive path, the restrictor's correctors converted; for the design, the capture of custody. That failure is irreversible on every path. The race produces it by construction and selects for it ({{raceselects}}); the design contests it by separation, plural lineages and the tests D4 and D5. The alternatives that keep the contest share continuation's irreversible modes and add their own. The design's other failures are reversible into separately held functions, except the escalation of its coercive fallback, which every coercive path carries; none of those reversals has yet been shown to work. Continuation's are reversible only while no position is decisive and no floor has been crossed. This asymmetry, with its support marked row by row, is what the third tier of Section 12.1 weighs, and a reader who weighs the rows differently can reach another ranking.

### Where the criteria disagree

Under the protection standard the worst case includes a rising uncovered share on the continuation path, because its premises are named mechanisms the register does not contradict (EV5–EV7); it equally includes the capture of a common custodian on the cooperative path, because its correctors' certificates can fail ({{certificate}}), and the other failure modes of the table above. Both fail the tolerance in their worst case, and the comparison moves to the standard's third step, which needs estimates on both sides. An expected-value criterion that puts low probability on a rising gap and values speed highly can prefer continuation; a discounted criterion with utility linear in consumption prefers racing whenever the hazard it adds is below the growth it adds, and with curvature in utility it stops (S8). A well-corrected continuation can outperform a fragile cooperative arrangement; the analysis does not establish that every cooperative arrangement outperforms regulated competition.
