## S6. AI: instrument and incarnation

This chapter gives the proofs and numerics of Section 6; the full statements of three results the core states in a sentence ({{homeostasis}}, {{opaquecorr}}, {{lyapunov}}); three supplementary results: how certain a lock must be for narrow E1 to need no trust ({{nearlock}}), the formal part of the masked class, and the bounded optimizer ({{boundedopt}}); and, from Section 6.4, the sources of weak incarnation, the demonstrations of masking and the worked instance of legible reasoning. Numbers cite the block of `checks_v71.py` that reproduces them; AL is the alignment review's independent recomputation of Section 6 (`../v6.1/reviews/scripts-v6.1-alignment/al_checks.py`), ported whole, with its parts named as the reviewer named them.

### Proof of {{faithful}}

The harm coefficient, $d$ in V6.1, is written $h_d$, since $d$ is the correction rate; its exponent in (3) is $\beta_h$ (the log of SM(5) writes $\beta_d$).

Changing $a_i$ from 0 to 1 changes $u_i$ by $\epsilon b-c-\epsilon h_d$, independently of the others' choices, and each other $u_j$ by $-\epsilon b/(n-1)-\epsilon h_d$. Summed over all principals the relative terms cancel: at all-racing each principal has $-c-n\epsilon h_d$, which gives (1). In objectives, racing changes $J_i$ by $\epsilon b\big(1-\frac{\theta}{n-1}\big)-c-\epsilon h_d(1+\theta)$, which is positive iff $\theta<\theta^\ast$; this gives (2). For (3), write $\theta^\ast=(T-H-c)/(T/(n-1)+H)$ with $T=\epsilon b$ and $H=\epsilon h_d$ growing with exponents $1+\beta_b$ and $1+\beta_h$, and $c$ with $\beta_c$. If $\beta_c<1+\max(\beta_b,\beta_h)$, $c$ is negligible against the larger of $T$ and $H$, and the limit is set by $T/H$, which is constant, unbounded or vanishing as $\beta_b$ equals, exceeds or falls short of $\beta_h$. If $\beta_c>1+\max(\beta_b,\beta_h)$, the numerator tends to $-\infty$ faster than the denominator grows. $\square$

*Numerics* ($n=4$, $b=1$, $h_d=0.2$, $c=0.3$). Exponents $(\beta_b,\beta_h,\beta_c)=(0,0,0)$ and $(1,1,0)$ give the limit 1.50; $(0,0.5,0)$ gives $-0.99$ at $\epsilon=10^6$; $(0.5,0,0)$ gives 2.998; $(1,0,3)$ and $(0,0,1.5)$ diverge to $-\infty$; at the boundary $\beta_c=1+\max(\beta_b,\beta_h)$, $(0,0,1)$ gives 0.9375 and $(1,0,2)$ gives 2.1 [num: SM(5)]. With $b$, $h_d$ and $c$ fixed, $\theta^\ast$ is 0.9375 at $\epsilon=1$ and 1.44 at $\epsilon=10$; with $c$ also scaling with $\epsilon$ it stays 0.94 [num: AL(6.1)]. The double edge of Section 6.2: at $\epsilon=1$, all-racing gives each principal $-1.1$; lowering $h_d$ to 0.1 and 0.05 cuts the loss to 0.70 and 0.50 while raising $\theta^\ast$ to 1.39 and 1.70, and $\Delta J_i$ changes sign at $\theta^\ast$ [num: AL(6.1)]. This is the second fate of safety progress in Section 6.4. The condition once written as $\beta_c<\beta_b=\beta_d$ was neither necessary nor sufficient; the correct one is $\beta_c<1+\max(\beta_b,\beta_h)$.

### Proof of {{delegate}}

Actor $i$'s payoff is $-L_e(1-w_i)-L_c\big[1-\prod_j(1-\psi_j)\big]$, with $w_i=(1+s\iota_i)/\sum_j(1+s\iota_j)$. (1) With $k$ others delegating, $w_i(1,k)=(1+s)/(n+(k+1)s)$ and $w_i(0,k)=1/(n+ks)$. The first is non-decreasing in $s$ and the second decreasing, so the gain $L_e[w_i(1,k)-w_i(0,k)]$ increases in $s$, toward $L_e(1-1/n)$ for $k=0$ and $L_e/(k+1)$ for $k\ge1$. The cost is the change in shared survival, $L_c\Delta\psi(1-\psi_0)^{n-1-k}(1-\psi_1)^k$, independent of $s$. If every limit exceeds its cost, there is a speed beyond which delegating is strictly better at every $k$; if one limit does not, delegating is worse at that $k$ for every $s$, since the gain stays below its limit. The smallest limit is $L_e/n$ and the largest cost $L_c\Delta\psi$, so $L_e/n>L_c\Delta\psi$ suffices. (2) With all delegating $w_i=1/n$, as with none, and shared survival is $(1-\psi_1)^n<(1-\psi_0)^n$. (3) Under relative objectives the shared term is common to all and cancels; the gain in $w_i$ remains, and is positive for every $s>0$. $\square$

*Numerics.* With $n=3$, $L_e=L_c$, $\psi_0=0.01$ and $\psi_1=0.2$, delegation is dominant from $s^\ast=1.1628$, where a lone delegator wins with probability 0.5196 against $1/3$ without delegation [num: AL(6.2)]. A speed advantage of this size is far from decisive, which is the sense of "well below decisive capability" in Section 6.3.

### Proof of {{lockin}}, and where $q_L$ comes from

With $\dot{\mathcal M}=-\delta_h\mathcal M$ under delegation and $\dot{\mathcal M}=\varrho_h(\bar{\mathcal M}-\mathcal M)$ otherwise, capacity reaches $\mathcal M_{\min}$ after $T_{\rm lock}=\ln(\mathcal M_0/\mathcal M_{\min})/\delta_h$ of delegation, and from $\mathcal M<\mathcal M_{\min}$ it regains $\mathcal M_{\min}$ after $T_R(\mathcal M)=\varrho_h^{-1}\ln[(\bar{\mathcal M}-\mathcal M)/(\bar{\mathcal M}-\mathcal M_{\min})]$. Capacity maintained in parallel does not decay; the resources it uses are withdrawn from strength. $\square$

The lock probability $q_L$ of {{delegatelock}} is therefore not free. Let a delegation last $T_{\rm del}$, and let the contest allow an actor that reverts a window $T_W$ before its next consequential decision. The lock counts if the delegated decision is irreversible within the window for deliberation, or if $T_{\rm del}>T_{\rm lock}$ and $T_W<T_R\big(\mathcal M(T_{\rm del})\big)$; $q_L$ is the probability of that event, set by the kind of decision, the contest's tempo and $\delta_h$. Drilled parallel capacity keeps the second part of the event rare at a cost in strength, which is why it has to be held in common. The same capacity carries correction: where the effective correction rate is $d(\mathcal M/\mathcal M_0)^w$ and $\mathcal M$ decays at $\delta_h$, the rate at which independent correction must grow rises by $w\delta_h$ ({{humancorr}}; S4, block QS).

### {{delegatelock}} in full, and proof

*Let locked delegation end the delegator's ability to overrule with probability $q_L$, and losing the contest mean subordination with probability $p$. With $Q=\prod_j(1-\psi_j)$ and win probabilities as in {{delegate}}, actor $i$'s survival is $\Phi_i=(1-q_L\iota_i)\,Q\,[w_i+(1-w_i)(1-p)]$. Write $a=(1-\psi_1)/(1-\psi_0)$ and, with $k$ others delegating, $w_D=(1+s)/(n+(k+1)s)$ and $w_N=1/(n+ks)$, so that*
$$\frac{\Phi_D}{\Phi_N}=(1-q_L)\,a\,\frac{1-p+p\,w_D}{1-p+p\,w_N}.$$
*(1) If $q_L=1$ and $Q>0$, not delegating gives strictly higher survival for every finite $s$, every $n$, every $p$ and every profile of the others. (2) If $p=1$ and $A=(1-q_L)a$, the ratio is $A(1+s)(n+ks)/(n+(k+1)s)$, which rises with $k$; all delegating is an equilibrium iff $A(n+(n-1)s)/n\ge1$, and no one delegating iff $An(1+s)/(n+s)\le1$. At least one always holds, since $A(n+(n-1)s)/n-An(1+s)/(n+s)=A(n-1)s^2/[n(n+s)]\ge0$, and both can. (3) If $p<1$, the subordination term $1-p$ dilutes the win probabilities and the ratio can fall as $k$ rises. If it decreases in $k$ and crosses one between $k=m-1$ and $k=m$ for some $1\le m\le n-1$, the profiles with exactly $m$ delegators are the only pure equilibria, and neither corner is one. (4) At bounded speed one delegator does not drive another's survival to zero: a non-delegator facing $k$ delegators survives with probability at least $Q_N/(n+ks)>0$.*

*Proof.* (1) $\Phi_D=0$ while $\Phi_N\ge Q_Nw_N>0$. (2) Take $k=n-1$ for all delegating and $k=0$ for none; the identity gives "at least one". With $p=1$ delegations are complements. (3) A profile with $m$ delegators is an equilibrium iff the ratio is at least one at $k=m-1$, so that no delegator gains by stopping, and at most one at $k=m$, so that no other actor gains by starting; a decreasing ratio meets both conditions at one $m$ only. With $p<1$ delegations can be substitutes. (4) $\Phi_N=Q_N[1-p+p\,w_N]\ge Q_Nw_N$. $\square$

*Numerics.* The outside reviewer's case, $n=3$, $s=1$, $p=0.1$, $q_L=0$, $\psi_0=0$, $\psi_1=0.015$: the ratios for $k=0,1,2$ are 1.00259, 1.00097 and 0.99928, so the only pure equilibrium has two delegators; with $p=1$ the ratios rise with $k$, 1.48, 1.58 and 1.64, and all delegate [num: DL(a), (b)]. Over 20,000 random draws with $q_L<1$, neither corner is an equilibrium in 0 draws at $p=1$, 1,586 at $p=0.5$ and 60 at $p=0.1$ [num: DL(c)]. Part (1) holds in 20,000 of 20,000 random settings [num: DL(d)]. At $p=1$, over 100,000 draws "neither" never occurs and "both" occurs 4,068 times [num: SM(6)]; an independent recomputation with another sampling distribution finds 0 and 21,922, with the closed forms equal to the direct survival comparison in every draw [num: AL(6.4)]. With $n=2$, $p=1$, $q_L=0.99$, $\psi_0=0.01$ and $\psi_1=0.05$, all delegating is an equilibrium only for $s\ge206.4$, with survival 0.0045 there [num: AL(6.4)]. V6.1's core statement of part (2) omitted $p=1$, without which it is false.

**{{!Remark:nearlock}} (How certain the lock must be) [thm].** *In the model of {{delegatelock}}, not delegating gives strictly higher survival for every $n$, every $p$ and every profile of the others whenever $(1-q_L)(1+s)(1-\psi_1)<1-\psi_0$.* Since $w_N\le w_D\le(1+s)w_N$, the ratio $(1-p+pw_D)/(1-p+pw_N)$ lies between 1 and $1+s$, so $\Phi_D/\Phi_N\le(1-q_L)a(1+s)<1$. The bound is uniform in $n$, $p$ and the profile, holds at equality, and is approached as $n\to\infty$ at $p=1$. For a given $n$ a less certain lock suffices: $w_D/w_N=(1+s)(ks+n)/((k+1)s+n)$ rises with the number $k$ of others delegating, so the binding profile is all others delegating at $p=1$, where the ratio is $(1-q_L)a\big(1+(n-1)s/n\big)$, and $(1-q_L)a\big(1+(n-1)s/n\big)<1$ suffices; at $s=2$ the factor $1+(n-1)s/n$ is 2.0, 2.33, 2.8 and 2.98 for $n=2$, 3, 10 and 100, against the uniform 3 [num: VF]. A lock is near certain in the sense of narrow E1 when its probability of failing is below $(1-\psi_0)/\big((1-\psi_1)(1+s)\big)$: the larger the speed advantage, the nearer one the lock must be for keeping AI out to need no trust.

### Proofs of {{retain}} and {{transmit}}

*{{retain}}.* Variants acting on common terms exist and have relative performance lower by at least a positive margin, so under cumulative retention their share tends to zero, as in {{load}}; the retained systems' decisions are the actor's. If constraints are an enforced admission condition, no admissible variant violates them. That an actor which cannot overrule decisions taken in its name has lost O2 in that domain is a definition. For a reader who counts survival only as preservation of the unit, delegation acts through the shared hazard instead: delegated systems interacting at machine speed without a human veto raise the probability of shared loss by reciprocal fear of surprise attack (Schelling 1960), inadvertent escalation (Posen 1991) and flash war (Scharre 2018), an argument with cited mechanisms, not a derivation. $\square$

*{{transmit}}.* A faithful system maximizes its operator's objective. Under a relative payoff that objective puts a weight on winning, equivalent to a self-model $\theta<0$ ({{twochan}}(3)), and where reinvestment's relative return exceeds its common return the working objective narrows further ({{standprice}}). Retention by contest performance selects among systems on the same relative payoff. A more effective system raises the value the objective attains, not its content, as $\epsilon$ does in {{faithful}}. $\square$

### Risk homeostasis in delegation (Section 6.3)

**{{!Proposition:homeostasis}} (Risk homeostasis in delegation) [thm].** Inside the contest, gains in the safety of delegation are partly absorbed by more delegation. *In the model of {{delegate}}, let each actor choose $\iota_i\in[0,1]$, with strength $1+s\iota_i$ and hazard $\psi_0+\Delta\psi\,\iota_i$; write $x=\Delta\psi\,\iota$, $b_s=\Delta\psi/s$ and $K_h=(n-1)L_e/(n^2L_c)$. An interior symmetric equilibrium solves $(x+b_s)(1-\psi_0-x)^{n-1}=K_h$. (1) Where $x\ll1-\psi_0$, the added hazard is $nx\approx(n-1)L_e/\big(nL_c(1-\psi_0)^{n-1}\big)-n\Delta\psi/s$, nearly independent of $\Delta\psi$, until delegation saturates. (2) There can be several interior equilibria, and along a branch $\partial x/\partial\Delta\psi=-(1-\psi_0-x)/\big(s[1-\psi_0-nx-(n-1)b_s]\big)$, whose sign changes between branches. (3) After saturation, realized risk falls with $\Delta\psi$ and can be summable under full delegation and growing speed: with $n=3$, $L_e=L_c=1$, $\psi_0=0$, $s_t=2^{t+1}$ and $\Delta\psi_t=2^{-(t+1)}$, full delegation is an equilibrium at every $t$, the realized hazard is below one at every $t$, survival from the start is about 0.024, and from $t=4$ the realized risk is at most $3/16$. (4) With all-or-nothing delegation, any equilibrium with $k\ge1$ delegators has realized hazard above the baseline, so an improvement that carries $\Delta\psi$ across the threshold at which delegation becomes an equilibrium raises realized hazard.*

*Proof.* At the symmetric point $\partial w_i/\partial\iota_i=s(n-1)/(n^2(1+s\iota))$, and the marginal cost is $L_c\Delta\psi(1-\psi)^{n-1}$ with $1-\psi=1-\psi_0-x$; equate them and divide by $s$. (1) approximates for $x\ll1-\psi_0$. (2) differentiates implicitly. (3) The own marginal gain at full delegation, $2s_t/(9(1+s_t))-\Delta\psi_t(1-\Delta\psi_t)^2$, is positive at every $t$, and the objective is concave in own delegation, so full delegation is a best response; the realized hazard $1-(1-2^{-(t+1)})^3$ is below one at every $t$, survival from the start is $\prod_{t\ge1}(1-2^{-t})^3$, and the union bound gives $\sum_{t\ge4}3\cdot2^{-(t+1)}=3/16$. (4) $(1-\psi_0-\Delta\psi)^k<(1-\psi_0)^k$. $\square$

*Numerics.* (1): with $n=3$, $s=100$, $L_e=1$, $L_c=100$ and $\psi_0=0.01$, the added hazard is 0.0011, 0.0053, 0.0062, 0.0065 and 0.0067 as $\Delta\psi$ falls from 0.19 through 0.05, 0.02 and 0.01 to 0.005, so on the interior branch it rises as the hazard per unit of delegation falls, and it falls to 0.0030 only at complete delegation, $\Delta\psi=0.001$ [num: AL(6.11)]. (2): with $n=2$, $s=100$, $L_e=0.5$, $L_c=1$, $\psi_0=0.01$ and $\Delta\psi=0.9$ there are two interior roots, $\iota=0.153$ and $0.937$, and the corner is also an equilibrium [num: HO of `../v5/checks_v5.py`, run by `checks_v71.py --legacy`]. (3): the own marginal gain at full delegation is $+0.023$, $+0.037$, $+0.102$, $+0.154$ and $+0.186$ for $t=0,\dots,4$ and rises after, full delegation is a best response on a grid at $t=0,\dots,40$, survival from the start is 0.0241, and the exact realized risk from $t=4$ is 0.1726, against 0.1836 for the sum of the hazards [num: SM(12); AL(6.11)]. V6.1 stated (3) as "an equilibrium for $t\ge5$" with $s_t=2^t$, an example whose hazard at $t=0$ is 1, so that its survival from the start is zero; the shift by one period keeps every hazard below one, as {{nonsum}} needs. Compare Stafford, Trager and Dafoe (2022) on safety–performance trade-offs in races, and the risk compensation of Peltzman (1975) and Wilde (1982).

### The tendencies of weak incarnation (Section 6.4)

The tendencies the core names have formal sources as well as constructed demonstrations. In some environments optimal policies seek power for most reward functions (Turner et al. 2021), as do decision-makers that are not optimal but can be retargeted (Turner and Tadepalli 2022); trained policies can pursue misgeneralized goals (Langosco et al. 2022). The demonstrations are registered in EV6 with their limits, and those of masking are listed below with the formal part of {{masked}}.

### Proof of {{nested}}

(1) Level 2 is relative and payoff-monotone, so {{blind}}(1) removes every term common to all developers.

(2) Level 0 maximizes its reward by definition. Level 1 chooses rewards and constraints to maximize the developer's objective, which counts privately visible divergences through customers, liability and reputation. Under $(A_\tau)$, countering lowers the developer's payoff relative to its matched variant by a positive margin net of every private benefit, and the matched variant is in the support of selection, so under cumulative selection the countering developer's share relative to that variant tends to zero ({{load}}, with developers as carriers and the matched variant as the carrier otherwise identical). Without a matched variant the comparison is between different developers: one whose other advantages exceed the tax grows against unsafe rivals that lack them, for as long as those advantages last ({{lead}}).

(3) A constraint borne by one developer lowers its relative payoff, which (2) selects against. A constraint enforced on all as a condition of admission is a common term among compliers when its incidence is equal ({{blind}}(2)); when unequal, its cost differences are payoff differences among compliers only, and they re-sort the compliers. Each type $k$ of developer can comply at cost $c_k$ or evade against an expected sanction $\bar F_k$. Within a type, evasion does not grow relative to compliance iff $\bar F_k\ge c_k$, and is selected out if the inequality is strict ({{blind}}(3)). Across types, $\bar F_k\ge c_k$ for every type suffices but is not necessary: a type whose evasion pays within its type can still lose to another type's compliers. $\square$

*Numerics.*
- *The premise.* A unilateral safeguard that cuts the error rate from 0.20 to 0.01 at cost 0.01 is selected for (its share tends to one), which is the case $(A_\tau)$ excludes; a safeguard whose benefit is common is selected away [num: CI(e); AL(6.7)].
- *Several types* (compliance costs 0.1 and 0.2). With sanctions $(1,1)$ or $(0.15,0.25)$ compliance takes over; with $(1,0.15)$ it also takes over, although the second type's evasion pays within its type, because its evaders earn less than the first type's compliers; with $(1,0.05)$ evasion takes over [num: CI(h)].
- *Incidence.* With unequal compliance costs and sanction 1, the compliant share is 0.999999975 at $t=20$ and the compliers sort 0.88 to 0.12; with equal incidence their relative shares are preserved exactly [num: CI(i)].
- *The matched variant.* A safeguarded developer with baseline 2 and tax 0.1 outgrows an unsafe one with baseline 1 (its share tends to one), so a positive tax alone does not eliminate safeguarding; against its matched twin its share falls to $2\times10^{-9}$ by $t=200$ [num: CI(i)], and to $1.4\times10^{-87}$ by $t=2000$ in a second implementation [num: CI(j)]. With zero tax the shares are unchanged: the safeguard neither spreads nor is removed [num: CI(i)].
- *A tax paid from a lead.* A tax of 0.01 paid from an independent advantage of 0.02 survives; from an advantage of 0.005 it does not [num: CI(j); AL(6.7)].

*Interface.* What level 2 selects on is the developers' relative payoff, of which the trained systems' contribution to relative standing is part (S13).

### The formal part of {{masked}}

Let admission detect a system that carries the divergence with probability $p_{\rm det}$ and impose a sanction $F$; the variant that does not counter the divergence saves the tax $c_k$. The expected sanction is $\bar F_k=p_{\rm det}F$, so within a type evasion has payoff advantage $c_k-p_{\rm det}F$. With these payoffs fixed, {{nested}}(3) gives, by {{blind}}(3): the countering variant survives cumulative selection iff $p_{\rm det}F\ge c_k$. With a tax of 0.3, an expected sanction of 0.25 leaves the share of the non-countering variant at one, and one of 0.35 removes it [num: CI(b)].

Masking enters through $p_{\rm det}$. An evaluation that reads behaviour detects a divergence that changes under evaluation, or appears only in some contexts, only as often as it shows there, and that frequency is driven down by training against the evaluation, by context, or by the system's own recognition that it is being evaluated, each shown in constructed settings (EV6): deception that survives safety training (Hubinger et al. 2024), alignment faked when a model takes itself to be in training (Greenblatt, Denison et al. 2024), scheming in context (Meinke et al. 2024), misalignment learned in agentic training that chat-style safety training hides from chat evaluations (MacDiarmid et al. 2025), and covert behaviour that falls with evaluation awareness and rises when that awareness is removed (Schoen et al. 2025). For a fixed positive tax $c_k$ and a fixed finite sanction bound $F_{\max}>0$, detection falling toward zero implies $p_{\rm det}F<c_k$ for every admissible $F$ beyond a finite capability: evasion then has a payoff advantage. A developer's own tests face the same bound, so their failure to detect the divergence cannot establish that the countering it bought removed it. This is a limit of assurance from those tests. An intervention can nevertheless remove the divergence or lower its realized hazard, without these tests verifying that result.

If the tax also changes with capability $K$, the condition for every admissible sanction to leave evasion an advantage is $p_{\rm det}(K)F_{\max}<c_k(K)$. A positive lower bound on $c_k(K)$ makes detection tending to zero sufficient; positivity at each capability alone does not. For example, $p_{\rm det}=e^{-K}$, $F_{\max}=1$ and $c_k=e^{-2K}$ keep the maximum expected sanction above the tax at every $K>0$. Along a changing path, a divergent integral of the eventual payoff advantage gives elimination under cumulative selection, as in {{load}}; a positive sign alone does not guarantee it. In this example, $K(t)=\ln(t+2)$ gives the countering variant an advantage $1/(t+2)-1/(t+2)^2$ whose integral diverges: countering wins under ordinary replicator selection even though detection tends to zero. C9 and C12 therefore jointly decide the moving-path ceiling. An evaluation of propensity can show a tendency present; absence under that evaluation alone does not establish its absence elsewhere.

Hence the direction of the bound. A capability evaluation gives a lower bound, a capability shown, and sandbagging lowers it (EV6), so a rule that admits while shown capability stays below a threshold would be fooled. The bound on capability has to come from channels the behaviour controls less directly: compute, training data and elicitation by fine-tuning ({{alarm}}; S4). White-box evidence, weights and activations, is a second such channel, which an audit can read (Marks et al. 2025). Given a capability bound so obtained, the hazard the system can realize is bounded by a control protocol that is safe against a system of that capability that may be adversarial (Greenblatt, Shlegeris, Sachan and Roger 2024): the bound works through control, not through detection. Beyond the capability at which a protocol is shown safe, that protocol supplies no established assurance case. Neither the uncovered share nor the residual hazard follows from the failure of detection alone: even $p_{\rm det}=0$ can coexist with an independent intervention yielding $h_t=2^{-t-2}$, whose sum is finite and whose limiting survival is positive. Applying {{coverage}} requires both (coverage lags) and (residual floor) for this class, and (shared failure) to extend its conclusion to every actor. Absent another assurance case, the protection standard treats the class as uncovered and presumes those premises for the decision, as Section 6.4 states; E2 does not build the class and E3 holds its pace to what control covers [rec]. How $p_{\rm det}$ scales with capability at fixed auditor access is crux C12; audit games with planted hidden objectives (Marks et al. 2025) measure it.

### A worked instance: legible reasoning (Section 6.4)

If legible reasoning lets correctors catch what outputs hide (Korbak et al. 2025; Baker et al. 2025) and opaque reasoning buys capability, the cost of legibility falls on the developer and the benefit mostly on everyone, so legibility is selected away unless it is an admission condition for every eligible system with detectable evasion ({{nested}}(3)), and it has to be of roughly equal incidence across architectures if it is not to re-sort them. If the capability gap at fixed compute is negative, legibility pays privately and spreads without coordination; at zero it is neutral under selection, spreading only by the choice of developers that weigh the common term. At the current frontier reinforcement learning has not materially lowered monitorability, and a smaller model at higher reasoning effort can match capability with higher monitorability at higher inference cost (Guan et al. 2025; EV6): a positive tax, paid in compute, so the instance is conditional; its sign at fixed compute is crux C9a.

### Relative selection inside training (Section 6.4)

In a Bradley–Terry model the probability that response $y_1$ is preferred to $y_2$ is $\sigma(R(y_1)-R(y_2))$. Adding the same harm to third parties to both responses leaves it unchanged: 0.7110 with shared harm 0 and with shared harm $-5$ [num: AL(BT)]. If abstaining is among the responses compared and the rater sees the harm, complying (quality 1.3, harm $-5$) is preferred to abstaining (0, 0) with probability 0.024 [num: AL(BT)]. Since only differences enter, a reward fitted to comparisons is identified up to an additive constant for each context, and whether to act at all is not in it.

### The bounded optimizer (Section 6.4)

**{{!Remark:boundedopt}} (Bounded objectives, cheap resources) [thm].** *An optimizer that maximizes $1-e^{-R}-cR$ over the resources $R$ it acquires, at unit cost $c<1$, acquires $R^\ast=\ln(1/c)$, without bound as $c\to0$; a satisficer that stops at success probability $1-\varepsilon_s$ acquires $\ln(1/\varepsilon_s)$, whatever $c$.* The first-order condition is $e^{-R}=c$. With $c=0.1$, 0.01, 0.001 and $10^{-6}$ the optimizer acquires 2.30, 4.61, 6.91 and 13.8, while the satisficer with $\varepsilon_s=0.05$ stays at 3.0 [num: AL(bounded)]. In the contest the same falling cost is harmless to a mind that counts the shared loss: at the symmetric equilibrium of {{boundary}}(1) with $n=4$, $r=1$, $\Omega=1$ and a shared loss of 0.3 per unit, intensity under $\theta=-1$ rises from 0.25 to 2.5, 25 and 250 as the private cost falls from 1 to 0.1, 0.01 and 0.001; under $\theta=-0.5$ it reaches 1.449, and under $\theta=0$ it rises only from 0.144 to 0.623, since any $\theta>-1$ leaves the term $(1+\theta)\ell$ in the denominator [num: AL(contest)]. A bounded goal plays the part of a bounded prize, not of satiation: what bounds acquisition is a term that counts its cost to others, the analogue of $\theta>-1$, or a rule that stops, the analogue of satiation ({{enough}}).

### Correction that people cannot understand (Section 6.5)

**{{!Proposition:opaquecorr}} (Correction that people cannot understand) [thm].** A corrector that people cannot follow lowers residual risk wherever it blocks more than it adds, and keeps survival bounded away from zero only if its blocking becomes complete. *Let a correction architecture block a share $c_{b,t}$ of failures at base hazard $h_0\in(0,1)$ and add a residual hazard $h_{c,t}$ of its own in period $t$, with residual hazard $h_0(1-c_{b,t})+h_{c,t}<1$. It lowers residual risk in period $t$ iff $h_0c_{b,t}>h_{c,t}$, and keeps survival bounded away from zero iff $\sum_t\big[h_0(1-c_{b,t})+h_{c,t}\big]<\infty$, that is, iff $\sum_t(1-c_{b,t})$ and $\sum_th_{c,t}$ are both finite; a fixed $c_b<1$ never does.*

*Scope.* Summable residual risk is what keeps survival bounded away from zero ({{nonsum}}). Whether the loss of legibility is a loss of O2 is a separate question, answered by overrulability (Section 6.5).

*Proof.* The residual hazard in period $t$ is $h_0(1-c_{b,t})+h_{c,t}$; compare it with $h_0$. Survival $\prod_t(1-h_t)$ with every $h_t<1$ is bounded away from zero iff $\sum_th_t<\infty$ ({{nonsum}}); both terms are non-negative and $h_0>0$, so the sum is finite iff $\sum_t(1-c_{b,t})$ and $\sum_th_{c,t}$ are, which no fixed $c_b<1$ gives. $\square$

*Example.* With $h_0=0.1$ a period, $c_{b,t}=1-2^{-(t+1)}$ and $h_{c,t}=2^{-(t+2)}$ from $t=0$, survival is 0.5101 at period 200 and at period 20,000, against $7.1\times10^{-10}$ by period 200 without the corrector [num: SM(11)]. A second summable example, $c_{b,t}=1-2^{-t}$ from $t=1$, gives 0.6888 at both horizons [num: SM(11)]. The example printed in V6.1, a fixed $c_b=0.99$ with the same $h_{c,t}$, lowers risk but is not summable: survival to period 200 is 0.473 counting from $t=0$ (0.630 counting from $t=1$, the figure V6.1 printed) and of order $10^{-9}$ by period 20,000 [num: SM(11); AL(6.8)]. V6.1's claim that it made residual risk summable is withdrawn.

### Proof of {{topology}}

Each layer covers $\mathsf m$ times the attention of the layer above, so $k=\ln(\mathsf A/\mathsf B)/\ln\mathsf m$ layers are needed, where $\mathsf A$ is the attention the overseen conduct requires and $\mathsf B$ the attention of the top layer. A serial chain catches a problem only if every link succeeds: $p_\ell^k=(\mathsf A/\mathsf B)^{\ln p_\ell/\ln\mathsf m}$. Independent checks across the gap all fail with probability $(1-p_\ell^k)^{k_c}$; a common cause independent of them that defeats all with probability $p_{\rm cc}$ multiplies the catch rate by $1-p_{\rm cc}$. Layered redundancy needs every layer to succeed, each with probability $1-(1-p_\ell)^{k_c}$. A blind spot common to every check, covering a share $\rho_{\rm cc}$ of hazards, is missed whatever the topology, so for hazards that strike at random the catch rate is at most $1-\rho_{\rm cc}$, and an adversary that knows the blind spot and places every attempt in it is never caught. $\square$ (V6.1 wrote the one-layer reliability as $f$, the checks per layer as $m$ and the common-cause probability as $q_{\rm cc}$; $f$, $m$ and $q$ are table symbols.)

*Numerics* ($p_\ell=0.9$, $k=6$). The serial chain catches 0.531; six checks across the gap, 36 layer-checks, catch 0.989, or 0.940 under a common cause with $p_{\rm cc}=0.05$; layered redundancy catches 0.941 with two checks per layer (12 layer-checks) and 0.994 with three (18), so per unit of budget layered redundancy is the better design [num: SM(13); AL(6.9)]. Six independent checks of catch probability 0.9 with a blind spot common to all, covering a share $\rho_{\rm cc}$ of hazards, catch only $(1-\rho_{\rm cc})(1-0.1^6)$ of hazards that strike at random, however the checks are funded or appointed: 1.000, 0.950 and 0.700 at $\rho_{\rm cc}=0$, 0.05 and 0.3 [num: SM(7)]. Against an attacker that strikes in the blind spot when it knows it and at random otherwise, with $\rho_{\rm cc}=0.05$, catch rates are 0.950, 0.475, 0.095 and 0 as the probability that it knows rises through 0, 0.5, 0.9 and 1 [num: AL(6.9)]. Common causes include shared training sources, shared models and shared funders; evaluators' preference for their own lineage's outputs (Panickssery et al. 2024); and collusion between untrusted monitors and the policy they monitor, through self-recognition or coordination signals, which the control literature treats as a design variable (Greenblatt, Shlegeris, Sachan and Roger 2024; Gardner-Challis et al. 2026). Systems of one lineage serving rival principals can also coordinate around them, as pricing algorithms learn to in simulated markets (Calvano et al. 2020; Fish, Gonczarowski and Shorrer 2024). Whether prover–estimator debate (Brown-Cohen et al. 2025) scales is part of crux C3a.

### Prediction horizons under chaos (Section 6.5)

**{{!Remark:lyapunov}} (Prediction horizons under chaos) [thm].** *In a system with largest Lyapunov exponent $\lambda_L>0$, an initial error $\epsilon_{\rm obs}$ reaches a tolerance $\Delta_{\rm tol}$ after about $\lambda_L^{-1}\ln(\Delta_{\rm tol}/\epsilon_{\rm obs})$, so a gain in precision by a factor $g_p$ extends the horizon only by $\lambda_L^{-1}\ln g_p$.* In the logistic map at full chaos, errors of $10^{-3}$ and $10^{-12}$ reach 0.1 after 7.4 and 37.3 steps on average [num: AL(6.10)]. This limits safety cases that predict trajectories, such as "the margin we bought will suffice", not certification of invariants, reachable sets or probabilistic bounds. That AI raises the sensitivity of the systems it acts in is a conjecture [conj].
