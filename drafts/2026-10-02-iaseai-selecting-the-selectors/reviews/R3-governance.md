## 1. Summary

The paper names and formalises a "meta-attractor": a practice whose success in one selection domain converts into rule support in other domains (benchmarks, evaluations, standards, procurement defaults, curricula, training data), so that it becomes self-maintaining even though it would lose under neutral rules (c_i > 0), survives replacement of its carriers, and, once it reaches the evaluation domain, masks the harm it causes. The model couples replicator dynamics for the share x_i of a practice in each of n domains with a linear stock r_i of rule support fed by a conversion matrix B. The results: stable configurations are the vertex states E_S whose captured set S is internally sustaining and externally closed (inbound normalized conversion s_i(S) above/below one); capture spreads through the closure cl(S) (Cascade), giving "no local certificate", re-absorption of single-domain reforms, and persistence after the origin domain is lost; the dual pruning operation core(C) yields an unravelling theorem and a keystone criterion; capture of evaluation masks a depleting shared condition V so that correction becomes a race between the cascade into E and the decline of V; a remark adds dependence rising with degradation. Six-domain numerics illustrate cascade, firewalls, closure, keystone reform and hysteresis. Section 5 argues general-purpose AI raises coupling, evaluator dependence/masking and adoption speed; Section 6 lists tests and falsifiers; Section 7 gives seven design principles; Section 8 answers objections.

- Quality: 6/10 (mathematics correct and numerics reproducible, but results are elementary and there is no empirical content; several claims in the text outrun what is proven).
- Clarity: 7/10 (well written and well organised; notation collisions and one misuse of the paper's own technical term).
- Originality: 6/10 (the field-level formalisation and the lineage/masking bridge are new; the conceptual claims have strong unacknowledged antecedents).
- Significance: 6/10 (useful vocabulary and two sharp policy-legible results; limited immediate actionability because the key quantity is not identifiable as defined).
- Overall recommendation: BORDERLINE. With the three-day revisions listed in Part 8 (positioning paragraph, identification caveat, keystone-term fix, three citation fixes, documented share cap) I would move to weak accept.
- Confidence: 4/5. I checked every proof by hand, re-implemented the numerics from Appendix B, and verified 20+ citations against sources.

## 3. Strengths

S1. The decomposition in Section 3 and Table 1 (concentration, capture, field-level capture, damage, collapse, closure as five separate conditions, none implying the next) is genuinely clarifying and is the kind of thing regulators currently lack vocabulary for. "Concentration is not capture" is a point the CMA/FTC foundation-model work has struggled to articulate.

S2. The core formal idea is right and crisp: stability is a property of a set (internally sustaining and externally closed), so a domain's safety depends on its inbound conversion from the captured set rather than its own incentives. Corollary 1 (no local certificate), Corollary 2 (re-absorption) and Corollary 4 (keystones) are correct, short, and translate directly into policy-relevant statements. I verified all proofs; the Jacobian block-triangularity argument, the Metzler/M-matrix step in Prop. 1(ii), the comparison argument in Prop. 2, the monotone squeeze in Prop. 3 and the V_eff computation in Prop. 4 are all sound.

S3. Proposition 4 and Remark 4 (the race between cascade into E and decline of V, with institutional decision delay tau_d that no technical speed-up shortens) is the paper's most original bridge: it ties the institutional-capture literature to the LLM-as-judge literature via beta and lineage overlap, and it yields a concrete condition (small inbound conversion into E and beta near 0) for when AI-assisted oversight helps.

S4. Reproducibility: I re-implemented the model from Appendix B alone and reproduced every reported number: capture of P at t=47.9 (paper ~48), E and I at 60.2 (~61), S at 77.2 (~78), F at 124.5 (~125); V reaching the 0.3 floor at t=209.9 (~210); alarm with firewalled selectors at 74.7 (~75); re-absorption of standards at 41.6 (~42); hysteresis appearing above theta=1 and persisting down to theta=0.3 for phi=0.1, and surviving theta=0 for phi=0.2. This is better than most governance papers with numerics.

S5. Intellectual honesty: "hypotheses about magnitudes, not established findings"; "possibility results about a stated model"; the falsifier paragraph; the reflexive clause that the framework must not treat objections as evidence of capture. The four-part evidentiary burden for claims about particular actors (Section 3.4) is a useful discipline.

S6. The "Obedience is not field alignment" paragraph states clearly why per-system alignment does not bound field-level capture, and locates the failure in conversion rather than conflict, which is a real refinement of the multi-principal framing.

## 4. Weaknesses (most serious first)

**W1 (MAJOR) — Positioning against intent-free and institution-shaping capture literatures is missing, and the novelty claim is overstated accordingly.** The paper says its notion is "broader than regulatory capture" and that its novelty is "treating recursive selection as a property of a field". But: (a) Carpenter and Moss's own definition requires "intent and action of the industry", and Kwak's chapter in that very volume ("Cultural Capture and the Financial Crisis") introduces intent-free capture via shared worldviews and status — exactly the "no manipulation required" mechanism of Section 3.2; (b) Hanson and Yosifon's "deep capture" (U. Penn. L. Rev. 2003) is capture of the institutions that shape how problems are conceptualised — the paper's formation domain and epistemic closure; (c) Hellman, Jones and Kaufmann's "state capture" (2000) distinguishes shaping the formation of rules from influencing their implementation — the paper's rule-support stock; (d) Acemoglu and Robinson 2008, which the paper cites only for de facto offsets, also contains the invariance result and "captured democracy", i.e. persistence of outcomes across changes in who holds power — the paper's "persistence across carriers" has a direct antecedent there; (e) Büthe and Mattli (The New Global Rulers, 2011) is the standard account of how firm expertise converts into standard-setting power at ISO/IEC — directly the standards row of Table 2; (f) Clark and Hadfield's regulatory markets (2019) are the obvious comparator for "firewall the selectors" and explicitly flag capture of private regulators; (g) the paper uses "field" throughout without citing field theory (Bourdieu; Fligstein and McAdam 2012) or institutional isomorphism (DiMaggio and Powell 1983), which already treat cross-organisational convergence of practice as a field property. The genuinely new part is the dynamical formalisation and the certification/keystone results; the paper should claim that and no more.

**W2 (MAJOR) — The formal results are elementary and their antecedents are not named.** cl(S) and core(C) are weighted bootstrap percolation and k-core pruning (Chalupa, Leath and Reich 1979; Seidman 1983; Dorogovtsev et al. 2006); the vertex-equilibrium structure with monotone comparative statics is the logic of supermodular games (Topkis; Milgrom and Roberts 1990). The authors concede "standard tools", but the Statement of Contributions still speaks of a "complete characterization of stable capture configurations" when Prop. 1 settles stability only at vertices and under strict inequalities (the "if and only if" in Prop. 1(iii) ignores the boundary case). Calling these "theorems" and "corollaries" invites a standard a reader of the proofs will find unmet. Reframe as propositions whose value is interpretive, cite the percolation/supermodularity antecedents, and spend the saved space on W3.

**W3 (MAJOR) — The central quantity is not identifiable as defined, so "measure conversion" and the "inbound over local" test are not yet operational.** R_ij = a_i b_ij / (c_i d_i) contains c_i, the practice's net relative cost "under neutral rules" — a counterfactual that no indicator in Table 2 observes. Table 2 lists shares and flows (funding shares, personnel movement, lineage overlap); R_ij is a ratio of rates. No mapping from indicators to R_ij, or even to its ordering, is proposed. The paper's own objection paragraph ("Is the winning practice not simply better?") concedes that the c_i > 0 case is exactly what must be established empirically and defers to the convertibility experiment, which is proposed but not run. Either supply an identification strategy (cross-jurisdiction variation in conversion channels with fixed reward; LLM-agent experiments with conversion switched on/off; dose-response in relapse rates after single-domain reforms) or reframe the principle as "map and monitor conversion channels" (ordinal), with the threshold claim kept qualitative.

**W4 (MAJOR) — Internal inconsistencies between the text and the model.** (i) Section 7: "Evaluation and formation are keystones of epistemic closure (Proposition 4)". "Keystone" is defined in Cor. 4 as a domain whose removal prunes the core; in the paper's own numerics development is the keystone and E and F are not (removing E leaves inbound into the others unchanged above one). Prop. 4 says nothing about keystones. This conflates two distinct roles the paper otherwise keeps apart: the keystone you reform (D, via liability) and the selectors you firewall (E, F, to keep the alarm). (ii) Section 5: "Formation is the slowest domain, and Corollary 2 explains why reforms elsewhere are re-absorbed while it remains captured." Nothing in the paper demonstrates this; with phi = 0.1 formation contributes 0.1 to any inbound sum, and the slowness of F (small kappa, epsilon) affects timing, not thresholds, as Section 5 itself says. (iii) The abstract's "no domain-by-domain audit can certify a field" overstates Cor. 1: an audit of domain i's inbound dependencies does certify i once the captured set is known; what fails is auditing internal incentives alone. The body says this correctly ("each see one row, so assessment must be joint"); align the abstract with it. (iv) The numerical keystone reform raises c_D rather than holding x_D = 0 as Prop. 3 assumes; it works because D becomes subcritical and unravels first, but that step is not covered by the stated proposition. Add the one-line generalisation.

**W5 (MAJOR) — All-or-nothing capture and the absorbing boundary are artefacts the paper should own.** Prop. 1(ii) rules out any stable mixed state. Real evaluation ecosystems are mixed (developer-affiliated, AISI, non-profit, academic), so the model's prediction of polarisation per domain is a consequence of linear replicator payoffs, not a finding. Frequency-dependent costs or heterogeneity within a domain would produce interior equilibria and change the hysteresis picture. Relatedly, the replicator boundary x = 1 is absorbing: Prop. 3's hypothesis "every share in (0,1)" is doing real work, because a domain in which the alternative is literally extinct cannot be unwound by any parameter reform. Appendix B documents only a floor of 1e-4 ("persistent entry"); the symmetric assumption (persistent dissent, shares bounded below one) is not stated, and the numerics are sensitive to it. In my reproduction, V bottomed at 0.456 (matching the paper's 0.46) when shares were allowed to saturate to one in floating point, but at 0.529 with a 1e-4 cap, because recovery time after the keystone reform depends on how deep the logit of x_D has gone. This is substantively interesting (the longer capture lasts, the slower the recovery even after the right reform, independently of the rule stock) and should be stated as a result and documented in Appendix B, not left implicit in the code.

**W6 (MAJOR) — The design principles map onto existing instruments whose track record the paper does not engage, so a regulator cannot tell what is new.** Levy-funded, arm's-length evaluation is the PDUFA user-fee model, which Carpenter (cited here for capture theory) and the Institute of Medicine argue made the FDA a case where "industry sets the terms of the agenda" and which Carpenter et al. (NEJM 2008) linked to deadline-driven safety problems — the paper's own dependence remark predicts exactly this failure mode, so the omission is conspicuous. Independence of evaluators from developers already exists as EU AI Act Art. 31 (notified bodies independent of providers, personnel not involved in design or development, no conflicting consultancy) and as auditor-independence law after Sarbanes-Oxley. Joint cross-regulator assessment exists as the UK DRCF. Sunset clauses are routine. Mixed-lineage judge panels exist as PoLL (Verga et al. 2024) and are recommended by Kim et al. 2025 themselves. What the paper actually adds — and should say in a short "what a regulator would do differently" paragraph — is: (a) the independence criterion is field-level, i.e. inbound conversion from all captured domains including formation and the AI-judge channel, so an evaluator funded by philanthropy but staffed by people formed on incumbent tooling and using same-lineage judges is still captured; (b) a provenance test for evaluators (training lineage, data, funding, access) with the quantitative hook beta; (c) the keystone-timing argument (liability on development works only while D is still a keystone, before mutual support among captured domains closes the window); (d) overshoot of reform past the conditions under which capture arose. Those four are real contributions; "answerable closures" and "apply the analysis to the regime" are sensible but are not derived from the model and should be marked as such.

**W7 (MAJOR, borderline minor) — Geopolitics ("power's two roles", dependence and contraction, symmetric application).** The form is cautious and the four-part evidentiary burden is valuable. But: (a) the contraction claim ("with less surplus to share, a relative position separates from everyone's absolute conditions") is asserted in words and in Remark 5 is built in by assumption (b^1 > 0), as the authors admit; nothing is derived; (b) the counter-dynamics that the IR literature treats as central — exit and diversification away from hubs (the backlash to weaponized interdependence; "de-risking"), hegemonic provision of public goods (Kindleberger) — appear only as the generic phrase "reciprocal dependence or effective correction"; (c) the actor-free ontology ("what persists is a logic of success, not an identity") sits uneasily with an application to states, where carriers obviously matter; the paper never says what a "practice" is at state level (standards-first market access? compute chokepoint strategy?), so the symmetric-application claim cannot be tested on any case; (d) the sentence "a change in who holds the strongest position is not evidence that the mechanism has changed" reads as a hedge against an unstated thesis about hegemonic transition. Either give one neutral worked example scored on the four criteria for two different dominant orders, or cut the paragraph to the evidentiary burden plus Remark 5. Also, Farrell and Newman's mechanism is coercion via panopticon and chokepoint effects conditional on network topology and jurisdiction; "the power to set terms" (Section 5) is broader than what they show.

**W8 (MINOR, citation)** — Zheng et al. 2023 do not establish that LLM judges favour their own outputs; they write that "due to limited data and small differences, our study cannot determine whether the models exhibit a self-enhancement bias". Section 2 should cite only Panickssery et al. for that claim (Section 5's use of Zheng for "LLMs serve as judges" is fine).

**W9 (MINOR, citation)** — Khan 2019 and Narechania and Sitaraman 2024 are cited for "structural separation between developers and the evaluations that license them". Khan is about platforms and commerce; Narechania and Sitaraman propose structural separations between layers of the AI stack (e.g. GPU producers not owning higher layers) and say nothing about evaluators. Cite them for the instrument and say the application is the authors' own.

**W10 (MINOR, citation)** — Shumailov et al. 2024 and Taori and Hashimoto 2023 are cited for "future training data are increasingly produced by deployed models". Both show consequences of model-generated training data and frame its prevalence as prospective ("What will happen to GPT-n once LLMs contribute much of the language found online?"; "as model outputs begin to replace human annotations"); neither measures the trend. Soften to "if/as", or cite data-composition evidence. Kleinberg and Raghavan 2021 is a welfare result (monoculture can lower aggregate decision quality even when the shared algorithm is individually more accurate), not a homogenisation result; Bommasani et al. 2022 found homogenisation reliably from shared training data but mixed results for foundation models depending on adaptation. Attribute precisely.

**W11 (MINOR)** — Notation collisions hurt an otherwise clear paper: S is both the standards domain and the captured set (E_S); E is both the evaluation domain and the equilibrium symbol; I is both the information domain and the index set in the proof of Prop. 1(ii); "conversion" also has an established and different meaning in institutional-change theory (Thelen). Table 1's column header "Does not follow from" does not fit the field-level row ("invisible to" is meant).

**W12 (MINOR)** — The "Agents" anecdote (one multi-session software project "that we observed") is n = 1, unverifiable, loosely mapped to r and d, and potentially traceable to the authors. Move to an appendix with enough detail to be checkable, or cut.

## 6. "Could this paper, in its current version, be an important paper that advances alignment?"

Not yet.

Why not: (1) The alignment-relevant thesis — that aligning each system to its principal does not bound field-level capture — is already present in Critch and Krueger, Gabriel, Hammond et al. and Kulveit et al.; the paper's refinement (locating the failure in conversion rather than conflict) is real but incremental. (2) The paper's genuinely new alignment hook is the claim that evaluator lineage overlap is a parameter (beta) of institutional closure, with the design condition "no evaluator drawn solely from the evaluated lineage". In the current version this is a sentence and a symbol, not a result: nothing is measured, no protocol is specified, and the convertibility experiment is proposed, not run. (3) The formal results are too elementary to carry importance on their own, and the key quantity is not identifiable as defined (W3). (4) Without positioning (W1), the alignment community will read the conceptual part as a restatement of cultural/deep capture plus gradual disempowerment.

What it would take: one empirical artefact that only this framework asks for — for example, a measured conversion map for the evaluation domain (funding, compute and access shares from evaluated firms; personnel flows; judge-model lineage overlap and error correlation among the judges actually used by AISIs, METR-type evaluators and public leaderboards), showing that evaluator independence judged by inbound conversion differs from independence judged by funding alone; or an LLM-agent experiment implementing the convertibility test with conversion switched on and off, showing persistence and hysteresis only when winners shape the next round's criteria. Plus: an explicit identification strategy for R_ij or an honest ordinal reframing; a concrete evaluator-provenance protocol compared against EU AI Act Art. 31 and the PDUFA record; and the positioning fixes. With those, this could be an important paper at the alignment–governance interface, because it would give the first quantitative handle on "who evaluates the evaluators" that treats AI judges and human institutions in one frame.

## 7. Questions for the authors

- Q1. How would you estimate c_i (the net relative cost under neutral rules) for any row of Table 2, even ordinally? If you cannot, what exactly does "measure conversion" ask a regulator to measure?
- Q2. In your numerics development is the only keystone and E, F are not. What do you mean by "Evaluation and formation are keystones of epistemic closure (Proposition 4)"?
- Q3. What cap, if any, does your code place on shares near one? Prop. 3 requires shares strictly below one; recovery time and V_min depend on this choice (I obtain 0.46 or 0.53 depending on it). Is "persistent dissent" an assumption you want to make explicit?
- Q4. How does the model handle a domain that is persistently mixed (e.g. an evaluation ecosystem with developer-affiliated and independent evaluators coexisting)? Prop. 1(ii) says this cannot be stable; is that a prediction you endorse?
- Q5. PDUFA is a levy-funded, arm's-length-by-design evaluator that many scholars, including Carpenter, regard as a capture case. Does your dependence remark predict this, and if so why does Section 7 recommend the instrument without the lesson?
- Q6. At the state level, what is the "practice" whose share and rule support you would track, and how would you score two different dominant orders symmetrically on your four criteria?
- Q7. Which of the design principles would change what the EU AI Office, a national AI Safety Institute, or the UK DRCF does today, given that evaluator independence (Art. 31), joint assessment (DRCF) and sunset review already exist?
- Q8. Is the "Agents" case drawn from the authors' own project, and can it be documented in a way a reader could check?

Longer-term:

- L2. Build a first empirical conversion map for the evaluation domain (funding/compute/access shares, personnel flows, judge-lineage overlap and error correlation across the evaluators in use), and show where field-level independence diverges from funding-based independence.
