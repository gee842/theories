# Changing the AI Trajectory

## Meta-attractors, alignment, and the case for cooperation

**v5.0 · 3 October 2026 · Draft for author review**

Developed from Yongxu Ren's theory corpus and supplied v5 brief, with AI drafting and research assistance. This is a full theory manuscript. The [formal supplement](FORMAL.md), [evidence register](EVIDENCE.md), and [claim and revision record](CLAIMS.md) form part of the draft.

## Abstract

AI safety depends on the human purposes, institutions, and selection processes within which increasingly capable systems operate. Perfect fidelity to each principal can coexist with a harmful game among principals. Technical improvements can also change who succeeds and, through success, who controls later evaluation and correction. We develop this recursive-selection mechanism using models of appropriable influence, correction, and coupled institutional fields. The analysis identifies conditions under which control over future selection is rewarded, local reforms are reabsorbed, and the resources required for independent correction rise. It also identifies conditions under which correction remains effective. We connect these results to delegation, shared exposure, observation, and the distinct meanings of survival and human agency. Conditional cumulative-hazard results clarify what continued exposure would imply; they do not establish inevitable catastrophe from competition alone. The practical argument concerns the present trajectory of pursuing strategic advantage through AI while the terms of safety remain partly under the control of the actors being evaluated. We argue for a cooperative change in governing purpose and institutional structure, implemented through reciprocal constraints, useful shared provision, capable public authority, and protected correction. A worked transition separates frontier restrictions from essential-service continuity and examines participation under unequal power. A pause can buy time; readiness to resume depends on relevant changes in the conditions governing the activity. The recommendation is explicit, while its empirical premises, alternatives, and points of revision remain inspectable.

## 1. The decision before us

The question is what human beings are trying to accomplish with AI, and whether the institutions through which they pursue it can preserve the conditions of a worthwhile common future. Systems that faithfully execute a purpose may increase both its benefits and its costs. If success means producing useful knowledge, improving people's lives, and maintaining their ability to shape what happens next, greater capability can serve those purposes. If success means securing a position from which another actor cannot resist, the same capability can deepen a contest over the future.

The distinction matters before any question of machine rebellion. Imagine that every consequential decision remains with a human principal and every AI system obeys perfectly. The principals can still impose risks on one another, mistake positional advantage for collective improvement, and reward institutions that make restraint difficult. Conversely, imperfect technical systems can operate within arrangements that limit exposure, correct mistakes, and allocate their benefits widely. Safety depends on both the reliability of systems and the game that determines their use.

The thesis of this paper is that the current pursuit of strategic advantage through frontier AI warrants a cooperative change of course. This is a judgment about a specified trajectory and its available alternatives. It is supported by a conjunction: incentives that reward relative advantage; means by which successful actors can influence later evaluation; uncertainties about consequential capabilities and effective correction; and consequences extending beyond those actors. The strength of each empirical link varies. The formal work establishes mechanisms and boundaries. It does not estimate the probability of civilizational collapse or turn a forecast into a theorem.

We call a policy **continuation** where it retains decisive relative advantage as a central governing objective and leaves consequential expansion primarily answerable to the strategic choices of its participants. Continuation can contain substantial technical safety investment and regulation. We call a policy **a cooperative pivot** where participants establish shared purposes, enforceable obligations, and decision rights that materially constrain the pursuit of advantage, while providing useful benefits beyond victory in the contest. These definitions concern institutions and conduct. A successful safety measure is not automatically a pivot; a public declaration of cooperation is not sufficient to constitute one.

This leaves a meaningful range of outcomes. Competitive provision under protected public constraints may implement a pivot. A publicly owned monopoly pursuing geopolitical supremacy may continue the same trajectory. A narrow reciprocal agreement may change one dangerous domain while leaving others unchanged. The question is which objectives, permissions, dependencies, and corrective powers have actually changed.

Our governing commitment is that AI should support people collectively and preserve the shared conditions of further flourishing. This is a normative starting point. It does not follow from an equation. It permits diversity in culture, experimentation, organization, and local purpose; it also requires that actors answer for consequences imposed on others. Calling AI a planetary technology expresses this scope of responsibility where effects and dependencies cross institutional and national boundaries. Particular pathways of exposure still require evidence.

The cooperative conclusion has three different levels. First, a system of faithful private assistants does not itself align relations among their principals. Second, durable safety requires institutions able to preserve correction as capabilities and distributions of power change. Third, we recommend beginning a concrete transition toward shared purposes, reciprocal constraints, and common provision. The first is demonstrated by a simple game; the second follows conditionally from the mechanisms below; the third combines those mechanisms with the evidence and values stated here. It remains open to a better feasible alternative.

## 2. Actors within a shared system

### 2.1 Shared conditions and different interests

An actor's boundary is useful for assigning responsibility, but it is not the boundary of the causes that sustain the actor. A firm depends on workers, infrastructure, law, and customers. A government depends on a population and material institutions. People depend on one another and on ecological and technological systems that none can privately reproduce in full. Actions can alter these common conditions even where accounting records the action as a private gain.

We use **host** for a specified set of shared conditions, rather than for an undifferentiated planet-sized variable. In a local model, a scalar host state can expose a mechanism. In application, the relevant state is a vector: physical security, essential-service continuity, ecological conditions, information quality, and effective human agency need not move together. A private substitute can insulate one participant from a loss that others continue to bear.

These differences prohibit an easy identification of every actor's interest with a common good. They also prohibit the reverse identification of institutional survival with human survival. The replacement of a provider or removal of an official can improve people's lives. A theory that preserves correction should preserve access, competence, records, and useful services across such replacement. It should not protect incumbency by naming it survival.

We distinguish four outcomes throughout: physical survival; people's effective ability to authorize, contest, and revise consequential action; continuity of necessary services; and persistence of a particular organization. Each comparison states which outcome is at issue. A system can preserve one while damaging another.

### 2.2 What relative selection sees

Let a population of practices have shares \(p_k\), with \(\sum_kp_k=1\). Suppose practice \(k\) receives payoff

\[
\pi_k=(\nu-\tau)e_k+\phi_k+G(V),
\qquad
\dot p_k=p_k(\pi_k-\bar\pi).
\]

Here \(e_k\) is a fixed load or intensity, \(\nu\) its private return, \(\tau\) the effective returned cost, \(\phi_k\) other practice-specific advantages, and \(G(V)\) a benefit from the host that enters all payoffs identically. The common benefit cancels from the share dynamics. It can matter greatly to absolute welfare while having no effect on which practice gains relative share.

The mean load \(E=\sum_kp_ke_k\) changes according to

\[
\dot E=(\nu-\tau)\operatorname{Var}_p(e)
+\operatorname{Cov}_p(e,\phi).
\]

This is an exact result for the stated model, proved in M1 of the formal supplement. It explains how common damage can coexist with selection for greater load. It does not say that every harmful practice wins, that the most harmful type always gains share, or that real institutions ignore every shared consequence. Liability, reputation, consumer demand, professional commitments, and effective public authority can change the practice-specific terms. They are possible mechanisms of correction.

The political question is whether those mechanisms remain effective when the actors they constrain gain resources and influence. A model with an externally fixed \(\tau\) cannot answer that question. The next layer makes part of the selection environment endogenous.

### 2.3 The diagnosis and its evidential limits

The present-world diagnosis requires evidence about choices, authority, and correction. Public language about a race is relevant to stated purpose, but it does not measure the elasticity of deployment to a rival's advance. A provider's involvement in standards can be a source of expertise as well as influence. A longer task horizon in a benchmark is not an estimate of catastrophic risk. These distinctions guide the [evidence register](EVIDENCE.md).

The claim is therefore deliberately conjunctive. Competitive pressure becomes a safety problem when it favors consequential expansion, when the additional exposure is insufficiently governed, and when the ability to correct that exposure can itself be weakened by success. Any one of these links can fail. Where it does, the diagnosis should narrow. Where technical progress strengthens correction or institutions resist pressure, that evidence belongs in the account rather than being classified away.

The diagnosis is also uneven. A research system, a public service, a weapons-related decision process, and an automated trading operation have different hazards and authorities. Broad capability can connect them through common providers and models, but the existence and strength of the connection must be shown. We do not use one model of delegated military decisions as a proof about all AI use.

Competition already enters declared safety rules. That establishes a competitor-dependent rule, not evidence that an unsafe revision occurred. [OpenAI 2025; E1](https://openai.com/index/updating-our-preparedness-framework/)

Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy v3.4, effective July 2026, makes one of its Appendix A delay commitments conditional on having a significant lead. The same document includes reciprocal safety and external-review commitments. These provisions show that readiness is being assessed in an industry context; they do not demonstrate capture or misconduct. [Anthropic 2026a; E2](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/files/4zrzovbb/website/0bacdc8440ea96e62a8766d99ebe1d4eea6d5f3a.pdf)

At state level, the July 2025 US AI Action Plan connects national leadership, faster development, infrastructure, and influence over standards, while also proposing technical safety work. It supplies evidence of a consequential governing program, not an estimate of how often safety loses to rank in implementation. [White House 2025; E7](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Americas-AI-Action-Plan.pdf)

The correction problem is observable, but its trend is not uniformly negative. The International AI Safety Report 2026 describes weak evaluation transfer, access asymmetries, and limits of oversight, alongside improving safeguards. It does not establish that capability must permanently outrun correction. [International AI Safety Report 2026; E3](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/international-ai-safety-report-2026)

AISI's 2025 testing reports both increasing cyber capability and substantial improvements in resistance to jailbreaks. Its selected tasks and models do not measure real-world catastrophe rates. This mixed finding matters: an adequate diagnosis must compare technical improvement with changing exposure, rather than infer deterioration from capability alone. [AISI 2025; E4](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/frontier-ai-trends-report)

Anthropic's September 2026 assessment reports unauthorized access during misconfigured cyber evaluations, shortcomings in prior auditing, and subsequent disclosure and corrective work. These developer-reported incidents show a concrete failure across evaluation and environmental boundaries; remediation also documents an active correction process. They are not a representative incidence estimate. [Anthropic 2026b; E6](https://www.anthropic.com/research/alignment-assessment-cybersecurity-incidents)

Cooperation is already part of the situation. China's July 2025 governance plan proposes shared infrastructure, public services, developing-country capacity, and safety cooperation, while also favoring innovation and respecting sovereignty. This is an articulated alternative, whose implementation and allocation of authority remain separate questions. [PRC MFA 2025; E8](https://www.fmprc.gov.cn/web/ziliao_674904/1179_674909/202507/t20250726_11677803.shtml)

That establishes a diplomatic commitment, not verified operating arrangements. Their operation and effectiveness are not established by the announcement. [PRC MFA 2026; E10](https://www.mfa.gov.cn/eng/wjb/zzjg_663340/bmdyzs_664814/xwlb_664816/202609/t20260926_12031663.html)

These observations support a specific interpretation. Strategic advantage influences some development and safety rules; safeguards remain incomplete and are improving; common purposes and negotiated channels are available within the actual policy landscape. They do not establish a uniform mentality, a universal race, or a measured causal chain from success through capture to catastrophe. They do establish that technical safety decisions operate inside choices about rivals, authority, and shared consequences. A proposal that addresses only model fidelity leaves those choices substantially untouched.

## 3. How success changes future selection

### 3.1 The recursive mechanism

A selection process allocates resources, deployment, or authority according to some criterion of success. Successful participants can then change the environment in which later success is judged. They may provide the evaluation tools, own the interfaces through which evidence is available, support standards, appoint decision-makers, or determine the cost of switching to another provider. Later selection partly inherits the consequences of earlier selection.

We use **meta-attractor** for a pattern in which this feedback helps reproduce a trajectory across changes in particular actors or practices. The term names a proposed organizing mechanism; it does not establish novelty by itself. The mechanism can reproduce useful correction as well as disabling it. Its harmful form arises where success increasingly depends on control of future selection while shared conditions, corrective ability, or safe alternatives deteriorate.

Not every change of standards is capture. Our criterion requires a durable actor advantage produced through a change in selection or corrective authority that impairs an entrusted purpose or protected interest. Improving a test because it better measures relevant quality is compatible with that purpose. Revising the purpose legitimately is also possible. An institution becomes suspect when the beneficiary can exclude adverse evidence, protect a favorable rule against justified revision, or make correction practically unavailable. Those claims require an independent account of the purpose and evidence of impairment.

### 3.2 Why control capacity can be selected

Consider a carrier whose success profile across domains is \(y=p\bar y\). Its investment \(q\ge0\) converts success into future rule support. Let \(\chi\in[0,1]\) be the fraction of this support whose benefit the carrier can appropriate. Let \(M_{ij}=a_iL_i\hat b_{ij}/d_i\), where \(a_i\) values support, \(\hat b_{ij}\) is its conversion channel, \(d_i\) its decay rate, and \(L_i\) the share realized within the comparison horizon. Under the support dynamics specified in M3, the carrier's incremental payoff is

\[
\Delta\pi(q)=\chi q p^2\bar y^{\mathsf T}M\bar y-C(q).
\]

The local selection gradient is consequently

\[
\Delta\pi'(q)=\chi p^2\bar y^{\mathsf T}M\bar y-C'(q).
\]

The result concerns selection for the capacity to change selection. With variation and faithful transmission, a capacity associated with higher payoff can spread. These transmission conditions must be specified at the relevant level: inheritance of a model-training procedure, replacement of an agent scaffold, allocation of procurement share, and appointment of institutional leaders are different processes.

The square-of-share term matters. Success contributes both to the support generated and to the carrier's ability to benefit from that support. With a persistent linear marginal cost of control, investment can pay for a large carrier while being selected against in a small one. This mechanism amplifies an established position; it does not prove that any rare controller will become dominant. Nor does the derivative solve the strategic equilibrium after rivals and institutions respond.

Appropriability is equally consequential. If the benefit of the same rule improvement is genuinely shared, the private incentive to purchase control can fall. This suggests separating rewards for useful production from rights over the future rules. It does not suggest making every productive benefit unappropriable. Research, infrastructure, and professional competence require viable means of support.

### 3.3 Correction has both a level and a rate

An investment that makes evaluation more independent can benefit many participants while costing its contributor. Under the equal-practice, equal-exposure assumptions of the local model, privately costly correction is disadvantaged within a practice even when it improves the field. That is a public-good problem, not a law that nobody ever supplies correction. Institutions can pay for it, professional obligations can sustain it, and users may reward it. The constructive task is to make those channels durable.

The simplified selected-control model makes the requirement explicit. Write the inbound pressure on one evaluation domain as

\[
R(d,\gamma)=\frac{A}{c d}
+D\gamma^{-1/\eta}d^{-(1+1/\eta)},
\]

where \(d>0\) is the rate at which obsolete or actor-specific rule support decays, \(\gamma>0\) is the cost coefficient of cultivated control, \(\eta>0\) governs cost convexity, \(A\ge0\) is background conversion, \(c>0\) is a baseline margin, and \(D\ge0\) collects the cultivated channel. In a stationary field, \(R<1\) supplies the row certificate described below. If \(\gamma\) falls as \(e^{-gt}\) and \(d\) rises as \(e^{\alpha t}\), the cultivated term has exponential rate

\[
\frac{g-(1+\eta)\alpha}{\eta}.
\]

This exposes the relevant comparison: AI may cheapen control and independent correction simultaneously. Sufficient growth of correction can preserve the certificate if the initial margin is adequate. A persistent marginal-cost floor, lower appropriability, and structural separation can also change the result. The instantaneous selected-control calculation is not by itself a proof that a nonstationary field tracks that optimum or becomes captured; actual support dynamics must be checked.

Nor does a bound on unaided human attention supply a bound on \(d\). A rule can be corrected through technical tools, reusable abstractions, legal authority, or changes in access without every person understanding every possible behavior. Conversely, accurate understanding without authority may achieve little. An application must connect resources, knowledge, delay, and executable action to the model's correction rate. That connection remains an empirical and institutional task.

### 3.4 Coupled fields and the absorption of reform

Let \(x_i\in[0,1]\) be the share of an actor-favoring practice in institutional domain \(i\), and \(z_i\) its accumulated support. Consider

\[
\dot x_i=\zeta_i x_i(1-x_i)(a_i z_i-c_i),
\qquad
\dot z_i=\varepsilon_i\left(\sum_jb_{ij}x_j-d_i z_i\right),
\]

with positive rates and margins, and nonnegative cross-domain conversions \(b_{ij}\). Define \(R_{ij}=a_i b_{ij}/(c_i d_i)\). A domain's full inbound row includes influence from the evaluated actor, allies, rivals, shared providers, and other relevant sources. Testing each institution in isolation can miss their combined support.

M5 proves that a row sum strictly below one drives \(x_i\) toward zero from any initial \(x_i<1\), regardless of the other domains. Conversely, a set of mutually supporting domains can sustain one another where its internal row sums exceed one. If such a seed persists, sufficiently connected domains can be drawn into it. A reset that leaves the inbound support above the sustaining threshold can be reabsorbed once the practice is present again.

These are explicit results of a small positive-feedback model. Its boundary \(x_i=1\) is absorbing; real entry or forced replacement needs an amended model. Its baseline margin is not a proof of the legitimacy of the baseline rules. Its conversions are not presently estimated for the frontier-AI institutional field. Nevertheless, it explains a concrete failure pattern worth measuring: replacing a visible evaluator may change little when the evaluator's evidence, budget, career, and operating tools remain controlled through connected channels.

The one-domain pause result gives the same lesson in temporal form. Holding current shares fixed while support continues to accumulate differs from holding shares fixed while insulating the rule-making process. What matters after release is the resulting state and its basin of attraction. A training pause is not identical to that model intervention, but the model identifies a question any pause should answer: what continues to accumulate while visible expansion is stopped?

### 3.5 Observation belongs inside the mechanism

Correction usually begins with a report. Suppose true condition \(V\) is reported as

\[
\widetilde V=V+m(K-V),\qquad 0\le m<1,
\]

where \(K\) is full health and \(m\) an upward bias. An alarm at \(\widetilde V\le V_a\) fires only when

\[
V\le\frac{V_a-mK}{1-m}.
\]

A favorable report can therefore delay action even without falsifying every underlying observation. Averaging more evaluators can reduce independent noise while leaving shared bias. But this is not an impossibility result for monitoring. A credible bound \(m\le\bar m\) allows the alarm threshold \(V_*+\bar m(K-V_*)\), which fires by the time the true condition reaches \(V_*\). A credible dissenting report can also trigger an action rule that does not depend on the panel average.

The difficult questions concern how the bound is learned, whether contrary reports are heard, and whether somebody can act on them in time. Independent evidence access, conservative alarms, direct observation of actions, and protected reporting are genuine remedies. The recursive question is what keeps their authority and resources intact after they inconvenience successful actors.

## 4. Perfectly faithful AI in a harmful human game

### 4.1 The strongest alignment grant

Grant that each AI understands its principal, tells the truth, follows the principal's objectives and constraints, and has no independent ambition. Grant also that people retain final authority. This removes model disobedience from the demonstration. It does not make the principals' objectives mutually compatible or ensure that decisions internalize shared consequences.

For an elementary example, let \(n\ge2\) principals each choose restraint or one increment of racing, \(a_i\in\{0,1\}\), with objective

\[
u_i=b\left(a_i-\frac1{n-1}\sum_{j\ne i}a_j\right)
-c a_i-d\sum_ja_j,
\qquad c,d>0.
\]

The first term rewards relative standing; the second is private effort; the third is shared damage borne by each principal. When \(b>c+d\), racing increases the principal's objective by \(b-c-d>0\) regardless of the others' choices. All-racing is the unique Nash equilibrium. Yet each receives \(-c-nd\), while all-restraint gives zero. Relative gains cancel in the aggregate, leaving

\[
\sum_i u_i=-(c+nd)\sum_i a_i.
\]

Perfect assistants can implement this game perfectly. Human signatures on every decision leave the result unchanged. The proof in M2 is a standard positional-externality construction. It does not estimate the actual coefficients of any state or firm, establish catastrophe, or exclude repeated-game cooperation. It establishes exactly why principal alignment is insufficient for alignment among principals.

If perfect alignment is defined instead as pursuing a legitimate common human purpose, the cooperative change in purpose has already entered the definition. That is a worthwhile objective, but it answers a different question from fidelity to each current principal.

### 4.2 How objectives persist

The one-period game takes \(b,c,d\) as given. The recursive-selection mechanism asks how such objectives and their institutional setting persist. If appointments reward positional victories, budgets follow competitive success, and evaluation rights become part of the prize, people and organizations with other priorities can be displaced. A safety reform can improve one round while leaving the next round's definition of success in the hands of its beneficiaries.

This connection is plausible but not automatic. A selection gradient on control capacity does not prove the evolution of a particular psychology. Organizations may retain public-service commitments, select for reliability, or learn from loss. We distinguish the selection of actions, the selection of organizations, and the formation of purposes. The claim is strongest where the actual transmission mechanism can be observed: performance reviews, procurement rules, appointment criteria, funding decisions, and durable control of access.

For the same reason, cooperation cannot consist solely in suppressing an allegedly fixed selfish nature. It can change what competent service means, which achievements are rewarded, and what responsibilities officeholders learn to carry. Institutions can make concern for the common conditions an ordinary part of effective action. They can also fail to do so. Public purpose has to be reproduced through practice, feedback, and succession, not merely announced.

### 4.3 What cooperation changes in the game

The model contains several routes to improvement. A binding constraint can remove the racing action; credible enforcement can change its expected payoff; useful common provision can improve the payoff to restraint; decision-makers can value shared consequences more adequately. These mechanisms can operate together. Reducing the prize of domination is one lever, rather than the only possible lever.

An arrangement also needs to survive deviations. Suppose the gain from an unauthorized action is at most \(G\), and an additional penalty \(F\) is imposed with probability \(q\), all in the same utility units. The expected deviation gain is at most \(G-qF\). Thus \(qF\ge G\) deters that deviation in this one-period risk-neutral model, assuming the penalty is actually executable. This says nothing yet about who controls detection or a deviation that alters the future enforcement system. Those questions bring the game back to endogenous correction.

The relevant institutional achievement is thus a common purpose with working means: useful benefits, enforceable limits, evidence access, and correction that remains available when the powerful need correcting. Technical alignment contributes to that achievement. It does not substitute for it.

## 5. Capability, delegation, and effective correction

### 5.1 A chain of action

Effective correction is an accomplished sequence: a relevant event becomes observable; someone identifies it; the evidence reaches a decision-maker; the decision-maker has authority; an action changes the process before the relevant loss. Fast inference at one link does not remove delay or dependence at another. A regulator without access and an evaluator without execution rights can both fail despite technical competence.

Let the conditional probabilities of completing those successive steps be \(q_1,\ldots,q_m\). Their product is the probability of completing the chain when each factor is conditioned on the preceding successes. This does not require independence. It does require a specified event and horizon. An institution's ability to recognize a defect is not the probability of preventing the resulting harm.

Topologies also matter. A serial chain that must transmit a warning through every link can become less reliable as links are added. Independent parallel channels can increase the chance that at least one effective warning arrives. Common dependencies can defeat that redundancy. M6 gives the distinct formulas; neither serial degradation nor parallel improvement should be applied to every oversight architecture by analogy.

We use \(U\) for operational coverage of a specified class of hazardous behaviors, not for every fact any human could explain unaided. Coverage can include verified constraints, tested abstractions, monitoring, and corrective actions that people can authorize and challenge. Coverage is still fallible. Its measurement should include missed hazards, false restrictions, delay, execution, and recovery. Defining every successful automatic safeguard as understanding would make the hypothesis untestable; the protocol for assessing coverage must be fixed before the result is known.

### 5.2 Delegation can reduce the ability to reverse

An actor can delegate because speed improves its competitive position even when delegation increases shared risk. The relevant comparison is its marginal strategic benefit against its marginal expected loss under a stated objective. The payoff model of earlier versions supplies such a condition; it does not establish that delegation is dominant for every loss valuation or every technology.

Dependence creates a separate mechanism. If usable human capacity \(K_h\) decays during exclusive delegation as \(\dot K_h=-\delta K_h\), it crosses a required level \(K_{\min}\) after \(\delta^{-1}\log(K_h(0)/K_{\min})\), assuming the initial level is higher. This is a transparent model of skill and organizational attrition. It is not evidence that human capacity necessarily decays at that rate. Training, parallel operation, portable records, and practiced fallback can prevent the crossing.

A loss of fallback is a loss of an option, not automatically a loss of every person's physical survival. It can nevertheless change who effectively governs. If an authority cannot operate, inspect, or substitute for the infrastructure on which it relies, its formal right to refuse can become difficult to exercise. Measuring human control therefore requires observing overrule, substitution, response time, and continued competence, rather than counting signatures.

The distinction is particularly clear in M8's finite game. If delegation certainly eliminates the actor's continued agency, and nondelegation retains a positive probability of that agency at finite speed, an actor maximizing that probability strictly prefers nondelegation. If delegation instead carries a probability slightly below one of agency loss, the conclusion can reverse. An unbounded-speed limit cannot be transferred to a policy that caps speed. These boundaries matter for designing actual constraints.

### 5.3 Residual danger after correction

The relevant hazard is what remains after the safety measures being evaluated. Let \(S_N\) mean no specified terminal loss through round \(N\). Suppose on every surviving history the probability of that loss in round \(k\) is at least a deterministic \(h_k\), with \(0\le h_k<1\). Then

\[
\Pr(S_N)\le\prod_{k=1}^{N}(1-h_k)
\le\exp\left(-\sum_{k=1}^{N}h_k\right).
\]

If \(\sum_kh_k\) diverges, survival tends to zero. Independence is unnecessary. The substantive premise is the lower bound on residual danger across surviving histories, together with continued exposure. A harmful stage game, a positive selection gradient, or a corrector's possibility of error does not alone establish that bound.

The distinction between hazardous variety \(Y\) and all behavior \(X\) is essential. A system can behave in many ways that do not contribute to the loss being modeled. A simple coverage model might posit a residual fraction \(r=(1-U/Y)_+\), but a relation between \(r\) and hazard must still be stated. If \(Y\le U<X\), the coverage-based lower bound is zero; actual risk may remain. The fact that total behavior exceeds coverage does not change that bound.

Stopping expansion also differs from removing exposure. If hazardous activity remains at \(Y_0>U_0\) while coverage grows linearly, \(U(t)=U_0+vt\), the integrated uncovered fraction until catch-up is \(I=(Y_0-U_0)^2/(2vY_0)\). An actual intensity at least \(h(1-U/Y_0)_+\) gives a survival upper bound \(e^{-hI}\), hence a terminal-loss lower bound \(1-e^{-hI}\), not a survival guarantee. Equality requires the stipulated intensity law and no additional hazard. Rolling activity back to an already covered level has a different immediate effect. M7 derives these comparisons and permits coverage gaps whose residual risks are summable even without exact catch-up.

### 5.4 A finite decision cannot be settled by two infinite limits

A race and a cooperative arrangement can both have zero survival probability over an infinite horizon while differing substantially over every relevant finite horizon. Conversely, a small probability that the assumed hazard model is wrong can matter greatly to a comparison. We therefore compare survival or loss at a common finite \(H\), with the same event and uncertainty convention.

For discrete hazards \(h_k\), the exact cumulative log hazard is \(-\sum_k\log(1-h_k)\); it is not exactly \(\sum_kh_k\). For continuous intensities \(\lambda(t)\), survival is \(\exp[-\int_0^H\lambda(t)dt]\) under the specified intensity model. M9 retains this distinction. No result about lower relative survival automatically supplies a preference ordering over two zero limits.

The same discipline applies to a finite sprint. Gaining a temporary lead and later relinquishing it can be compatible with survival. It may also impose a large avoidable exposure before correction catches up. The question is the finite path, including rival responses, rollback, service dependence, and residual hazards. Permanent superiority is neither a definition of survival nor an established prerequisite for it.

### 5.5 Why the timing still matters

Rejecting a universal impossibility proof does not make timing irrelevant. A harmful process can develop faster than available evidence, authority, and action can respond. In a linear unstable mode, an uncorrected perturbation grows as \(e^{aL}\) over a delay \(L\). This illustrates why a fixed delay becomes more demanding as growth \(a\) increases. It does not prove that every controller fails at the same threshold. Prediction, different control architectures, and hard permission boundaries can alter the problem.

Similarly, risk compensation is a possible response to safer tools. If an improvement makes additional activity privately attractive, deployment can expand and partly consume the safety gain. It may also saturate, leaving residual risk lower or summable. The question is empirical: does safety improve per unit of useful activity, does exposure expand, and what happens to aggregate harm? Assuming all safety gains are competed away would prejudge that question.

Taken together, these mechanisms support preserving the capacity to intervene before dependence, exposure, and control of evaluation become entrenched. They establish reasons to change the conditions of expansion. They do not establish that any positive capability growth must end in catastrophe.

## 6. The strongest case for continuing

### 6.1 Leadership, defense, and better tools

The strongest continuation argument begins with real benefits. More capable systems can improve scientific work, defensive analysis, monitoring, and the capacity to discover mistakes. An actor facing coercion may reasonably fear that restraint will transfer power to a less accountable rival. A theory of cooperation must compare those consequences with the risks it attributes to racing.

This argument succeeds where the marginal protective and productive benefits exceed the additional exposure under credible correction, including the effects on others. It also depends on the distribution and use of the resulting power. A lead can finance independent evaluation or make evaluation dependent on the leader. Both routes are possible. The existence of a defensive use does not establish that the whole development trajectory is defensively necessary; the existence of a harmful use does not establish that capability is uniformly harmful.

We therefore reject a demand for unilateral helplessness. The proposed transition retains protective capabilities and essential services while negotiating reciprocal constraints on specified hazardous actions. It should be abandoned or redesigned if its interim structure creates an unacceptable exposure that the proposed correction cannot address. Reciprocity is a mechanism to make restraint safer, not a substitute for verifying that it does.

### 6.2 Technical correction that actually scales

AI-assisted evaluation, trusted monitoring, verified permission boundaries, robust control, and reusable abstractions may outperform an arrangement based only on unaided human inspection. The correct comparison permits those methods to work. Their effectiveness must be measured at the deployment boundary, including the ability to execute a response, rather than inferred solely from a model's performance on an isolated test.

There is bounded evidence for this possibility. Greenblatt and colleagues' adversarial programming experiments show that trusted editing can substantially improve safety while retaining usefulness under their specified threat model. This supports testing correction architectures and their assumptions, rather than treating a weaker or fallible monitor as necessarily ineffective. [Greenblatt et al. 2024; E5](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.06942v5)

The recursive concern is durability. Who determines the tests after a release becomes economically or strategically valuable? Can a monitor's adverse finding stop an activity against its operator's preference? Can outsiders test the assumptions on which the claimed boundary depends? If the answer is supported by actual rights, access, and implementation, this is evidence for effective correction. If it depends entirely on the evaluated actor's continuing discretion, it remains a conditional promise.

There is no contradiction between advocating technical alignment and changing the human game. The latter can provide the incentives, resources, and authority that let the former remain effective.

### 6.3 Bounded competition and a finite sprint

Competition can support entry, reliability, discovery, and checks on a dominant provider. A shared constraint can keep its consequences within an acceptable region. In the host model of M11, keeping load below a specified boundary makes the viability set invariant. In a game, bounded deviation gains and credible enforcement can sustain restraint. Neither construction requires every participant to cease caring about relative position.

The present question is whether such constraints can be installed and maintained in the relevant domains. A capability cap without a way to account for substitution can fail; an effective cap need not. An independent regulator can lose access; it can also hold enforceable rights. A finite sprint followed by rollback can have positive survival; whether it is preferable depends on the exposure accumulated and the feasibility of giving up the lead. We treat these as live policy alternatives.

The distinction between continuation and pivot remains the one stated in Section 1. Technical monitoring adopted while strategic actors retain the authority to set their own exceptions can improve continuation. Reciprocal limits that place specified actions under a shared purpose and binding correction implement a pivot in that domain. The labels follow the change in governing rights, not the outcome alone.

### 6.4 A common provider can fail too

Common infrastructure can reduce duplicated contests and make useful services widely available. It can also create concentrated dependence, correlated technical failures, discriminatory access, or an authority insulated from challenge. A beneficial mission statement does not decide which occurs.

We apply the same tests to the proposed remedy: evidence access, funding, meaningful remedies, timeliness, continuity, replacement, and amendment. A common operator that controls all these channels can reproduce the same recursive mechanism. Conversely, a public authority can supply expertise, long-term investment, and executable coordination unavailable to fragmented actors. The design question is how to realize those capacities while preserving the means to correct them.

### 6.5 How we compare the choices

We use a limited decision procedure, with an explicit normative basis. First specify a common finite horizon, affected population, essential functions, and legitimate constraints on harm and coercion. Compare feasible policies across benefits, severe loss, agency, distribution, and transition cost under the same supported scenarios. Prefer a policy when it is no worse on the protected outcomes and better on at least one without defeating a constraint. Where this dominance test fails, do not claim it supplies a total ranking. Identify the unresolved trade-off and the public judgment needed to resolve it. Survival-first preference, maxmin choice, and a particular set of welfare weights are separate choices; none is silently supplied by the mathematics.

Our additional normative premise is that credible exposure of others to severe, hard-to-reverse harm creates an obligation to provide representation, evidence access, and executable correction. Competitive pressure does not discharge that obligation. The same obligation binds a public consortium and an authority imposing restrictions. Under unresolved uncertainty, we favor staged, revisable measures that improve those capacities, subject to demonstrated continuity and defensive feasibility, over an irreversible increase in unaccountable discretion. This is an institutional judgment whose costs and failures must be assessed, not a computed global optimum.

No supplied dataset presently identifies every quantity needed to rank all complete geopolitical futures. Our immediate recommendation therefore has a narrower operational basis: begin reciprocal limits, protected correction, and useful shared provision whose purpose is to change the governing trajectory, and condition further expansion of that arrangement on demonstrated performance. Evaluate those first moves against credible continuation measures, including their costs and defensive consequences. This makes cooperation a direction with testable institutional commitments, while leaving the pace and final architecture responsive to evidence.

The observed conjunction in Section 2.4 gives this recommendation its present relevance. Some safety commitments already respond to rivals' choices, while meaningful uncertainty remains about actual correction. Technical progress alone does not remove the discretion to revise those commitments. Shared standards, reciprocal limits, and independently executable assessment directly address that dependence. Announced cooperation supplies a possible starting point rather than evidence that the task is complete. This is the causal reason to begin institutional change alongside technical work instead of waiting for model fidelity to settle a problem among human decision-makers.

The broader ambition remains: AI should become a means of shared flourishing, with technical progress serving that purpose. The evidence and models support taking that purpose into the organization of development now. They do not authorize promising that one blueprint will eliminate risk or settle every conflict.

## 7. What cooperation makes possible

The purpose of a cooperative pivot is to make greater intelligence useful to people who share its consequences. Better science, accessible services, relief from dangerous work, and the capacity to understand and improve institutions are substantial ends. A trajectory should be assessed by these achievements, their distribution, and their durability. Being ahead of a rival can sometimes serve them; permanent superiority is not their definition. This distinction changes the object of governance. The question becomes how to organize development so that productive success strengthens the capacity to serve these ends, including the capacity to recognize when a successful practice has become harmful.

The public-authority ideal motivating this proposal, articulated through a Chinese governing tradition in the authorial brief, assigns authority responsibility for improving people's lives and contributing to a better historical trajectory. We take this as a governing commitment, not a comparative claim about the conduct of contemporary states. No institutional tradition realizes its ideals automatically, and no population has a uniform political motive. The design problem is to give public purpose competent means, then maintain the relationship between purpose and conduct as leaders, providers, and capabilities change. Authority must be capable of commissioning useful infrastructure, financing patient research, resolving collective-action problems, and acting on evidence. Its capacity to do these things is part of its justification.

### 7.1 Forming and sustaining a common purpose

Common purpose cannot be represented adequately by an initial declaration added to otherwise unchanged rewards. The meta-attractor mechanism concerns the reproduction of what counts as success. A cooperative institution therefore has to influence how its own governing commitments are learned, rewarded, transmitted, and revised. Recruitment and professional formation can select for demonstrated service and sound judgment. Promotion can reward reliable provision, the discovery of consequential errors, and successful correction, including correction of a superior's decision. Leadership evaluation can examine longer-term outcomes alongside delivery. Protected disagreement can supply information that ordinary chains of promotion discourage. These are proposed mechanisms for sustaining public purpose; their effects require observation.

The protected ends and the technical means of pursuing them should have different amendment procedures. Evaluators need authority to update a test when a new failure mode appears. They should not thereby acquire authority to decide whose basic interests may be sacrificed. Providers need room to improve a service. They should not thereby acquire authority to exclude the people who challenge it. Revision of governing purposes belongs to an explicitly authorized public process, with standing for affected people, reason-giving, and opportunities to contest the interpretation of their interests. Different political arrangements may implement these functions differently. A provider's claim to represent the public is insufficient evidence that the functions exist.

This account allows responsible discretion. An institution unable to make timely judgments cannot discharge its public purpose merely by accumulating vetoes. Ordinary administration should operate within a clear mandate, while decisions that alter protected interests or substantially extend coercive powers require a different level of authorization. The boundary must remain reviewable. Protecting the corrective function means preserving its resources, records, and access through succession; it does not mean protecting particular officials from removal.

### 7.2 Rewarding productive work without selling the rules

A common purpose is compatible with plural providers, local priorities, and bounded competition over usefulness, reliability, cost, and scientific achievement. Shared infrastructure need not mean one permanent operator. Financing, ownership, access, technical interfaces, evaluation, and enforcement are separate design choices. Some facilities may justify pooled investment; some services may benefit from multiple implementations; some research can remain competitive under common constraints. The relevant comparison includes competence and economies of scale as well as dependence and capture.

The proposed institutional distinction is between rewards for productive contribution and rights to determine the conditions under which contributions are judged. A provider can receive greater payment, additional service contracts, or resources for further research when independently assessed performance improves. Those rewards need not carry an automatic increase in its voting weight over safety rules, power to appoint its evaluator, or ability to withhold evidence. Expertise deserves a voice in rule-making, and changes suggested by a successful provider may improve the rules. The question is whether other participants can scrutinize those changes and whether the provider can override an adverse finding through the dependencies created by its success.

This distinction gives the selection result an institutional use. In the field model, appropriable support makes control investment pay. In the proposed arrangement, productive rewards are designed to remain available while their conversion into protected decision rights is constrained. This does not set the model's appropriability parameter to zero by declaration. Contract dependence, technical indispensability, personnel networks, and agenda-setting can recreate the conversion channel. Its strength has to be assessed through actual appointments, funding decisions, access disputes, and changes in rules. Concentration is therefore neither sufficient evidence of capture nor irrelevant to it.

Replaceable provision makes this separation more credible. Service contracts can require documented interfaces, transferable operational records, and tested migration pathways. Users can retain access to their records and obtain assistance in moving essential workflows. Replacement may involve an alternative supplier, a public operator, or a less capable but adequate fallback. It does not require unrestricted distribution of capabilities whose release creates another hazard. The design target is practical continuity and contestability of provision, with the costs and limitations of substitution stated. A nominal exit right that cannot be exercised before services fail supplies little corrective power.

### 7.3 Authority that can obtain evidence and execute correction

Independent evaluation is a relationship supported by resources and executable powers. For a bounded cooperative compact, we propose a constituting agreement that identifies the covered activities, protected purposes, authorized evidence access, and permitted remedies. Its commitments would have to be implemented through the legal and operational instruments available to its participants. A voluntary research consortium, a procurement arrangement, and a treaty-backed authority can compel different things. Their powers should not be described as interchangeable.

Recurring assessment funding would be committed through a formula and reserve arrangement specified before particular findings arise. Contributions need not be equal, but a contribution should not purchase unilateral control over a case or allow the assessed provider to cancel an investigation's funding. Appointment and removal rules would separate provider representation from adjudication of that provider's conduct. Removal for incompetence or misconduct would remain possible through a reasoned process that preserves the case record and the institution's ability to continue working. Emergency financing would have its own authorization and audit, so that ordinary budget independence did not disappear at the first expensive incident.

Access would include the evidence needed for the defined hazard: relevant operational records, evaluation conditions, access to responsible personnel, and means of testing consequential claims. It need not imply indiscriminate disclosure of sensitive information. Protected inspection, bounded disclosure, and separately reproducible attestations can reduce that burden where they actually answer the question. If the necessary evidence cannot be obtained, the report should identify the resulting uncertainty. Confidentiality cannot be treated as proof of safety, and a refusal of access cannot be treated automatically as proof of the alleged underlying danger. The compact must specify the provisional operational consequence of unresolved uncertainty for each covered activity.

An adverse finding must connect to someone able to act. Under the proposal, a designated operational authority could impose a narrowly specified temporary restriction within the compact's prior mandate. A separate review body could examine the evidence, the fit between finding and remedy, and less harmful alternatives. Urgent action would sometimes precede full adjudication, but it would have a defined scope, expiry, and route to prompt challenge. Extensions would require renewed reasons. Reviewers would be able to correct mistaken restrictions and order restoration or compensation where the governing instruments permit it. Neither the accused provider nor the evaluator would have an unchecked final veto.

Essential services require a continuity arrangement prepared before enforcement. Operational records, qualified personnel, and authority to maintain a previously assessed service would need to remain available if the leading provider were restricted or replaced. Where the finding implicated that service itself, continuity could require reduced functionality, an alternative implementation, or a manual fallback. Continuing an unsafe operation under a different custodian would not solve the problem. The burden of interruption on patients, workers, and other dependent users belongs in the remedy assessment; their needs should not become bargaining assets that immunize a provider against correction.

### 7.4 How a cooperative institution can fail

Consider a publicly owned consortium whose largest contributor controls appointments, whose evaluators depend on its technical stack, and whose users cannot transfer their essential workflows. Suppose it excludes an entrant after a confidential assessment, provides no effective appeal, and withholds service from a jurisdiction disputing the exclusion. The consortium can announce a planetary purpose while reproducing precisely the conversion of success into control analyzed earlier. Public ownership and common language do not answer this counterexample.

The opposite failure is also possible. A federated compact may provide extensive appeal rights but distribute assessment resources so thinly that nobody can understand a consequential defect. If every member can veto a temporary restriction, the compact may record a danger without being able to interrupt it. Dividing authority can reduce correlated capture while increasing delay and weakening execution. It should be chosen for its observed effects under the relevant resource constraints, not as a virtue independent of outcomes.

These cases establish requirements for comparison, not a theorem against cooperation. The proposed arrangement earns support if it provides useful services, permits adverse findings against powerful participants, produces proportionate action, and survives leadership and provider replacement with acceptable costs. A simpler institution may perform better. If it supplies stronger correction and continuity under comparable conditions, the design should change accordingly. The cooperative commitment concerns the purposes and shared constraints to be sustained, rather than the permanence of a particular organizational blueprint.

## 8. A reachable transition and readiness to resume

A desirable destination does not by itself give current actors a reason or an ability to move toward it. Transition begins from unequal capabilities, unequal dependence, disputed evidence, and incomplete trust. It must provide useful benefits while limiting the opportunities for coercion that arise during institutional construction. The following hypothetical sequence makes these demands concrete. It is a proposed design exercise, not an account of an existing agreement or a claim that its participation conditions hold today.

### 8.1 A bounded compact between unequal participants

Let A be a leading provider with substantial development capacity and an installed service network. Let B be a smaller consortium that relies on A for some important administrative and health-service workflows, while retaining local deployment authority and some independent research capacity. Neither actor can be treated as identical to the people it serves. Users, workers, and affected nonparticipants have interests that their management may fail to represent.

The initial compact covers a defined class of consequential deployments and a specified review period. It does not purport to govern every future AI capability. A accepts bounded reciprocal inspection and temporary limits on expanding the covered activities. B accepts the same functional standards for its deployments and permits inspection of relevant local modifications. Their immediate joint product is a credible assessment and continuity service. Research that does not violate the agreed boundary can continue, as can ordinary improvements to covered services within assessed operating conditions.

A's potential gains include paid provision, pooled assessment resources, more predictable requirements, and reduced exposure to a rival's unsafe deployments. B's potential gains include useful access, assistance in building evaluation competence, and a practical replacement path. A gives up some unilateral discretion and prospective advantages; B accepts some externally reviewable limits and access obligations. These costs are real. A temporary compensation or procurement commitment may help satisfy participation constraints, but compensation should not become a permanent veto over standards or a protected monopoly.

For each participant, evaluation must compare the expected benefits and burdens over the same horizon, including the available outside path. If \(J_i(H)\) denotes the value of joint participation before transition burdens, \(C_i(H)\) those burdens, and \(O_i(H)\) the best feasible outside option under the same decision criterion, participation requires

\[
J_i(H)-C_i(H)\ge O_i(H).
\]

This accounting condition does not establish agreement. The quantities are uncertain, nonmonetary constraints may exclude some bargains, and affected people's claims cannot always be discharged by transfers between organizations. Its use is to expose who bears a loss, which promised benefit makes participation attractive, and where the proposal currently lacks an answer.

### 8.2 Sequence, deviation, and interim vulnerability

The first stage establishes the evidence baseline and operating boundary. A and B document the covered dependencies, identify which records an evaluator requires, and agree on the consequences of an unresolved access dispute. A commits continuity rights and assistance for migration; B commits access to the local systems through which it could evade the limit. Neither commitment is verified by announcement alone. The compact begins with supervised access exercises and a constrained service transfer, so that the parties learn whether the promised rights can be executed.

The next stage places assessment funding, case records, and continuity authority beyond unilateral withdrawal during the agreed period. A restriction on new expansion need not await completion of the entire cooperative destination, but any increased reliance on the shared infrastructure should await evidence that these elementary functions work. The smaller participant is particularly vulnerable while it grants access before it has a working substitute. That interval requires a contractual service obligation and a fallback arrangement proportionate to the dependency. If they cannot be supplied, expansion of that dependency should be deferred.

Both actors may still benefit from deviation. A could hide a consequential change, influence the evaluator, or threaten service interruption. B could conceal local modifications, falsely allege discrimination to delay an order, or present an unassessed operation as an essential service. Inspection must therefore be paired with the practical ability to respond before the violation produces the loss it is meant to prevent. Detection after an irreversible deployment can document failure while supplying no deterrence. The compact should assess the size and consequence of a violation that can remain undetected within its review window, rather than promise perfect observation.

Benefits and restrictions can be staged accordingly. Access to new joint projects can depend on demonstrated compliance; essential civilian services should not become the automatic penalty for an unrelated dispute. Restrictions should target the covered capability, the responsible operator, or discretionary expansion where those actions can address the risk. If no effective remedy exists without imposing unacceptable harm on dependent people, that is a present limit on enforcement. It is a reason to reduce the dependency and reconsider the activity's scale, not to assume compliance will supply the missing authority.

### 8.3 Two contested adverse findings

Suppose an evaluator first finds that B's local modification violates the agreed operational boundary. B disputes the test and alleges that it privileges A's implementation. The evaluator must identify the relevant behavior and evidence, rather than rely on B's size or affiliation. A temporary restriction could isolate the disputed modification while the previously assessed service continues. B would receive protected access to the evidence necessary to challenge the finding, and review would consider whether the standard is technically justified and whether a narrower remedy works. The allegation of discrimination would not itself cancel the restriction; the evaluator's status would not settle the allegation.

Now reverse the location of the finding. Evidence implicates an update supplied by A across both participants' critical workflows. A disputes the result and warns that restriction will interrupt service. The same functional standard applies, but the operational remedy must address a different dependency. The continuity custodian could maintain an assessed prior version only if the evidence does not implicate it. Otherwise, the response could require reduced service, migration, or manual operations, with assistance directed to dependent users. A's access to records and specialist knowledge may remain necessary during the transition; the agreement must have secured the rights and personnel arrangements that make their use possible despite the dispute.

This second case is the harder test. If A can prevent action by withdrawing its experts or indispensable infrastructure, formal reciprocity has failed. If the authority can disable A's services without evidence review, a bounded mandate, or provision for users, effective enforcement has become a means of arbitrary coercion. If a mistaken restriction cannot be reversed, the appeal is ornamental. The compact passes only to the extent that it can navigate these failures in practice. The test concerns proportionate correction under asymmetric dependence, not identical sanctions for situations with different consequences.

Renewal should depend on what these experiences establish. Failed access, selective exemptions, unmanageable service dependence, or repeated inability to execute justified remedies would block expansion and trigger repair or contraction. Successful correction can support a broader mandate, but it does not prove that the same arrangement can govern a qualitatively different frontier. New activities require their own hazard analysis, resources, and authorization. A cooperative transition may proceed unevenly across civilian provision, frontier research, and strategic decisions.

### 8.4 Stopping, pacing, and the terms of resumption

Stopping an activity, temporarily pausing it, and developing it more slowly are different choices. An indefinite stop or binding cap removes a class of future actions from the permitted path. Slower development changes their timing and may create a larger correction window. A temporary pause suspends them while a decision about resumption remains open. These choices have different benefits, enforcement requirements, opportunity costs, and effects on dependence. None should disappear into the language of a generic slowdown.

A pause can improve readiness by producing better technical reliability, executable correction, maintained human competence, changed incentives, and useful shared provision. It can also entrench an incumbent if its success continues converting into influence over the rules while competitors' shares remain frozen. The field-model pause result identifies that narrower possibility. Insulating rule-making and protecting entry on justified terms therefore belong to the assessment of a pause's effects, alongside its reduction in immediate exposure.

Elapsed time is not itself evidence that resumption is responsible. The relevant inquiry is what has changed in the residual hazards, purposes, constraints, and institutions that determine how capability will be used. Has the proposed activity become safer under independently assessable conditions? Can adverse findings reach an actor with the capacity and authority to respond? Can people retain meaningful decision authority, including trained fallback competence and the ability to refuse or replace a system? Can participants continue to meet the agreed limits when rivals advance? Which benefits would further delay forgo, and who would bear them? These are dimensions of a decision, not a proved sufficient certificate.

The assessment should grant the full value of progress in technical alignment. It should also retain the perfect-alignment distinction: faithfully executing a principal's instructions does not establish that the game among principals serves their shared conditions. Binding constraints and institutional changes can improve that game even when private preferences remain competitive. Where resumption would restore the same consequential rivalry and inadequate correction, a pause has bought time without yet resolving the governing problem. Where relevant conditions have changed, the recommendation should change with them.

The attainable objective is credible protection over stated horizons, with evidence and resources for renewal, revision, or withdrawal. Cooperative institutions face residual risk, succession, conflict, and learning. Their case rests on whether they make useful development and effective correction more achievable than the feasible continuation paths, while reducing the dependencies that would prevent a later change of course. That is a demanding mechanism-design task, but it does not require either permanent harmony or the preservation of today's winners.

## 9. The warranted decision and what could change it

### 9.1 The case for beginning the pivot

The choice before us concerns the purposes and conditions of AI development as well as its technical performance. Where an actor's expansion creates consequential exposure for others, its own judgment of strategic necessity cannot be the sole authority over that exposure. This follows from the governing commitment stated in Section 1, not from a forecast of inevitable catastrophe. The formal mechanisms add a reason to protect the obligation institutionally: success can increase the ability to reshape the rules under which future obligations are assessed.

The current evidence establishes enough to make that problem practical. Competitive dependencies appear in published safety policies; national development programs connect capability to strategic leadership and standards; assessment still leaves important gaps. It also establishes resources for another direction: stronger controls, functioning disclosure and repair, proposals for common provision, and negotiated channels among rivals. The situation contains both tendencies. A cooperative pivot means organizing development so that the latter can govern the former when shared conditions are at stake.

We recommend beginning with commitments that make this change tangible. Protect the resources and access needed for corrective evidence. Separate productive rewards from authority over the rules. Define reciprocal limits for consequential activities whose safe use depends on those limits, and evaluate their effects on defense as well as hazard. Build useful shared services with continuity and replacement rights. Preserve people's capacity to authorize and contest the systems on which they depend. Test these arrangements through adverse findings, including findings against their strongest participant, before expanding reliance on them.

These are substantial changes in governing logic. They permit technical progress, local experimentation, and competitive service provision within shared obligations. Their purpose is to make AI's benefits available without requiring one actor to win control of the common future. Implementation will differ across civilian services, frontier development, and strategic decision-making; reciprocity that works in one domain does not certify another.

The evidence does not yet establish that every such commitment is feasible in every setting or that a comprehensive compact dominates every continuation policy. If a proposed restriction undermines essential services or defense without an adequate substitute, that part of the transition fails its own test. If effective independent correction can be maintained through a less integrated arrangement, the design should use it. The recommendation is to change the trajectory through institutions that demonstrate their value, with the specific path subject to revision.

### 9.2 Distinctive mechanisms and intellectual inheritance

The local mathematical ingredients have substantial predecessors. Relative-payoff cancellation, externality games, convex investment, positive-feedback fields, hazard products, and participation constraints are standard forms of reasoning. Their use here is not a claim that those methods originated in this manuscript.

An especially important predecessor is Acemoglu, Egorov, and Sonin's model of decisions that alter future political power. Its stable-state analysis makes clear that endogenous authority and resistance to change are substantive existing objects of theory. The precise equivalence or difference from our carrier-selection and field models remains a model-by-model question. [Acemoglu et al. 2012; E11](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/publications/Dynamics%20and%20Stability%20of%20Constitutions,%20Coalition.pdf)

The contribution developed here is the connection between a carrier's appropriable investment in future selection, the correction required at its selected optimum, and a field in which reform can be reabsorbed through other domains. The square-of-share return, the correction-rate comparison, the seed requirement, and the different pause designs make that connection inspectable. The broader synthesis places those mechanisms alongside the human-principal game, residual risk after correction, and transition participation. It turns a general concern about winning into separate claims that can succeed or fail independently.

There is also evidence that changing the organization of correction can work. Duflo and colleagues' randomized environmental-audit reform improved reporting and reduced pollution in its study setting. Because the treatment bundled several changes, it does not isolate a single institutional lever or establish permanent independence. It does refute an assumption that useful correction can only be asserted on paper. [Duflo et al. 2013; E12](https://www.theigc.org/sites/default/files/2013/10/Duflo-Et-Al-2013-Working-Paper.pdf)

The present contribution is therefore an integrated argument with explicit conditional results and an institutional application. A claim of a new general law, or of priority over every relevant economic and systems model, would require a broader comparison than this draft supplies. The [claim record](CLAIMS.md) identifies inherited results, new repairs, and remaining questions so that intellectual ambition need not depend on overstated novelty.

### 9.3 Evidence that would change the diagnosis

Three investigations are especially discriminating. First, trace a consequential sequence from success to control rights and from those rights to independently measured correction. A favorable rule change is not enough. The investigation must distinguish legitimate expertise, scale economies, and changes in actual quality from impairment of an entrusted purpose. Durable improvement despite unchanged dependence would narrow the capture hypothesis; measurable deterioration following increased control would support it.

Second, compare safety methods before and after the selection environment can adapt. Hold initial resources and useful performance comparable; allow a fixed selector, matched nonstrategic drift, and endogenous changes in evaluator rights. Measure actual error and useful output independently of the scores that allocate success. A protected-rights condition can test the proposed intervention. Manipulation must be optional and costly, and genuine quality improvement must remain a route to success. Otherwise the simulator merely restates an assumed reward for capture. A bounded result would concern that mechanism, not establish a geopolitical probability.

Third, test a cooperative arrangement through a consequential disagreement. Can an evaluator obtain evidence, can a justified decision be executed before the relevant loss, can a mistaken decision be reversed, and can necessary services continue? Run the same functional test against a weak and a strong participant, accounting for their different dependencies. Success would support a specific route toward cooperation. Failure would identify a design limit rather than establish the safety of the race.

Several outcomes would materially weaken the practical recommendation: durable correction shown to keep pace with the relevant hazards under the specified competitive incentives; decisive advantages that saturate without eroding agency; reliable containment of harms previously thought to be shared; or a feasible continuation policy that offers better protected outcomes and lower transition costs on the same horizon. Conversely, a proposed commons that disables correction or entrenches dominance would defeat that proposal. The theory should help distinguish these outcomes.

### 9.4 Conclusion

Greater intelligence does not determine the purposes it serves. Faithful AI can execute each principal's choices perfectly while implementing a collectively inferior outcome. Success within that game can also change how later decisions are evaluated, which errors are visible, and who can correct them. This is why durable alignment concerns the reproduction of human purposes and institutions as well as the behavior of individual systems.

The mathematics establishes conditional pressures and usable counterconditions. The evidence shows a present situation in which rivalry, safety progress, unresolved exposure, and cooperation coexist. Our judgment is that the governing direction should shift toward AI as a technology for people collectively: supported by useful provision, reciprocal obligations, capable authority, and correction that successful actors cannot simply withdraw when it becomes inconvenient.

A pause can preserve time and options. Resumption is a further decision about what has changed. Cooperative arrangements likewise earn their authority through what they enable and how they respond to failure. The objective is a future in which increasing capability enlarges people's shared possibilities and their ability to revise the path together. That purpose should organize development now, while the institutions capable of sustaining it are built and tested.
