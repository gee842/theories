### The vocabulary of winning, and its critics

The doctrine of winning has at least two strands. *Within firms and public institutions:* an essay of 1970 argued that the social responsibility of business is to increase its profits within the rules of the game, which include law and ethical custom (Friedman 1970); agency theory recast the firm around aligning managers with owners (Jensen and Meckling 1976); a leading business association declared shareholder primacy the purpose of the corporation in 1997 and revised that statement in 2019; and market criteria were carried into institutions generally (Harvey 2005). In that turn self-interest moved from an assumption inside models to a norm of conduct and a criterion of design [obs, interpretive; EV15]. The doctrine's strongest form is that the residual claimant bears the residual risk, so that with complete contracts and externalities priced by law, maximizing the residual maximizes surplus; that separation fails where externalities cannot be separated from production decisions (Hart and Zingales 2017), which can be read as the unpriced coupling $\kappa$ [interpretive]. An early defence of commerce was that interests would tame the passions for glory and domination (Hirschman 1977); read that way, the pressure of winning is the passion for domination returning in the dress of interest, not the commercial tradition itself. *Between states:* doctrines of national survival and technological rivalry, from the Melian dialogue (Thucydides) and the Legalist programme of enriching the state and strengthening its army to the national Darwinism of late- and early-industrializing states alike and the technology races of the Cold War.

Both strands appear across civilizations, and so do their critics: Smith on sympathy; Darwin on the social instincts; Kropotkin on mutual aid; Polanyi on markets embedded in society; Ostrom on governing the commons; and, in China, Mozi's impartial care and condemnation of offensive war, and Kang Youwei's *Book of Great Unity*. Translated into doctrines of national survival in states that industrialized late, the strand was answered there too. Yan Fu rendered Huxley's 1893 lecture, whose point was that ethical progress consists in resisting the cosmic process, as a doctrine of 物竞天择, "things contend and heaven selects" (Yan 1898; Schwartz 1964); others set mutual aid against struggle and imagined the removal of boundaries. These histories illustrate how the pressure is narrated; they are not an account of any civilization's essence.

**The tradition behind V4.** One strand of the Chinese tradition the author draws on justifies authority by improving people's lives and contributing to a better historical trajectory. Like every tradition, that one also contains strands that justified power by strength. The theory takes the ideal as a positive account of what power is for; it does not assume that every institution realizes it, that any other tradition has one uniform motive, or that power necessarily corrupts.

### Ψ in full

*Its borrowed authority is a misreading.* "Survival of the fittest" was Spencer's phrase (1864), adopted by Darwin from the fifth edition of the *Origin*; Spencer himself expected societies to move from a militant to an industrial type. Darwinian arguments were later recruited for laissez-faire, and equally for the racism, eugenics and imperialism of reformers who rejected laissez-faire (Hofstadter 1944, who popularized the label; Leonard 2009); the label was largely a critics' term (Bannister 1979), and the borrowing belongs to no single strand of thought. *It misdescribes human beings.* In experiments in fifteen small-scale societies the canonical self-interest model failed in every society, and cooperation rose with market integration (Henrich et al. 2001); about half the subjects of a public-goods experiment were conditional cooperators (Fischbacher, Gächter and Fehr 2001); users govern commons without either a sovereign or privatization where institutions fit the resource (Ostrom 1990), though those commons were bounded and small, and scaling their design principles to global commons is itself an open problem (Stern 2011). Markets are institutions of cooperation among strangers; the target is the reading of them as a contest of all against all (EV14). Students of economics defected more often in a prisoner's dilemma, a difference later work attributes mainly to who chooses the subject (Frank, Gilovich and Regan 1993). *Held and attributed.* Realism's strongest reply to verifiable conduct is that conduct verified today does not settle uncertainty about a rival's future intentions (Copeland 2000b; Mearsheimer 2001), which is why the arrangements of Sections 10 and 11 verify capabilities and custody, which can be checked, rather than intentions.

**Status.** The desire for status, meaning respect, admiration and voluntary deference from others and distinct from power, is a fundamental human motive, observed across cultures, genders, ages and personalities (Anderson, Hildreth and Howland 2015). Status is relative, so it is never completed. Rank has two routes (Henrich and Gil-White 2001): dominance, gained through fear and control, and prestige, deference freely conferred on skill and success, and both yield real influence (Cheng et al. 2013). The drive is for respect; dominance is a substitute route to rank. Prestige also compounds (Merton 1968), which is why the condition on a generative order is that it not convert into control over the shared conditions or their correctors (EV16).

### The strongest objection from biology

Within-group cooperation was often favoured by competition between groups; Darwin's account of the social instincts rested on competition between tribes, and parochial altruism, cooperation inside and hostility outside, may have co-evolved with intergroup conflict (Choi and Bowles 2007). The answer is Lemma 3.10: a benefit common to all is never favoured by selection at any level of grouping, while a benefit confined to one group can be favoured between groups. At the scale of one host the benefits of the rules that keep it viable are of the first kind. Two routes to integration that need no rival group remain, and the core keeps both: polycentric trial among sub-units, as in nested commons governance and experiments within federations, which works wherever failure is not final; and reputation and indirect reciprocity, which change within-group payoffs without design (Panchanathan and Boyd 2004). What the core claims is narrower than in v6.0: failures that are global and irreversible cannot be learned by trial (Price 1972; Hamilton 1975).

### International relations

The logic of Section 3.8 has two sources: actors hold it, and selection on actions rewards its behaviour; and structure produces it, since anarchy, decisive advantage and the absence of a commitment device produce outcomes of the same kind among actors who do not hold it (Propositions 7.2, 5.4). An actor holding it does not stop compounding, because no level of standing makes more stop rewarding; on a continuation branch where the premises of Section 5.3 hold, Theorem 5.12 applies to its own capability too. To oppose the logic is to change the structure and the selection environment, not to oppose any actor: credible, verifiable limits on what anyone would do with decisive advantage; no decisive advantage without a commitment device; and common knowledge that the logic endangers its holder. In the vocabulary of international relations this is defensive realism with one more variable (Jervis 1978; Glaser 1994/95, 2010): a hazard in development that endangers its holder acts as a negative payoff to the offense, as a weapon that endangers its holder does, and the Biological Weapons Convention is the closest precedent. To the extent that the offense has the advantage and offense and defense are hard to tell apart, AI sits in the doubly dangerous quadrant of the offense–defense framework, where cooperation is hardest. The outcomes of the enforcement condition correspond to constructivism's three cultures of anarchy (Wendt 1999): enmity, where only stage equilibria hold; rivalry, where cooperation is enforced; friendship, where it is built into objectives. The theory adds the selection that moves a system between them, and the decisiveness that collapses the middle one. Balancing by others against a rising actor (Waltz 1979; Walt 1987) is a saturation mechanism independent of technology, and Proposition 5.15(2) is its counterpart in talk.

### The alignment literature

The insufficiency of single-principal alignment follows from the multiplicity thesis of Critch and Krueger (2020), developed in work on robust agent-agnostic processes (Critch 2021), societal-scale risks (Critch and Russell 2023), natural selection among AI systems (Hendrycks 2023), cooperative AI (Dafoe et al. 2020), multi-agent risk, which already lists selection pressures as a risk factor (Hammond et al. 2025), and gradual disempowerment (Kulveit et al. 2025). Section 6.2 is an elementary illustration of that thesis. What is added is the mechanism by which the game reproduces itself and its correctors: selection on relative standing that is blind to what all share, with no misalignment needed; the second-order selection for control over correction; and the nested reward loop with its alignment-tax premise. Multi-agent failures include miscoordination and collusion among AI systems as well as conflict among principals, and the stress tests count them.

### What is absorbed, and what is new

Much of the anatomy is known and absorbed: multipolar traps; realism's account of competition under anarchy (Waltz 1979; Mearsheimer 2001; Glaser 2010; Jervis 1976); Goodhart's and Campbell's laws; regulatory capture (Stigler 1971; Laffont and Tirole 1991; Dal Bó 2006); increasing returns and lock-in (Arthur 1989); the systems archetypes "success to the successful" and "escalation" (Senge 1990; Sterman 2000); self-help as an institution (Wendt 1992); security communities (Deutsch 1957); the translation invariance of relative selection (Weibull 1995), which is Lemma 3.3(1); Powell's (1991) account of relative gains; the "generator functions" of catastrophic risk; and the AI literature above. The theory adds:
1. the reflexive closure as a specified selection mechanism, with its certificates, rate law, keystones and pause design (Section 4);
2. the interconnection of the conditions: one loop gain that decides compounding and prices standing, and the shared parameters that link it to enforcement, correction and talk (Propositions 2.2, 3.9);
3. the enforcement condition and the scissors: how winning moves each of its terms (Proposition 5.4);
4. the compounding condition with provision not won in the contest, the formal content of trees (Proposition 2.3);
5. the two-channel accounting identity (Proposition 3.5);
6. how common constraints survive: not the invisibility itself, which is absorbed, but its connection to incidence, evasion and the capture of enforcement (Lemma 3.3(2)(3));
7. AI as instrument and as weak and strong incarnation, with the alignment-tax premise and the nested loop (Section 6);
8. at the scale of one host, what can be learned by trial and what must be anticipated, and where understanding acts (Lemma 3.10).

**Endogenous measurement and preference [conj].** The masking model is the simplest form of epistemic closure: reality exists and the sensor is biased. Deeper forms are listed for research (TESTS, Study 4): capture of interpretation; capture of the concepts in which a failure could be expressed, so that the variable itself disappears from what the system can represent; and capture of preference, in which people no longer regard the state as harm. Each is a further step of the reflexive closure; none is modelled here.

## X2. Substrates

The pressure needs only selection on relative success, convertible winnings and, for its closure, the ability of the successful to reshape their selection. The following are demonstrations; none is the theory's ground.

- **Cells.** A cancer lineage evades the signals that limit proliferation, recruits the surrounding tissue into supporting its expansion, and evades the immune response that would remove it (Hanahan and Weinberg 2011; Aktipis et al. 2015). Growth is not the fault line: large animals, with far more cells, show no higher cancer risk than small ones, because suppression scales with size (Peto's paradox; Caulin and Maley 2011).
- **Firms.** Markets with increasing returns lock in early leads (Arthur 1989). The doctrine that the purpose of business is profit "so long as it stays within the rules of the game" (Friedman 1970) meets its limit where the successful can buy the rules. The same school analysed that possibility (Stigler 1971), and its remedy was to shrink discretionary rule-making that could be captured. The disagreement is about the remedy: a planetary technology with unpriced coupling has no option of making no rules, and where common rules are absent, the winners' systems make them in fact. The doctrine's own argument that managers spending others' money on social goals tax without representation supports public, independent correction, not voluntary self-regulation by firms.
- **States.** In security competition a gain for one is a threat to another, and falling behind is read as fatal.
- **Federations.** Members compete for capital and residents, and a race to the bottom in standards follows; the strongest can capture the centre; common standards and fiscal equalization change what the federation selects for.
- **Science.** Metrics allocate positions, and the positions set the metrics.
- **Training loops.** A policy is selected by a reward a developer sets, and the developer is selected by a contest; pairwise preference comparisons see only differences between responses.
- **Another planet.** Nothing in Definition 2.1 refers to a culture, a political system or a species. A civilization on another planet, with other drives and institutions, would face the same pressure wherever selection is relative, winnings convert and the successful can edit the selection.

## X8. The alternatives in full

Each alternative is given its best case, how it would be installed, what risk remains, what it changes, and what evidence would show that it suffices. Section 8.3 of the core summarizes each; the formal results are in the core and S8.

### Technical alignment and AI-assisted correction

**Best case.** The strongest form is not "technical alignment alone". Control is scoped to early transformative systems, used under control to accelerate alignment and oversight research while the window lasts (Greenblatt, Shlegeris, Sachan and Roger 2024): control protocols tested against red teams raise safety at a given level of usefulness in the settings studied; weak supervisors can elicit part of a stronger model's capability (Burns et al. 2023); and in auditing games, teams with access to training data found a planted hidden objective that a team with black-box access alone did not, which supports mandated access (Marks et al. 2025). Oversight can be built redundantly or adversarially by debate, and formal methods certify invariants without predicting trajectories. AI may cheapen independent correction as fast as control (Proposition 4.5), and correction people cannot follow can still lower residual risk (Proposition 6.8). Its proponents' own plans rank a strong international agreement with a long slowdown first, and treat technical work as what one does when that is unavailable.

**What remains.** Principal alignment does not change the game among principals (Proposition 6.1). The window lasts only as long as the race allows, and the race shortens it. Systems are retained for performance (Lemma 6.5), and on interior branches safety gains are absorbed by more delegation (Proposition 6.11). Redundant oversight is capped by common causes, which selection produces, and certified row sums do not establish independent errors (Proposition 6.9). AI-assisted correction is only as independent as its funding, access and appointment. The common-cost part of incarnation is set at the outer loop (Proposition 6.7).

**What it changes.** Tempo, observation and residual harm per unit. It becomes part of a change of selection when held by correctors separated from competitive success, under constraints common to all. **What would show it suffices:** the safety of control protocols at a fixed audit budget not falling across generations; catch rates of planted defects at fixed human hours not falling as capability rises, controlled for evaluation awareness; independent evaluators keeping certificates while depending on the evaluated for access; residual risk per hazard class falling over generations.

### Regulated competition with strong correction

**Best case.** Competition in products and services continues; liability and licensing make consequences return; evaluation access is mandated and thresholds fixed in advance; and where the effective hazard is high and capability transparent, pacing can be the unique equilibrium of a capability race without any agreement (Fudenberg and Koh 2026). There are precedents of durable safety with unchanged commercial motives: an industry that knows one accident can end it for all, as civil nuclear power came to after a major accident; and aviation, with independent accident investigation, liability and insurance pricing. This is the bounded competition a cooperative order can contain (V5).

**Installation.** Each jurisdiction regulates its own developers; coverage must reach every capable actor, since one actor can start the race; the rule-makers are themselves in a contest of standing, so no one of them imposes the liability alone (Proposition 9.2); rule-making is subject to Section 4.

**What remains.** Liability cannot exceed the assets of the liable for catastrophic harm. Regulation in one market leaves the load elsewhere. Written safety commitments are already set relative to rivals and are revised mostly toward weakening, and one developer's own framework states that it cannot commit to mitigations unilaterally because of a collective action problem (EV2). Public evaluation bodies have shifted their mandates toward industry contact under voluntary access (EV3). The precedents had no decisive prize; the pacing equilibrium has no delegation channel and assumes transparency.

**What it changes.** This depends on content. Regulation that returns consequences and separates evaluators from success, across every capable actor, changes the basin and is the change this paper argues for, whatever it is called. Regulation that constrains conduct while success can still buy the rule-makers is predicted to erode as stakes and speed grow. **What would show it suffices:** competitor clauses not invoked; thresholds held when rivals advance; the mandates of evaluation bodies widening as the stakes rise.

### Leadership and a safety margin

**Best case.** A leader spends part of its lead on safety: it slows, evaluates and hardens before the follower catches up. A large lead can discourage the follower (Harris and Vickers 1987; Konrad and Kovenock 2009); a laggard far behind under moderate hostility does not gamble on risky technology (Stafford, Trager and Dafoe 2022); and where the shared hazard is significant, a leader that commits to slow can lead rivals to slow too (Bueno de Mesquita, Dziuda and Polborn 2026). One developer's framework includes such a "leading" scenario (EV2).

**What remains.** Remark 8.6: leads diffuse; the margin must buy coverage; it is judged inside the contest; and a leader seeing its window close has a motive to strike first. **What it changes.** Tempo for a time; it keeps the objective of winning. Its best case, a leader slowing and inducing rivals to slow, is a step toward reciprocal restraint, the first stage of a transition. **What would show it suffices:** leads not spent on speed; rivals slowing in response; margins long relative to the evidence lags of the hazard classes.

### The benign winner: win, bind, share

**Best case.** The strongest form is that a winner wins, binds itself and shares. After great wars, victors have bound themselves through institutions to lower others' fear and their own costs of enforcement (Ikenberry 2001); a dominant power can supply public goods a field of equals would not (Kindleberger 1973; Gilpin 1981), and unipolarity can be stable (Wohlforth 1999); an encompassing ruler internalizes the shared conditions (Olson 1993): in the model of Section 3.5 a single developer restrains whenever restraint is worth more to the field than it costs, so the failure lies in the race, not in the existence of a winner. The holder of the first nuclear monopoly did not extend its dominance to everything it could reach. In AI the strategy has been proposed in explicitly humanitarian and defensive terms: a coalition seeks a clear advantage, including military superiority and the denial of key inputs such as chips and semiconductor equipment to adversaries, and then offers its benefits to others in exchange for their support, so that adversaries give up competing (EV1). Its force comes from the combination of stick and carrot. Most strong international institutions were built by victors, a real challenge to a path that assumes institutions built among rivals at rough parity.

**What remains.** The commitment tests: after a decisive win what the others receive is set by the winner's objective alone, and a promise to share is worth breaking (Proposition 5.7); the race shifts winners toward those that extend dominance (Proposition 7.2); and the winner is itself a field of agencies, firms, laboratories and systems, whose restraint after victory depends on whether its own correctors can be bought by its successes. The historical cases had advantages that were not decisive, and their restraint rested on institutions others could check. Whether AI removes a winner's dependence on the cooperation of the governed (Acemoglu and Robinson 2012) is part of crux C4.

**What it changes.** Nothing in selection before victory; after victory, everything depends on the winner's internal field. **What would show it suffices**, before victory: binding limits on what victory would confer; benefits shared contemporaneously rather than promised; the coalition's own correctors, constitutional correctors included, certified with row sums below one, with succession rules that survive changes of leadership (Test I). A winner that adopts these has changed what it selects for.

### A finite sprint, then restraint

**Best case.** An actor sprints to a decisive lead and then stops compounding: it freezes or rolls back, and governs at the tempo of understanding; decisive contests are won with a modest edge when $r$ is large, so the sprint need not run far beyond understanding. **What remains.** Remark 8.7: the path passes through its greatest exposure, and through the rivals' strongest motive for prevention, before any restraint begins; a decisive advantage not used to stop others' growth does not stay decisive. Under a survival-first comparison a sprint can preserve the sprinter's survival while giving up permanent superiority, which is not survival. **What it changes.** Nothing during the sprint.

### Deterrence and prevention

Prevention can be survival-preferred for the preventer (Proposition 8.8). The objections are three, and none is a change of criterion: normative, since it ends or endangers others' O1 and O2; epistemic, since aimed at a rival believed certain to race it destroys, when the rival is in fact survival-first, a game in which restraint was possible, and can lead the rival to act as the type it was feared to be (the spiral model; Jervis 1976; Kydd 1997), while shifts that cannot be observed raise the chance of mistaken prevention (Debs and Monteiro 2014); and technical, since detecting unobservable progress and striking at machine speed brings the delegation of Section 6.3. Deterrence by threatened sabotage aims to make a decisive sprint not worth attempting. *Best case:* large datacentres can be observed and compute thresholds made salient (Hendrycks, Schmidt and Wang 2025; Abecassis 2025). *What remains:* while the value of winning stays large, timely detection and credibility approach their limits (Theorem 8.11); among nuclear-armed rivals credibility is the first problem, and under survival-first comparisons it can reverse; pushing AI into military capability strengthens the delegation pressure; verification that supports it can also supply targets (Coe and Vaynman 2020); and algorithmic progress erodes thresholds stated in compute (EV9). *What it changes:* it acts on $p_d$ by threat, and its fallback is war or race.

**The hard case.** Let a survival-first actor hold the prior $\mu$ that a capable rival holds an unbounded relative objective. When $\mu$ is low, its best course is to propose verifiable limits, and to take narrow E1 unilaterally and verifiably in its own strategic loops, the costly signal of Proposition 11.2. When $\mu$ is high, it compares denial, a coalition that stops the decisive class, and prevention. Coverage of every capable actor then comes from consent or from coercion. Denying the inputs to the decisive class is the coercive form of a stop; it raises the weight on winning and the trust threshold, and if it escalates it falls under Proposition 8.8. The theory does not derive that its net effect is positive and does not endorse it. If neither consent nor a coercion of positive net effect is available, no actor has a secure position: that is the hard case, stated as such. Insuring against falling behind is not an exit (Remark 8.9).

### A verified agreement over a contest that remains

**Best case.** Rivals agree to slow and to delay decisive capability, with compute declarations, supply-chain accounting, recomputation of workloads, research transparency, and mutually assured destruction of compute if the agreement dissolves; it works without trust and bounds the largest covert project that could escape detection. One detailed plan of this kind, a deal among leading powers to delay superintelligence to about 2040 (Larsen et al. 2026; EV10), adds inference-only retrofits of datacentres and hardened reserves of about two per cent of pre-deal compute, and aims to end winner-take-all dynamics through many frontier developers, transparency, broad diffusion and a citizens' dividend. Its own scenario estimates do not show near-certain dissolution: one author's judgment puts decline in years five to ten at about four per cent a year, half dissolution and half impairment, and cumulative decline over ten years near half. Its handoff to AI systems is a conditional decision that weighs further alignment research against other risks, and it keeps an option to extend the pause.

**What remains.** Verification is required exactly to the degree that the value of winning exceeds the value of cooperating, and the net gain from exit can grow with the capability held in check (Theorem 8.11; Proposition 8.12). **What it changes.** It slows and caps, acting on tempo and decisiveness, and keeps relative standing in its enforcement, through proportional reserves and a coalition that must stay ahead of covert projects; the conversion of success into control over correction is addressed only through transparency. That makes it a bridge. Whether it can be the end depends on the durability tests and on whether it lowers the value of winning or only bounds defection. Common custody faces the same verification limits as any agreement; what differs is its fallback, and whether it lowers the value of winning, a claim with the same standing as any agreement's.

**Where the criteria disagree.** Under the protection standard the worst case includes a rising uncovered share on the continuation path, because its premises are named mechanisms the register does not contradict (EV5–EV7); it equally includes the capture of a common custodian on the cooperative path, because its correctors' certificates can fail (Proposition 4.9). Both fail the tolerance in their worst case, and the comparison moves to the standard's third step, which needs estimates on both sides. An expected-value criterion that puts low probability on a rising gap and values speed highly can prefer continuation; a discounted criterion prefers racing whenever the hazard it adds is below the growth it adds. A well-corrected continuation can outperform a fragile cooperative arrangement; the analysis does not establish that every cooperative arrangement outperforms regulated competition.

## X10. The design in full

One configuration is developed far enough to expose its assumptions. It is a candidate, compared in Section 12.1 with other designs under one range of uncertainty: a polycentric complex of mutually recognized institutions, a verification agreement together with shared provision, and regulated competition meeting the requirements of Section 9. The text does not claim it is superior; which design is best is part of crux C4. It is called here a *compact*: an arrangement for shared AI provision and correction, made of separately held functions rather than one institution.

### Functions, held separately

**Separation rule.** No body both revises the levy formula and appoints evaluators, and neither of those executes findings. Each has its own row-sum certificate. Supermajorities and the chamber's consent apply to the slow loops, amendment and the formula; the fast loop, restricting a specific capability, stays fast, with review after the fact, because every veto point adds delay, and the robustness margin of Proposition 5.11 depends on it.

**The chamber.** It is a corrector, and Section 4 applies to it. Its members are drawn by a rule no participant controls, they rotate, and its inbound row sum is certified like any evaluator's. The regress of guardians ends not at a trusted body but at node-by-node certificates that many parties can recompute from public data; what remains of the regress falls on standard-setting, which has its own row.

**Recusal.** A participant under assessment does not vote on revisions of the purpose under which it is assessed. Assessments are opened by the evaluation network on stated evidence and lapse if not concluded within their deadline.

**The levy formula is itself a prize.** A levy on frontier compute above a threshold is paid into two funds by formula. The correction fund is indexed to the coverage each hazard class requires, the $d^\ast$ of Proposition 4.5, not to the field's capability and not to perceived harm, so that correctors do not become stakeholders in capability growth and do not lapse after a recovery. Its revenue base is decoupled from the activity it constrains, so that it does not shrink under E2. The formula is fixed for a registered period; revision needs the supermajority and the chamber, and revisers are separated from beneficiaries and carry row-sum certificates, because by Proposition 4.3 the formula is a conversion target.

**End conditions.** If coverage falls below the participation threshold, or a stated number of its functions' certificates fail for a stated period, the compact dissolves into its separately held functions, each continuing under its own rule. Protections against unilateral domination are entrenched. Every power that confers authority states what would end it.

**Other provisions.** A charter with three purposes: useful provision for all participants' populations; protection against O1 and O2; effective correction. Membership open to every state and provider above a capability threshold on non-discriminatory terms, participation made individually rational by access to shared services, a share of the dividend and capacity building. Participation benefits, voting rights and protection against unilateral domination kept apart. Baseline access to useful services with continuity guarantees; portable interfaces, escrow of systems providing essential services, and support for each participant to audit, operate and replace them. Narrow E1 with human authorization and a minimum time for deliberation for decisions on the use of force and on nuclear command, drilled parallel capacity, and doctrine open to mutual inspection. Admission constraints common to every system, before release, for divergences with common costs, with decision policies retained by performance only inside them. Tempo: no building of the decisive class while readiness is unmet; capability thresholds by evaluation, and input thresholds in effective compute that tighten with algorithmic progress. Graduated remedies: notice; restriction of a specific capability; suspension of specific deployments; never suspension of essential services; emergency powers narrow, time-limited, reviewable and stating what would end them. Providers earn revenue and contracts by usefulness and have no rights to appoint evaluators, set thresholds, exclude entrants or amend the rules. Bounded competition continues inside: providers compete on usefulness, cost and reliability, and jurisdictions keep plural local purposes.

### Hard cases

**An adverse finding against a weaker participant.** Pending appeal, only the capability at issue may be restricted; essential services continue through alternative providers; the appellant sees the evidence under confidentiality, the panel decides within the deadline, and compensation follows if the finding is overturned. The power to suspend could become containment; the safeguards are the narrow scope of interim measures, the burden of evidence on the compact, and the appellant's standing.

**An adverse finding against the leading provider.** Suspending it could interrupt services for everyone, so formally equal procedures would be unequal in effect. The share cap, escrowed systems and funded standby capacity mean that restricting the leader's hazardous capability need not stop services. Execution falls first to the leader's host jurisdiction. If that fails, the fallback is a restriction of inputs to the decisive class: coercive, the same form whose endorsement Section 8.3 withholds, and treated the same way, authorized only under the custody rules, with a sunset and the stated end condition, its escalation risk counted in $\Theta$. The theory does not derive that its net effect is positive. Where neither consent nor such a fallback is available, the case is the hard case of Section 8.3. This is the hardest case, and the design does not dissolve it; it reduces the asymmetry of dependence that would otherwise make enforcement against the strongest impossible.

**A coalition of insiders.** Rival elites agree on mutual secrecy and immunity while weakening public correction. Four channels answer to people outside the coalition: the chamber, protected reporting, evaluators funded by formula, and publication of findings by default after a fixed delay.

**An unjustified restriction.** The compact is used to contain a developing participant. It has standing to challenge, the burden is on the compact to show evidence of hazard, restrictions carry sunsets, and the compact has obligations to build capacity.

**Succession and withdrawal.** A change of leadership triggers review, not suspension. A withdrawal by a large participant makes the constraints borne by the rest unilateral and can trigger further exits; the participation threshold and the conditions of market access are what keep coverage above it. Because the decisive class was not built and benefits and knowledge were diffused, a withdrawing party starts any race far from decisive capability and with a lower value of winning.

**Loss of an essential service.** Redundancy, escrow and standby capacity carry the service.

| Test | Result | Status |
|---|---|---|

| D2 Breakdown | Driven by the value of winning, changes of leadership and violation by the strongest | Unknown; crux C4. No estimate is given, where the Plan A type agreement gives one |
| D3 Authority | No handoff deadline; legibility, overrulability, exercise and resources maintained by drills and rates of overrule | By design; to be tested |
| D4 Its own correctors | The hardest case: by Proposition 4.3 the arrangement is the largest conversion target the theory describes. Formula funding, insulated appointment, diversity counted in control domains | Certificates to be measured |
| D5 Strongest violates | Dependence caps and standby reduce the asymmetry; the coercive fallback as above | The hard case remains |
| D6 Succession | Review rather than suspension; continuity of essential services | By design |
| D7 Common causes | Evaluators and reference systems of different lineage and funding; collusion among AI monitors a design variable; error correlation measured | To be tested |
| D8 Exclusion | Open membership; the chamber for non-members' populations | Fails if membership closes into a club |

Three things could make its hazard of breakdown lower than that of an agreement that keeps the contest underneath: shared benefits lower the value of winning (Corollary 11.1; Proposition 10.3); keeping AI out of strategic loops is a strict equilibrium among informed actors (Proposition 6.4); and its fallback is not a decisive race from parity (Lemma 8.2). These are hypotheses about the design, not established results.

### Federations, at both orders

Competition among member units for capital and residents can become a race to the bottom, and the strongest members can capture the centre. At the first order, common minimum standards bind every member, and fiscal equalization gives members capacity they do not have to win, both widening the effective self of members that value their absolute payoff and supplying the $k_0$ of the loop gain; for states that act strategically, pooling raises the working self-model rather than only slowing the race. At the second order, equal representation of small members and supermajorities for constitutional change keep the centre from being bought by the largest, a precedent for voting that does not follow compute. A global minimum tax on corporate profits is a recent instance of one rule for all, and of its leakage where coverage is incomplete. The same federal form can select for winning or for contribution; what decides is what it rewards.

### Narrow E1 and the value of commitment

Deterrence theory holds that removing human discretion from retaliation makes a threat credible: a commitment that cannot be revoked is worth more than one that can (Schelling 1960). Its stronger form is that semi-automated retaliation could relieve the pressure to launch on warning. Five answers follow from the core. The commitment value comes from removing the possibility of human overrule, which is the lock of Lemma 6.3 and, in its domain, a loss of O2. Survivable, human-controlled forces relieve the pressure to launch on warning without removing judgment. An automated commitment cannot tell a false alarm from an attack; several false alarms of early-warning systems are documented, and in 1983 an officer judged an alarm false and did not report an attack. Automation adds a common risk, escalation at machine speed on a false alarm, that relative selection does not see (Lemma 3.3), so the contest under-prices it. And among actors who price in the lock, keeping AI out of these loops is a strict equilibrium, and doing so verifiably is a costly signal. Proposition 6.4's strict equilibrium needs the lock to be near certain, which holds in the narrow domain; in tactical domains, where the speed advantage is large and the lock uncertain, delegation can be an equilibrium, and bounded, reversible defensive automation falls under the O2 test of Section 6.6.

### Feasibility

Each actor can take part of E1 alone, and taken verifiably it is the signal of Proposition 11.2. E2 and E3 are the most verifiable among rivals, because chips, fabs, datacentres and compute can be observed; algorithmic progress erodes thresholds stated in compute, so with efficiency doubling about every eight months a nominal cap must tighten roughly two and a half to three times a year (EV9), a rate condition verification must meet. Hardware-enabled mechanisms for verifying compute use are proposed but not deployed (EV17). E1 is the hardest to verify, since it concerns software and internal procedure, and the most self-enforcing among informed actors, which is why it can begin as a norm. Staying out of the contest alone while others race can be worse than joining: if staying out raises the rival's decisive chance from $1-\varepsilon$ to $1-\varepsilon^2$, survival falls from $\varepsilon(1+\varepsilon)/2$ under joining to $\varepsilon^2$, which is why E1 must be held in common and begins with decision loops.

### Precedents, corrected against the sources

| Precedent | What made it work or fail | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| The Acheson–Lilienthal report (1946) | International ownership of the dangerous activity was proposed | Custody of the dangerous activity as the object of agreement |
| The Baruch plan (1946) | It added that sanctions on violators would not be subject to veto; the other side opposed this and on-site inspection, and the leader would keep its monopoly until control was in place (Bundy 1988) | Custody must precede decisiveness, and enforcement against violators is part of the bargain |
| The first nuclear monopoly | Preventive war was argued and not carried out; the advantage was small in stockpile and balanced by conventional forces (Trachtenberg 1988/89 for 1949–54; Rosenberg 1983 for the early stockpile, unchecked) | Restraint where the advantage was not decisive |
| The ABM Treaty (1972) | Reciprocal limits on defences reduced the offensive race, while capabilities in which one side held a lead, such as multiple warheads, were not limited; the treaty was abandoned in 2002, for reasons that are a matter of interpretation | Reciprocal constraint can change the value of extra advantage; durability fails when the value of winning rises again |
| Test bans | The partial ban forbade atmospheric, space and underwater tests while underground testing continued | Not a stop of the decisive class |
| The Outer Space and Seabed treaties | Domains excluded from weapons of mass destruction | A domain kept out of the contest, close to E1 |
| The Biological Weapons Convention (1972) | A weapon hazardous in development and dangerous to its holder; one party renounced it unilaterally on grounds of uncontrollability and low military value, while another kept a large covert programme under the convention (Leitenberg and Zilinskas 2012) | A weapon that endangers its holder can be renounced unilaterally; without verification the hard case persists |
| Non-proliferation and counter-proliferation | Great powers cooperated to limit others (Coe and Vaynman 2015); strikes on others' programmes were considered (Burr and Richelson 2000/01), and several were carried out, mostly not by the cooperating powers (Fuhrmann and Kreps 2010, unchecked) | Partial coalitions enforcing a stop on outsiders; the hard case answered by coercion |
| The stability–instability paradox | Stability among decisive weapons can intensify competition below them (Snyder 1965) | A stop of the decisive class can push competition into cyber operations and influence, the domain of broad E1 |
| Nuclear power after a serious accident (1979 onward) | An industry institution of mutual inspection and ranking produced durable improvements with profit motives unchanged, because one more accident would harm every operator (Rees 1994); later lapses show the durability is not automatic | Durable restraint with unchanged motives where the shared loss returns and no prize is decisive |
| The Montreal Protocol (1987) and its 1990 amendment | Phase-out with trade measures against non-parties, and a fund for developing countries created by the amendment; agreement became possible as substitutes became available (Barrett 2003) | Coverage made attractive by provision and enforced by conditions of access; a low value of winning |

The Cold War ended largely by the internal collapse of one side, accompanied by negotiated reductions in intermediate forces, conventional forces and strategic arms, and its hazards fell with learning (Nye 1987), the case in which residual risk becomes summable. Rivals survived when nature supplied saturation, and gave up capabilities by agreement when reciprocal limits made the extra advantage worth less.

### Crisis windows and standing

Arrangements of this kind are often adopted when a crisis opens a window, not through negotiation among the strong at a time of their choosing (Gunderson and Holling 2002; Olsson et al. 2006). A design prepared in advance, with its functions and protections worked out, is what a crisis can adopt; unprepared responses tend to strengthen the contest, through emergency powers without sunsets and measures aimed at rivals. Smaller jurisdictions, users and people who depend on services controlled by others need standing at every stage, because they cannot leave the shared exposure; the chamber of X10 and the service guarantees are how they get it.
