## S5. Acceleration and the three erosions

### Proposition {{#join}} in full, and proof

*(1) In the objective an actor acts on, if $S_i<P_i$, restraint is never a best response to a racing rival; in the contest of Proposition {{#boundary}} this holds for $r<n/(n-1)$, and in the $n$-player game it also needs restraint against one racer to pay less than racing alongside it. (2) A best response in the objective does not imply $S<P$ in material payoff or true welfare: with $n=2$, $r=1$, $\Omega=c=1$, $\ell=0.1$ and $\theta=-1$, racing is the strict best response in the objective, while staying out gives $S^\pi=-0.05$ against $P^\pi=-0.10$, and with $\kappa=0.2$, $S^W=+0.04$ against $P^W=-0.12$. (3) If falling behind costs $L_b\ge0$ beyond losing the prize, joining instead of staying out gains, at $\theta=0$, $\frac{\Omega+L_b}{n}(1-\frac{r(n-1)}{n})$ for $r\le n/(n-1)$, zero in the fully dissipative limit; with asymmetric costs $c_1<c_2$ the strongest keeps a rent $(\Omega+L_b)(1-c_1/c_2)$ (Hillman and Riley 1989; Baye, Kovenock and de Vries 1996). (4) If the events $T_i>R_i$ are independent with probabilities $\xi_i$, the race begins with probability $1-\prod_i(1-\xi_i)$, which tends to one iff $\sum_i\xi_i$ diverges.*

*Proof.* (2): at $\theta=-1$ intensity is $1/2$ and $P^\pi=-0.1$; against $e=1/2$ zero intensity gives $-0.05$ and the other $0.45$. (3): with penalty $L_b$ the contest has prize $\Omega+L_b$; joining minus staying out is $(\Omega+L_b)/n-(c+\ell)e$ with $e=r(n-1)(\Omega+L_b)/(n^2(c+\ell))$; in the symmetric all-pay contest each player's expected payoff is that of the lowest bid in its support, $-L_b$ (Baye, Kovenock and de Vries 1996), as for two actors with $r\ge2$ (Ewerhart 2015). (4): $\prod(1-\xi_i)\to0$ iff $\sum\xi_i=\infty$. $\square$

### Proof of Proposition {{#dissipate}}

At the symmetric equilibrium the win probabilities sum to one, so total material payoff is $\Omega-ne(c+n\ell)$; substituting $e(\theta)$ gives $s_{\rm dis}$. With stakes $s\Omega$ and loss $s\ell$ the share is $r(n-1-\theta)(c+ns\ell)/(n(c+(1+\theta)s\ell))$, whose derivative in $s$ has the sign of $c\ell(n-1-\theta)>0$, with limit $r(n-1-\theta)/(1+\theta)$. Total payoff stays positive at every scale iff the limit is at most one, $r(n-1-\theta)\le1+\theta$, that is, $\theta\ge\theta_g$. At $\theta=-1$ the share is $r(1+ns\ell/c)$. $\square$ At $\theta=0$ each actor's loss relative to joint restraint is $\frac{r(n-1)}n\frac\Omega n\frac{c+n\ell}{c+\ell}$, which exceeds $\Omega/n$ iff $r(n-1)(c+n\ell)>n(c+\ell)$ ($1.27\,\Omega/n$ for $n=4$, $r=1$, $\ell=0.3c$). If the shared loss per unit did not grow with power, the share would stay constant (1.007 at $\theta=0.4$).

### Remark {{#frontier}} in full

Let a unit of capacity expand into an unclaimed frontier at marginal value $\varphi(F)-c_x-(1+\theta)H/n$, with $\varphi$ falling as the remaining frontier $F$ shrinks; destroy rivals' capacity at value $-c_y-\theta\Delta_y/(n-1)$; or rest. (1) If $\theta\ge-(n-1)c_y/\Delta_y$, war among copies never pays. (2) At $\theta=-1$ expansion stops only where $\varphi=c_x$; at $\theta>-1$ at $\varphi=c_x+(1+\theta)H/n$, the point of enough. (3) At $\theta=-1$ expansion gives way to destruction once $\varphi(F)<c_x+\Delta_y/(n-1)-c_y$, before the frontier is exhausted. *Toy (FS):* four copies of one design with compounding expansion, a finite frontier and a logistic host. At $\theta=-1$ expansion peaks at a stock of 494 near $t=25$; the switch comes with 32% of the frontier left (predicted at $F^\ast=322$, simulated at 320), and total stock then falls to about $10^{-9}$. At $\theta=0$ the copies stop at the point of enough, keep a stock of 518 and never fight; at $\theta=3$ they stop earlier. With asymmetric starts one survivor keeps 17% of the peak. With destruction too costly to pay, no accounting fights, but $\theta=-1$ drives the host to 0.109. One result runs against the narrative: in the run with war, the host's minimum under $\theta=-1$ (0.245) is higher than under $\theta=0$ (0.194), because capacity spent on war is not spent on the host. The parameters were chosen by the author; the toy illustrates the static condition and estimates nothing (compare Hirshleifer 1991; Skaperdas 1992).

### Proof of Proposition {{#enforce}}

Cooperation is worth $C_i/(1-\delta)$. A one-period deviation is worth $C_i+g_i+\delta[p_dD_i/(1-\delta)+(1-p_d)v_i/(1-\delta)]$, where $v_i/(1-\delta)$ is the continuation value of punishment computed in the same game, with breakouts possible during punishment. Multiplying the no-deviation condition by $1-\delta$ and rearranging gives {{eq:enforceeq}}. In this stochastic game with an absorbing state, discounting and bounded payoffs, the one-shot deviation principle holds, so the condition is necessary and sufficient for the given punishment value. That $a^\ast$ is the path of some subgame-perfect equilibrium needs $v_i$ to be the least subgame-perfect continuation value, the extension of Abreu's optimal penal codes to stochastic games with an absorbing state; this is asserted for finite stage games with discounting and flagged for outside review. (2) Solve (1) for $p_d$; the monotonicities hold on the region where $\bar p_{d,i}\in[0,1)$ (100,000 random draws, no violation). (3) The two bounds differ by $\delta p_d(D_i-C_i)$. (4) Set $p_d=1$; with $D_i\ge C_i$ the condition forces $g_i\le-\delta(D_i-C_i)/(1-\delta)\le0$. (5) The punishment phase satisfies $V_P=P+\delta[qD/(1-\delta)+qU/(1-\delta)+(1-2q)V_P]$; solve. $\square$

*Numerics (EC).* Across 200,000 random settings, a third each with $D<C$, $D=C$ and $D>C$, value iteration on an explicit Markov chain agrees with (1) in every case. The bound without the breakout term says "sustainable" when deviation pays in 10,723 of 66,538 cases with $D>C$ and in none with $D\le C$, and says "not sustainable" when restraint is sustainable in 10,032 of 66,842 cases with $D<C$. Both of Codex's counterexamples are reproduced (cooperation worth 30, deviation 22). With $D=4$, $U=0$ and $q=0.1$, the punishment value rises to 1.29, above $C=1$, and restraint is not sustainable at $p_d=0$; with $D=3$, $U=-5$ the punishment is harsher.

### Proof of Corollary {{#relpunish}}

At $\theta=-1$ every symmetric profile gives each objective 0. With two actors, let the punisher play a mixed strategy $y$; the relative objective of a symmetric two-player game is antisymmetric, $A=-A^\top$, so imitating $y$ gives $y^\top Ay=0$. With $n\ge3$ in an anonymous game and all punishers using the same, possibly mixed and independent, strategy, imitation yields an exchangeable profile with expected relative payoff 0. In either case $v_i\ge0=C_i$, and {{eq:enforceeq}} requires $(1-\delta)g_i+\delta p_dD_i\le\delta(1-p_d)(0-v_i)\le0$. $\square$ *Numerics (CR):* over 20,000 random two-player games, mixed imitation gives at most $2\times10^{-16}$ in absolute value; in 3,000 anonymous games with $n\ge3$, no violation. *The role-differentiated counterexample:* three players, actions (restrain or race) × (X or Y), payoff one to a player whose second component is unique, racing gaining 0.1 and costing each other 0.1. All restraining with X gives each 0, and the best deviation gains 1.2. The punishment in which the deviator and one punisher take X and the other punisher Y holds the deviator at $-0.5$ and is a stage equilibrium in objectives, so grim reversion to it is credible, and cooperation is sustained for $\delta\ge1.2/1.7=0.706$. A symmetric punishment could be imitated for 0. Duersch, Oechssler and Schipper (2012) concern a dynamic imitation rule; the corollary uses only that a symmetric two-player zero-sum game has value 0, so rock–paper–scissors games are covered once mixed imitation is allowed (the wording of that citation is to be checked against the source).

### Proof of Corollary {{#restraint}}

With $p_d=0$, {{eq:enforceeq}} reads $(1-\delta)g_i\le\delta(C_i-v_i)$, satisfiable for $C_i>v_i$ and $\delta$ large, given a punishment that does not raise the chance of breakout (else $v_i$ rises, part (5) of Proposition {{#enforce}}); at $\theta=-1$ apply Corollary {{#relpunish}}. With $p_d\to1$ and $D_i\ge C_i$, the condition is $p_d\le\bar p_{d,i}$, and (a) and (b) classify the terms of its expression: (a) lowers $p_d$ directly; (b) raises $\bar p_{d,i}$ through $D_i$ relative to $C_i$, through $v_i$, or through $g_i$. A slow breakout enters with $p_d=\Pr(T_{\rm dec}<\tau)$, with $\tau$ the time to detect and respond. $\square$

**{{!Remark:zonesillus}} (An illustration: the faithful-AI game at the boundary case).** In the binary game of Proposition {{#faithful}} with a common $\theta$, write $A=\epsilon(b-d)-c$, $B=\epsilon(b/(n-1)+d)$, $K=c+n\epsilon d$ and $\Lambda=\delta(1-p_d)/(1-\delta)$. Restraint is a stage equilibrium when $\theta\ge A/B$, and, at the boundary case $D=C$, can be sustained by reversion when $\theta\ge\theta_{\rm rep}=(A-\Lambda K)/(B+\Lambda K)$. With $n=4$, $b=1$, $c=0.3$, $d=0.2$, $\epsilon=1$, $\delta=0.9$: $A/B=0.94$, and $\theta_{\rm rep}$ is $-0.90$, $-0.81$, $-0.32$, $0.63$ and $0.94$ at $p_d=0,0.5,0.9,0.99,1$, so an isolated self loses enforced restraint once $p_d>0.95$. These are the thresholds of the boundary case, not bounds: a decisive position worth more than cooperation raises them (with $D=0.5$ in objective units, $\theta_{\rm rep}$ at $p_d=0.9$ rises from $-0.32$ to $+2.34$), so the restraint gap between wider and narrower objectives grows with $p_d$, the sign Test G registers. In the continuous contest intensity is positive for every $\theta<n-1$, so self-enforcing restraint there needs full inclusion of the contestants. (BI; NB.)

### Proofs of Propositions {{#wingives}} and {{#preventattack}}

*Winner.* With linear payoffs the objective $(1-s)W+\theta sW/(n-1)$ has slope $W(\theta/(n-1)-1)$; with logarithmic utility the first-order condition of $\ln((1-s)W)+\theta\ln(sW/(n-1))$ gives $s=\theta/(1+\theta)$; the promise is Proposition {{#twochan}}(4). (WG; SM(1).)

*Preventive attack, in full.* Two actors bargain over a flow of value 1 per period with discount $\delta$; fighting ends bargaining, A then obtaining the flow with its probability of prevailing less a cost $c_A$ per period; A prevails with probability $w_1$ today and $w_2<w_1$ after B's advantage arrives one period later. (1) Let $x$ be A's per-period share after the shift; fighting now, worth $(w_1-c_A)/(1-\delta)$, is rational if $w_1-c_A>(1-\delta)+\delta x$, and only if when peace gives A the whole flow today. (2) Under (A8), B offers A its reservation value $x=\max(w_2-c_A,0)$. (3) Under other protocols $x$ can reach $\min(w_2+c_B,1)$ and attack can cease to be rational: with $w_1=0.5$, $w_2=0$, $c_A=c_B=0.2$, $\delta=0.8$, A attacks under (A8), while if A sets the future terms peace gives A 1.8 and B 3.2, against 1.5 each from war. (4) If $c_A\ge w_1$, war is worth at most zero. A shift spread over enough periods removes the motive: with $w$ falling from 0.5 to 0 over $N$ periods, $c_A=0.05$, $\delta=0.8$, attack is rational at the start for $N\le3$ and never for $N\ge4$, by backward induction on $V_t=\max(W_t,\delta V_{t+1})$ (`../v4/checks_v44.py`).

### Proofs for the race and growth channels

*Proposition {{#racehazard}}.* By Proposition {{#boundary}}(1), total intensity is $ne(\theta_t)$, which gives $h_t$; survival is $\prod_te^{-h_t}$ by the chain rule. Sharing a fraction $\beta$ of the divisible prize gives the contest with prize $(1-\beta)\Omega_t$ (Proposition {{#share}}(2)), linear in $h_t$; a rebated levy enters at $\tau(n-1-\theta_t)/n$. Since $\ell/(c+(1+\theta)\ell)<1/(1+\theta)$, $\sum_tr_t\Omega_t(n-1-\theta_t)/(1+\theta_t)<\infty$ suffices for $\theta_t>-1$ whatever $\ell_t$. A background hazard from capability growth adds to $h_t$, and no lever removes it. $\square$

*Lemma {{#nonsum}} in full.* Fix one irreversible event; if on every surviving history the residual probability in round $k$ is at least $h_k$ with $\sum_kh_k=\infty$, then $\Pr(\Phi_N)\le\prod_{k\le N}(1-h_k)\le\exp(-\sum_{k\le N}h_k)\to0$, by the chain rule, without independence; if it is at most $\bar h_k<1$ with $\sum\bar h_k<\infty$, survival is at least $\prod(1-\bar h_k)>0$. With $h_k=0.2/k$ survival is 0.34, 0.22 and 0.14 by rounds 100, 1,000 and 10,000; with $0.2/k^2$ it settles at 0.70.

*Proposition {{#tempo}}.* Proportional correction: Hayes (1950). Derivative action extends the range: for $a=1$, $\tau_c=1.5$, gains $k_p=1.02$, $k_d=0.644$ stabilize the mode (`../v4/checks_v42.py`). For the sensitivity bound, with plant $e^{-s\tau_c}/(s-a)$ and any stabilizing linear time-invariant controller, the complementary sensitivity is stable with value 1 at $s=a$; writing it as $e^{-s\tau_c}$ times a stable function and applying the maximum modulus principle gives $\|\mathcal T\|_\infty\ge e^{a\tau_c}$, and robust stability $\|w\mathcal T\|_\infty<1$ gives $|w(a)|e^{a\tau_c}<1$, necessary only: $w(s)=2(s-a)/(s+a)$ has $w(a)=0$ but $\|w\mathcal T\|_\infty\ge2e^{a\tau_c}$. Evidence cannot be acted on before it exists. $\square$

*Why coverage may lag.* Consequences take their time, and tools raise the throughput of observation without making consequences arrive sooner; institutions deliberate at rates set by attention and procedure; learning theoretical models and forming new mechanisms are human processes; and understanding produced with human time, $\dot U=A_UL_U^{\lambda_U}U^{\phi_U}$ with $\phi_U<1$ and bounded labour, grows polynomially (Jones 1995; Bloom et al. 2020). None settles the premise: if AI raises $A_U$ exponentially at rate $g_A$, $U$ grows exponentially at $g_A/(1-\phi_U)$. Measures: the safety of control protocols at a fixed audit budget across generations, and catch rates of planted defects at fixed human hours, controlled for evaluation awareness. Under strict legibility, (coverage lags) is close to definitional for systems beyond human capability, and the empirical content lies in (residual floor), which a safety case with a numerical bound on the uncovered part would state (Clymer et al. 2024).

*Theorem {{#coverage}}.* For $t\ge T_\varepsilon$ the conditional probability of the event is at least $h(1-\varepsilon)$ on every surviving history; the chain rule gives the bound. The converse is the upper part of Lemma {{#nonsum}} with $\bar h_k=\bar hu_k$. (residual floor) gives a liminf of the hazard of at least $h$, not that the hazard tends to $h$. With $u_t=1/(t+2)^2$ coverage stays behind forever and survival with $h=0.05$ stays above 0.98. With $h=0.05$, $\varepsilon=0.1$, survival eighty periods after $T_\varepsilon$ is at most 0.025. $\square$

**Proposition {{#freeze}} in full.** *Let hazardous variety be frozen at $Y_\tau$ from $\tau$ while coverage grows. (1) The exposure after $\tau$ is $\int_\tau^\infty(1-U(t)/Y_\tau)^+dt$: $Y_\tau(1-U(\tau)/Y_\tau)^2/(2\dot U)$ for linear coverage, and $(\ln\varrho-1+1/\varrho)/g_U$ for coverage growing at rate $g_U$, with $\varrho=Y_\tau/U(\tau)>1$. (2) Rolled back to $Y\le U$, exposure stops. (3) If a sprint builds capability $\varrho_X$ times the rivals' level and hazardous variety is a fraction $\varphi$ of capability's, frozen exposure is zero when $\varphi\varrho_X\le1$. (4) For linear coverage the same frozen ratio costs more the later the sprint; this fails for some sub-exponential coverage.* *Proof.* Integrate. For (4), let $\log U$ rise by 0.5 on each interval $[n^2,n^2+1]$ and stay constant otherwise: $\log U\approx0.5\sqrt t=o(t)$, while $\dot U/U=0.5$ inside each interval. $\square$ With hazardous variety equal to capability's, growth 0.3, linear coverage $1+0.5t$ and $h=0.05$, a sprint to $\tau=10$ leaves survival 0.86; freezing then gives 0.53, and rolling back keeps 0.86.

*Proposition {{#enough}}.* After $t_N$, $U_t\ge U_{t_N}+u(t-t_N)$, so the uncovered share is zero from $t_N+Y_N/u$ on, and Lemma {{#nonsum}}'s upper part bounds survival below. The success function is homogeneous of degree zero, so symmetric intensity is proportional to the stakes for every $\theta<n-1$; an objective bounded in the stakes, such as $1-e^{-\Omega}$, has intensity bounded as they grow (SM(8): intensities 0.19, 1.88 and 18.75 for linear payoffs at stakes 1, 10, 100; 0.12, 0.19, 0.19 for the saturating one). $\square$ (PE.)

### Proof of Proposition {{#talk}}

(1) If the sender's and receiver's payoffs sum to zero, each sender type induces, among the equilibrium actions, one that minimizes the receiver's payoff in its state, so the receiver's payoff is at most that of ignoring messages, which it can always do; equilibrium payoffs equal the no-talk values (TK: 300 games, no exception). (2) At $\theta=-1$ with $n$ actors, $J_k=\pi_k-\frac1{n-1}\sum_{j\ne k}\pi_j$, and direct expansion gives $J_S-J_R=\frac n{n-1}(\pi_S-\pi_R)$ and $J_S+J_R=\frac{n-2}{n-1}(\pi_S+\pi_R)-\frac2{n-1}\sum_{k\ne S,R}\pi_k$: effects on third parties enter both alike. In the uniform-quadratic model with $\pi_S=-(y-u-b_0)^2$, $\pi_R=-(y-u)^2$ and third parties' payoffs linear in $y$, the ideal points differ by $b_0n/(n-2)$, independent of the third-party terms, and Crawford and Sobel's condition applies to that bias. (3) A sender that intends to race prefers the receiver to cooperate: with $R=1$, $T=2\varepsilon$, $P=\varepsilon$, $S=0$, it gains $\varepsilon$ from the receiver's cooperation however small $\varepsilon$ is (compare Aumann 1990). $\square$ *Numerics (T3):* the identity holds to $3.6\times10^{-15}$; numerically maximized ideal points match $b_0n/(n-2)$ for $n=3,4,6,10$; a two-message partition equilibrium, built directly with indifferent boundary types and numerical best responses, exists iff the effective bias is below 1/4; with $b_0=0$ and a common cost to third parties, full revelation is an equilibrium.
