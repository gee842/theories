### Narrow E1 and the value of commitment

Deterrence theory holds that removing human discretion from retaliation makes a threat credible: a commitment that cannot be revoked is worth more than one that can (Schelling 1960). Its stronger form is that semi-automated retaliation could relieve the pressure to launch on warning. Five answers follow from the core. The commitment value comes from removing the possibility of human overrule, which is the lock of Lemma {{#lockin}} and, in its domain, a loss of O2. Survivable, human-controlled forces relieve the pressure to launch on warning without removing judgment. An automated commitment cannot tell a false alarm from an attack; several false alarms of early-warning systems are documented, and on 26 September 1983 a satellite early-warning system reported launches that were sunlight reflected from high clouds, and the duty officer judged the alarm false and did not report an attack (Podvig 2002; Hoffman 2009). Automation adds a common risk, escalation at machine speed on a false alarm, that relative selection does not see (Lemma {{#blind}}), so the contest under-prices it. And among actors who price in the lock, keeping AI out of these loops is a strict equilibrium, and doing so verifiably is a costly signal. The strict equilibrium of Proposition {{#delegatelock}} needs the lock to be certain ($q_L=1$), which is the case in the narrow domain, where a delegated decision to use force cannot be overruled once taken; in tactical domains, where the speed advantage is large and the lock uncertain, delegation can be an equilibrium, and bounded, reversible defensive automation falls under the O2 test of Section 6.6.

### Feasibility

Each actor can take part of E1 alone, and taken verifiably it is the signal of Proposition {{#signal}}. E2 and E3 are the most verifiable among rivals, because chips, fabs, datacentres and compute can be observed; algorithmic progress erodes thresholds stated in compute, so with pre-training efficiency doubling about every eight months (90% interval about two to twenty-two months, on data to 2023) a nominal cap must tighten roughly two and a half to three times a year at the central estimate, and across the interval from about one and a half to about sixty-four times a year (EV9), a rate condition verification must meet. Hardware-enabled mechanisms for verifying compute use are proposed and prototyped, not deployed (EV17). E1 is the hardest to verify, since it concerns software and internal procedure, and the most self-enforcing among actors who price in the lock, which is why it can begin as a norm. Staying out of the contest alone while others race can be worse than joining: mutual racing is a strict equilibrium at every horizon at which racing alongside a racer survives better than staying out (Proposition {{#survassure}}), which is why E1 must be held in common and begins with decision loops, where it is each actor's own best response.

### Precedents, corrected against the sources

The fourth column is the limiting condition: where the analogy to AI fails, and so what the precedent does not show.

| Precedent | What made it work or fail | What it shows | Limiting condition for AI |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Acheson–Lilienthal report (1946) | International ownership of the dangerous activity was proposed | Custody of the dangerous activity as the object of agreement | Proposed, not adopted |
| The Baruch plan (1946) | It added that sanctions on violators would not be subject to veto; the other side opposed this and on-site inspection, and the leader would keep its monopoly until control was in place (Bundy 1988) | Custody must precede decisiveness, and enforcement against violators is part of the bargain | The sequence offered was custody after the leader's monopoly, which the other side read as a bid to keep it |
| The first nuclear monopoly and the period of superiority (1945–54) | Preventive war was argued and not carried out; the advantage was small in stockpile and balanced by conventional forces (Trachtenberg 1988/89 for 1949–54; Rosenberg 1983 for the early stockpile) | Restraint where the advantage was not decisive | The advantage was not decisive; a decisive one is the case at issue |
| The ABM Treaty (1972) | Reciprocal limits on defences removed one driver of the offensive race; warheads still multiplied through multiple warheads, which were not limited; the treaty was abandoned in 2002, for reasons that are a matter of interpretation | Reciprocal constraint can change the value of extra advantage; if the withdrawal reflected a renewed value of winning, durability fails when that value rises again | The capability in which one side led was left outside the limit |
| Test bans | The partial ban forbade atmospheric, space and underwater tests while underground testing continued | Not a stop of the decisive class | Slowed one channel of a contest that continued |
| The Outer Space and Seabed treaties | Weapons of mass destruction excluded from orbit and the seabed; military use of space continued | A class of weapons kept out of a domain, close to E1 | A class kept out of a domain, not a domain kept out of the contest |
| The Biological Weapons Convention (1972) | A weapon hazardous in development and dangerous to its holder; one party renounced it unilaterally on grounds of uncontrollability and low military value, while another kept a large covert programme under the convention (Leitenberg and Zilinskas 2012) | A weapon that endangers its holder can be renounced unilaterally; without verification the hard case persists | Low military value, and the renouncer held another deterrent; AI's value to its holder is the racers' premise |
| Non-proliferation and counter-proliferation | Great powers cooperated to limit others (Coe and Vaynman 2015); strikes on others' programmes were considered (Burr and Richelson 2000/01), and several were carried out, mostly not by the cooperating powers (Fuhrmann and Kreps 2010) | Partial coalitions enforcing a stop on outsiders; the hard case answered by coercion | A stop imposed by a leading coalition ends others' O2 without ending the contest among its members; coercion's conditions are those of Section 12.1 |
| The stability–instability paradox | Stability among decisive weapons can intensify competition below them (Snyder 1965) | A stop of the decisive class can push competition into cyber operations and influence, the domain of broad E1 | The precedent's stability rested on saturation; AI's is the question |
| Nuclear power after a serious accident (1979 onward) | An industry institution of mutual inspection and ranking produced durable improvements with profit motives unchanged, because one more accident would harm every operator (Rees 1994); later lapses show the durability is not automatic | Durable restraint with unchanged motives where the shared loss returns and no prize is decisive | No decisive prize, and the loss returned to every operator; the later lapses |
| The Montreal Protocol (1987) and its 1990 amendment | Phase-out with trade measures against non-parties, and a fund for developing countries created by the amendment; agreement became possible as substitutes became available (Barrett 2003) | Coverage made attractive by provision and enforced by conditions of access; a low value of winning | Substitutes were available and the value of winning low; for the decisive class no substitute and, by the racers' premise, a high value |

The Cold War ended largely by the internal collapse of one side, accompanied by negotiated reductions in intermediate forces, conventional forces and strategic arms, and its hazards fell with learning (Nye 1987), the case in which residual risk becomes summable. Rivals survived when nature or balancing supplied saturation, and gave up capabilities by agreement when reciprocal limits made the extra advantage worth less. Balancing by others is a saturation mechanism that does not depend on the technology, and whether it operates for AI is crux C5; if advantage in AI is unsaturated, as the racers' own premise holds, none of these precedents supplies the saturation, and safety has to come from changing what is selected before advantage becomes decisive.

### Crisis windows and standing

Arrangements of this kind are often adopted when a crisis opens a window, not through negotiation among the strong at a time of their choosing (Gunderson and Holling 2002; Olsson et al. 2006). A design prepared in advance, with its functions and protections worked out, is what a crisis can adopt; unprepared responses tend to strengthen the contest, through emergency powers without sunsets and measures aimed at rivals, and a measure adopted in a crisis without a stated end condition is the kind of step that Section 11.4's ratchet runs backward: held past its need, it becomes the lock-in the next reform must repay. Smaller jurisdictions, users and people who depend on services controlled by others need standing at every track, because they cannot leave the shared exposure; the chamber of X10 and the service guarantees are how they get it.
