## S3. One system

### Hosts harsher than the forgiving one

Real shared conditions, such as trust, institutions, information environments and skills, are closer to hosts that degrade by themselves below a threshold, and they are vectors of coupled components. For a threshold host a load later removed can leave it collapsed, and with two coupled components collapse can begin at half the load at which one would fail while their average reads as healthy. Comparison results transfer in one direction only. A host whose regeneration per unit is no greater than $\rho(1-V)$ at every level, such as $\rho_A(V/A-1)(1-V)$ with $\rho_A=\rho A/(1-A)$, makes every *crossing* result hold a fortiori. *Viability* results do not transfer, and need a margin even on the forgiving host: {{safeguards}} keeps the trajectory above the floor only with a liability margin set by the host, the floor, the initial composition and the speed of selection; the forgiving host dips below its floor without the margin and recovers, while a host that degrades by itself below a threshold does not recover (S10). A host normalized to the same peak regeneration is not harsher in this sense, since above about a third of capacity it regenerates faster.

### Proof of Lemma {{#blind}}

(1) A relative rule satisfies $\mathcal F(p,\pi+G\mathbf 1)=\mathcal F(p,\pi)$ at every instant, so shares solve the same equation whatever $G(V(t))$ is; a common term leaves every $\pi_k-\pi_l$, and so the sign of relative growth, unchanged.

(2) An equal additive cost is a common term among those who bear it. Costs $c_k$ differing across carriers change $\pi_k-\pi_l$ by $c_l-c_k$, which a payoff-monotone rule selects on; the carrier the constraint costs least gains share among the compliers, and a carrier that alone bears a cost loses share to every other.

(3) *Within a type.* An evader of type $k$ pays the expected sanction $\bar F_k$ instead of the cost $c_k$, so its payoff exceeds that of a complier of its own type by $c_k-\bar F_k$. Under a payoff-monotone rule the ratio of the evading to the complying variant of type $k$ rises iff $c_k>\bar F_k$. *Prevailing.* If every such difference is negative, then under cumulative selection, in which the log-ratio of two variants moves with the integral of their payoff gap as under replicator dynamics, each evading variant's share relative to its own type's complying variant tends to zero, so the compliant share tends to one whatever the payoffs across types, which only re-sort the compliers. *Not necessary across types.* The converse fails across types: a type whose evasion pays is still eliminated, evaders included, if another type's compliers earn more than its evaders. *Falling sanction.* With one type and $\bar F(x)$ continuous and strictly decreasing in the evading share $x$, $\dot x=x(1-x)\big(c-\bar F(x)\big)$. A root $x^\dagger\in(0,1)$ of $\bar F(x)=c$ exists iff $\bar F(0)>c>\bar F(1)$; it is unique and unstable, so full compliance and full evasion are both stable and $x^\dagger$ separates their basins. Otherwise $c-\bar F$ has one sign on $(0,1)$: compliance is globally stable if $\bar F(1)\ge c$, evasion if $\bar F(0)\le c$. $\square$

*Numerics (CI).* With costs $(0.3,0.1)$ relative growth rates are $(-0.1,+0.1)$; with an equal cost 0.2, $(0,0)$. With $c=0.3$ evaders take over when the expected sanction is 0.25 or 0.27 and vanish when it is 0.35 or 0.4. Detection funded by compliers, $\bar F(x)=q_0(1-x)F$ with $q_0=0.8$ and $F=0.6$, gives the tipping share $1-c/(q_0F)=0.375$: starts at evading shares 0.05 and 0.2 return to full compliance, and 0.4 and 0.6 go to full evasion. The endpoints decide, as stated: with $c=0.3$, $\bar F=0.5+0.5(1-x)$ restores compliance from every start, $\bar F=0.24(1-x)$ leads to evasion from every start, and $\bar F=0.48(1-x)$ and $1.2(1-x)$ are bistable with tipping shares 0.375 and 0.75. Two types with compliance costs 0.1 and 0.2: with sanctions 1 and 1, compliance goes to one and all of it is the cheaper type, the dearer complier being displaced (from equal shares the compliant share is 0.99999997 at $t=20$); with sanctions 1 and 0.15, type B's evasion pays, yet its evaders lose to type A's compliers and compliance still goes to one; with sanctions 1 and 0.05, B's evaders earn most and evasion takes over. Equal costs leave the compliers' relative shares where they started. A common liability $\tau=1.5>\nu=1$ drives the high-load share to zero; $\tau=0.5$ drives it to one.

### Proof of Proposition {{#load}}

With $\phi_k=\phi_l$, $\pi_k-\pi_l=(\nu-\tau_{\rm eff})(e_k-e_l)>0$ and $G$ cancels. Under replicator dynamics $\dot E=\operatorname{Cov}_p(e,\pi)=(\nu-\tau_{\rm eff})\operatorname{Var}_p(e)+\operatorname{Cov}_p(e,\phi)$, so the mean load rises unless restraint is correlated strongly enough with other advantages; a rising mean does not imply that the maximal-load carrier gains share (shares $1/3$, loads $(0,0.9,1)$, other advantages $(0,10,0)$, $\nu=1$: the mean load rises at 1.09 while the maximal-load share falls at 0.99). With all $\phi_k$ equal, $\frac{d}{dt}\ln(p_k/p_l)=(e_k-e_l)(\nu-\tau_{\rm eff})$, so $B(t)\to\infty$ sends a race carrier's share to one and $E\to\bar e$. For $\epsilon>0$, eventually $\dot V\le V[\rho(1-V)-h_V(\bar e-\epsilon)]$, so $\limsup V\le(1-h_V(\bar e-\epsilon)/\rho)^+$; let $\epsilon\to0$, and (reach) puts the bound below $V_{\min}$. If $B(\infty)<\infty$, each $\ln(p_k/p_l)$ converges. $\square$

### Proof of Proposition {{#twochan}}, and measuring $\theta$

With $\Delta\bar\pi_{-i}=(n\bar\Delta-\Delta\pi_i)/(n-1)$, $\Delta J_i=\frac{n-1-\theta}{n-1}\Delta\pi_i+\frac{n\theta}{n-1}\bar\Delta$; writing $\Delta\pi_i=\bar\Delta+(\Delta\pi_i-\bar\Delta)$ the coefficient of $\bar\Delta$ is $1+\theta$. (1): each $\pi_j$ appears in $n-1$ averages, so $\sum_iJ_i=(1+\theta)\sum_i\pi_i$. (2): at $\theta=-1$ objectives sum to zero at every profile; at $\theta=n-1$ each objective is the total, so a total-maximizing profile admits no profitable deviation. (3): $\pi_k-\sum_jp_j\pi_j=(1-p_k)[\pi_k-\sum_{j\ne k}\frac{p_j}{1-p_k}\pi_j]$; and $\pi_i+\lambda(\pi_i-\bar\pi)=(1+\lambda\frac{n-1}n)\pi_i-\lambda\frac{n-1}n\bar\pi_{-i}$. (4): a rebated levy is a transfer with $\bar\Delta=0$ and own part $-\tau(n-1)/n$; withholding a promised $\beta\Omega$ from the $n-1$ others gives own part $\beta\Omega$ and $\bar\Delta=0$, hence $\beta\Omega(n-1-\theta)/(n-1)$; if the promised share was to be divided among all $n$, the gain is $\beta\Omega(n-1-\theta)/n$. (5): $\Delta\pi_i=g-H/n$ and the others' $-H/n$, so $\Delta J_i=g-(1+\theta)H/n$. An act of spite that costs its taker $c_y$ and the others $\Delta_y$ in total pays iff $\theta<-(n-1)c_y/\Delta_y$. $\square$ (TC: 4,000 random games, an identity check.)

**Measuring the weight.** In the contest of Section 3.4 the symmetric intensity depends on the private cost $c$ and the shared loss $\ell$ only through $c+(1+\theta)\ell$, so the ratio of its response to the shared loss to its response to private cost is exactly $1+\theta$, the weight of the common channel; numerically 0.50004, 0.99996, 0.56997 and 1.99960 against $1+\theta=0.5$, 1, 0.57 and 2 [num: SP]. This is the observable Test Γ needs (Section 7.4). A second identification sets a change in the shared loss, which enters through the common channel, against a rebated levy, which enters through the differential one: the ratio of the two responses is $n(1+\theta)/(n-1-\theta)$ [num: TH]. If actors perceive only a share $\psi$ of the shared loss, both ratios are scaled by $\psi$: what is identified is $(1+\theta)\psi$, and with half perceived a true $\theta=0$ reads as $-0.43$ [num: SM(3)]. Separating $\theta$ from $\psi$ needs a third perturbation that changes the visibility of shared harm without changing its size, or an independent measure of perceived harm. The identification fails exactly where the theory says shared harm goes unseen.

### Full statement and proof of Proposition {{#wow}}

*Let $n$ symmetric developers with $\tau_{\rm eff}=0$ and the host at steady state maximize $u_k=\pi_k+\lambda(\pi_k-\bar\pi)$, with $G(V)=g_VV$, $h_V\bar e<\rho$ and $\Upsilon=g_Vh_V/(\rho\nu)$; write $w_\lambda=1+\lambda(n-1)/n$. (1) Restraint is dominant if $w_\lambda<\Upsilon/n$, depletion if reversed. (2) If $\Upsilon>1$, joint payoff is maximized by full restraint while the equilibrium is full depletion whenever $\lambda>(\Upsilon-n)/(n-1)$, at every $\lambda$ when $n>\Upsilon$. (3) Every developer then loses $\nu\bar e(\Upsilon-1)$. (4) Under (reach) the equilibrium host is below the floor.*

*Proof.* $\pi_k-\bar\pi$ gives $G$ weight zero, so $\partial u_k/\partial e_k=w_\lambda\nu-g_Vh_V/(n\rho)$, independent of others' choices. Joint payoff at a common $e$ has slope $n\nu(1-\Upsilon)$. Payoffs are $\nu\bar e+g_V(1-h_V\bar e/\rho)$ at full depletion and $g_V$ under restraint. $\square$

**{{!Remark:thrrep}} (Thresholds and repetition need observation).** If crossing the floor costs each a common loss $L$, a developer pivotal for crossing restrains when $L>(w_\lambda-\Upsilon/n)\nu\bar e$, and the game becomes coordination at the threshold (Barrett 2013). Joint restraint is sustained by reversion whenever $\delta\ge(w_\lambda-\Upsilon/n)/(w_\lambda-\Upsilon/n+\Upsilon-1)$: for $\Upsilon=3$, $n=4$ the cutoff is 0.11 at $\lambda=0$, 0.47 at $\lambda=2$ and 0.95 at $\lambda=50$. Uncertainty about the threshold turns coordination back into a dilemma (Barrett and Dannenberg 2012), and noisy monitoring makes reversion costly (Green and Porter 1984).

### Proof of Proposition {{#boundary}}

Write $\pi_i=w_i\Omega_e-ce_i-\ell\mathcal E+w_i\Omega_s$: the first three terms are the divisible part, the last the positional part. (1) Expanding, an objective with one weight $\theta$ on all payoffs is $J_i=\Omega(1-\frac{\theta}{n-1})w_i-(c+(1+\theta)\ell)e_i+$ terms independent of $e_i$: a contest with prize $\Omega(n-1-\theta)/(n-1)$ and unit cost $c+(1+\theta)\ell$, whose symmetric pure equilibrium for $r\le n/(n-1)$ gives $e(\theta)$ (Pérez-Castrillo and Verdier 1992). At a symmetric profile $\pi^{\rm e}=\Omega_e/n-e(c+n\ell)$ and $\pi^{\rm s}=\Omega_s/n$, so $W=(1+\kappa)\pi^{\rm e}+(1+\kappa_s)\pi^{\rm s}$, which falls with $e$ and therefore rises with $\theta$. (2) As a function of $e_i$ with the others fixed, $W_i$ is a contest objective with prize $\big[\Omega_e(n-1-\kappa)+\Omega_s(n-1-\kappa_s)\big]/(n-1)$ and unit cost $c+(1+\kappa)\ell$: it is the objective that weighs the others' divisible payoffs by $\kappa$ and their positional payoffs by $\kappa_s$, and its best response maximizes $W_i$. Lowering the divisible weight below $\kappa$ raises the marginal value of intensity by $\Omega_e\,(\partial w_i/\partial e_i)/(n-1)+\ell>0$ per unit of weight, so the chosen intensity moves away from the welfare maximizer whenever it moves. On the positional part, $\kappa_s\le0$ makes the positional prize count at least fully: a rival's positional gain is the holder's loss, and relative concern there is accurate. (3) A finite-population evolutionarily stable intensity maximizes $\pi_i-\bar\pi_{-i}$ (Schaffer 1988), the case $\theta=-1$; at $r=1$ it dissipates the prize fully (Hehenkamp, Leininger and Possajennikov 2004), and with $\ell>0$ every actor ends below zero. For $r>1$ asymmetric equilibria with lower total intensity also exist in a band below $n/(n-1)$; the symmetric one is used throughout. $\square$ (RH, CB.)

**Which preferences evolve.** It depends on the population: with unobserved preferences in large random populations, material self-interest (Ok and Vega-Redondo 2001; Dekel, Ely and Yilankaya 2007); with observed preferences and at least three actors in this contest, a commitment to a narrower objective (Güth and Yaari 1992; Heifetz, Shannon and Spiegel 2007); with high observability and assortative matching, cooperative preferences (Alger and Weibull 2013). Local competition favours spite (Alger, Weibull and Lehmann 2020), and an observable commitment to aggression is itself a race (Dixit 1987; Fershtman and Judd 1987).

### Full form and proof of Proposition {{#standprice}}

*Full form.* In the model of Proposition {{#loopgain}} let the prize depend on the total stocks $S^j=\sum_iX^j_i$, with $\Omega'_j=\partial\Omega/\partial S^j\ge0$, and take as reference a symmetric steady state or, where the prize is proportional to the stocks, the balanced path. Write
$$\Gamma_\delta=(1-\beta)\sum_j\frac{\delta\kappa_jr_j\,\Omega/S^j}{1-\delta(1-d_j-\delta_{f,j})},\qquad \Gamma^{\rm c}_\delta=\sum_j\frac{\delta\kappa_j\Omega'_j}{1-\delta(1-d_j)},$$
the relative and the common loop gain, both evaluated at the reference state and both below one. At a fixed-prize steady state $\kappa_j\Omega/S^j=\omega_jd_j$, so $\Gamma_\delta$ is the discounted loop gain of Proposition {{#loopgain}}(3), and $\Gamma^{\rm c}_\delta=0$. Then $\lambda_{\rm rel}=\Gamma_\delta/(1-\Gamma_\delta)$ and $\lambda_{\rm com}=\Gamma^{\rm c}_\delta/(1-\Gamma^{\rm c}_\delta)$, and {{eq:thetagamma}} reads
$$\frac{1+\theta}{n-1-\theta}=\frac{1+\theta_0}{n-1-\theta_0}\cdot\frac{1-\Gamma_\delta}{1-\Gamma^{\rm c}_\delta}:$$
the working self-model narrows iff the relative loop gain exceeds the common one.

*Decomposition.* Split a change in today's net payoffs into its common part $\bar\Delta$ and the relative part $y=\Delta\pi_i-\bar\Delta$, and let every actor convert net payoff into stock $j$ at the rate $\kappa_j$. To first order the two parts evolve separately: the prize depends on the totals, and a relative shift leaves the totals unchanged.

*Relative part.* The actor's stocks rise by $\kappa_jy$ and the others' fall by as much in total. Relative deviations follow the Jacobian $\mathcal D+\frac1n\mathbf u\mathbf v^\top$ of S2, with $\frac1nu_jv_j=(1-\beta)\kappa_jr_j\Omega/S^j$ at the reference state. By the Sherman–Morrison argument of S2, own discounted future winnings rise by $\lambda_{\rm rel}y$ with $\lambda_{\rm rel}=\Gamma_\delta/(1-\Gamma_\delta)$. Since the totals are unchanged, the others' winnings fall by $\lambda_{\rm rel}y$ in total.

*Common part.* Every stock $j$ rises by $\kappa_j\bar\Delta$ per actor. Common deviations $c^j$ per actor follow $c^{j\prime}=(1-d_j)c^j+\kappa_j\sum_k\Omega'_kc^k$, a rank-one Jacobian $\operatorname{diag}(1-d_j)+\boldsymbol\kappa\boldsymbol\Omega'^\top$ on which diffusion does not act. Each actor's winnings rise by $\sum_k\Omega'_kc^k_t$ in period $t$, and by Sherman–Morrison the discounted sum is $\lambda_{\rm com}\bar\Delta$ with $\lambda_{\rm com}=\Gamma^{\rm c}_\delta/(1-\Gamma^{\rm c}_\delta)$.

*Matching.* The others' average future winnings change by $\lambda_{\rm com}\bar\Delta-\lambda_{\rm rel}y/(n-1)$, so the total change in the objective with weight $\theta_0$ is
$$\Delta J_i=(1+\theta_0)(1+\lambda_{\rm com})\,\bar\Delta+\frac{n-1-\theta_0}{n-1}(1+\lambda_{\rm rel})\,y .$$
Matching it to $(1+\theta)\bar\Delta+\frac{n-1-\theta}{n-1}y$ up to a positive factor gives {{eq:thetagamma}}. With $\varrho=\frac{(1+\theta_0)(1+\lambda_{\rm com})}{(n-1-\theta_0)(1+\lambda_{\rm rel})}$, explicitly $\theta=\big(\varrho(n-1)-1\big)/(1+\varrho)$. The map $\theta\mapsto(1+\theta)/(n-1-\theta)$ is increasing on $[-1,n-1)$, so $\theta<\theta_0$ iff $(1+\lambda_{\rm com})/(1+\lambda_{\rm rel})<1$, and $\theta$ rises with $\theta_0$: a wider held self-model narrows less, and none short of $\theta_0=n-1$ escapes the narrowing.

(1) With a fixed prize every $\Omega'_j=0$, so $\lambda_{\rm com}=0$; $1+\lambda_{\rm rel}=1/(1-\Gamma_\delta)$ makes $\varrho=(1+\theta_0)(1-\Gamma_\delta)/(n-1-\theta_0)$, which gives $\theta_\Gamma$; as $\Gamma_\delta\to1^-$, $\varrho\to0$ and $\theta\to-1$. In Proposition {{#twochan}} the host's visibility $(1+\theta)/n$ and the point of enough $(1+\theta)H/n$ fall with $\theta$, while a rebated levy $\tau(n-1-\theta)/n$ and the gain from breaking a promise $\beta\Omega(n-1-\theta)/(n-1)$ rise. (2) With $\Omega=\Omega'S$ in one channel and $k_0=\delta_f=0$, $\Gamma_\delta=\delta(1-\beta)r\kappa_c\Omega'/(1-\delta(1-d_K))$ and $\Gamma^{\rm c}_\delta=\delta\kappa_c\Omega'/(1-\delta(1-d_K))$, whose ratio is $(1-\beta)r$; Proposition {{#growprize}} gives the instability condition and the finite horizons. $\square$

**{{!Proposition:growprize}} (Stakes that grow with capacity) [thm].** *In the one-channel model of Proposition {{#compound}} without provision, let the prize be $\Omega=\Omega_0+\Omega'S$ with $S=\sum_iK_i$, a share $\beta$ of it divided equally; let actors convert net payoff into capacity at the rate $\kappa_c$ and discount at $\delta$.*
1. *Two periods. If first-period net payoffs become second-period capacity, then at equal standing $\lambda_{\rm rel}=\delta\kappa_c(1-\beta)r\,\Omega_2/S$ and $\lambda_{\rm com}=\delta\kappa_c\Omega'$, with $\Omega_2$ the second-period prize, so $\lambda_{\rm rel}/\lambda_{\rm com}=(1-\beta)r\big(1+\Omega_0/(\Omega'S)\big)$. A part of the stakes that does not grow with capacity adds to the relative return only; with $\Omega_0=0$ the ratio is $(1-\beta)r$.*
2. *Infinite horizon. If $\Omega=\Omega'S$ and capacity depreciates at $d_K$ and diffuses at $\delta_f$, then along the balanced path, on which $S$ grows by the factor $1-d_K+\kappa_c\Omega'$ a period, $\lambda_{\rm rel}=\Gamma_\delta/(1-\Gamma_\delta)$ and $\lambda_{\rm com}=\Gamma^{\rm c}_\delta/(1-\Gamma^{\rm c}_\delta)$ with $\Gamma_\delta=\delta(1-\beta)r\kappa_c\Omega'/\big(1-\delta(1-d_K-\delta_f)\big)$ and $\Gamma^{\rm c}_\delta=\delta\kappa_c\Omega'/\big(1-\delta(1-d_K)\big)$, both below one. The working self-model narrows iff $(1-\beta)r>1+\delta\delta_f/\big(1-\delta(1-d_K)\big)$, and equal shares are unstable iff $(1-\beta)r>1+\delta_f/(\kappa_c\Omega')$.*
3. *Without diffusion both conditions are $(1-\beta)r>1$, and over every horizon a relative unit is worth more than a common one exactly when $(1-\beta)r>1$. With diffusion the narrowing threshold lies strictly below the compounding one, their gap being $\delta_f(1-\Gamma^{\rm c}_\delta)/(\kappa_c\Omega')$; between them looking ahead narrows the self while equal shares are stable.*

*Proof.* (1) Future winnings are $w_i=\Omega_2\big[(1-\beta)p_i+\beta/n\big]$ with $p_i=K_i^r/\sum_jK_j^r$ and $\Omega_2=\Omega_0+\Omega'\sum_jK_j$, and each actor reinvests $\kappa_c\Delta\pi_j$. At equal standing $\partial p_i/\partial K_i=r(n-1)/(nS)$ and $\partial p_i/\partial K_j=-r/(nS)$, so $\Delta w_i=(1-\beta)r\,\Omega_2\,(x_i-\bar x)/S+\Omega'\bar x$ with $x_j=\kappa_c\Delta\pi_j$; discounting gives the two returns. (2) A relative unit raises the actor's capacity by $\kappa_c$ and lowers the others' by as much in total; its own winnings rise by $(1-\beta)r\Omega'$ per unit of capacity deviation, and the deviation is multiplied each period by $1-d_K-\delta_f+(1-\beta)r\kappa_c\Omega'$. A common unit raises every capacity by $\kappa_c$; each actor's winnings rise by $\Omega'$ per unit, and the deviation is multiplied by $1-d_K+\kappa_c\Omega'$. Summing the discounted geometric series gives the closed forms. Narrowing is $\Gamma_\delta>\Gamma^{\rm c}_\delta$, that is $(1-\beta)r\big(1-\delta(1-d_K)\big)>1-\delta(1-d_K-\delta_f)$. Shares are unstable iff the relative multiplier exceeds the common one, $\kappa_c\Omega'\big((1-\beta)r-1\big)>\delta_f$. (3) Without diffusion the ratio of the relative to the common increment in period $t$ is $(1-\beta)r\,m^{t-1}$ with $m=\big(1-d_K+(1-\beta)r\kappa_c\Omega'\big)/\big(1-d_K+\kappa_c\Omega'\big)$, and both factors exceed one exactly when $(1-\beta)r>1$, so every partial sum compares the same way. The gap between the thresholds is $\delta_f/(\kappa_c\Omega')-\delta\delta_f/\big(1-\delta(1-d_K)\big)=\delta_f(1-\Gamma^{\rm c}_\delta)/(\kappa_c\Omega')$, positive inside the domain. $\square$

**The two errors.** For Remark {{#twoerrors}}: $\theta_\Gamma(\kappa)=0$ iff $n\kappa=(n-1)(1+\kappa)\Gamma_\delta$, so an actor whose held weight is the coupling acts on a weight below the isolated self once $\Gamma_\delta>n\kappa/\big((n-1)(1+\kappa)\big)$: 0.222, 0.381 and 0.667 for $\kappa=0.2$, 0.4 and 1 at $n=4$; 0.333 and 0.571 for $\kappa=0.2$ and 0.4 at $n=2$ [num: SP].

In a tug-of-war contest total effort is highest at parity and falls to 41% and 1.3% of it at leads of one and two steps. With a strong motive to catch up, the equilibrium elasticity falls below the structural one ($0.993r$). When contests run in many small rounds, a winner-take-all equilibrium coexists with the smooth one, with a price 12.5 times the structural value at $\Gamma=0.2$ and 8.8 times at 0.3; at coarser rounds only the smooth one is found. The multiplicity is held for outside review.

*Bounds.* First order. The infinite horizon with a prize that has both a fixed and a growing part, and depreciation, is not closed here; the two-period form covers mixed prizes. A myopic actor has $\lambda_{\rm rel}=0$, but selection still favours $\theta=-1$ (Proposition {{#boundary}}(3)).

### Proof of Lemma {{#onegroup}}

Under any partition into groups $g$, the Price equation gives $\bar w\Delta\bar z=\operatorname{Cov}_g(\bar w_g,\bar z_g)+\operatorname E_g\operatorname{Cov}_{j\in g}(w_j,z_j)+\operatorname E[w\Delta z]$. Let the trait cost its bearer $c>0$ and give every member a benefit $b\bar z$ equal across members, so that $\pi_j=\pi_0+b\bar z-cz_j$ is the same decreasing function of $z_j$ for everyone. A payoff-monotone rule orders growth as payoff, so fitness is $w_j=\varphi(\pi_j)$ with $\varphi$ increasing on the payoffs present, and $w_j$ is a decreasing function of $z_j$ alone. By Chebyshev's sum inequality, $\operatorname{Cov}(w,z)\le0$ in the population and within every group. Where $\varphi$ is affine, $\bar w_g=\varphi(\pi_0+b\bar z-c\bar z_g)$ is decreasing in $\bar z_g$ and the between-group term is $\le0$ as well. Where $\varphi$ is not affine, $\bar w_g$ depends on the spread within the group as well as its mean (Jensen), and the between-group term can be positive; the total is unchanged, since the decomposition is an identity. The trait can rise only if the transmission term is positive, by biased transmission, or if a rule changes the bearer's relative payoff. If the benefit goes only to the bearer's group, $\bar w_g$ contains $b\bar z_g$, and under the partition that matches its reach the between-group term can be positive. $\square$

*Numerics (PR).* With 600 individuals in 30 units and five partitions (one group, units, five blocs, fifty random groups, individuals), the between-group term under a global benefit is $0$, $-0.0019$, $-0.00002$, $-0.0011$ and $-0.0123$; under a benefit local to units it is $+0.0097$ at the unit partition. With nonlinear increasing fitness, over 3,000 random partitioned populations for each of six maps (affine, $e^{5\pi}$, $\log$, $\pi^3$, logistic, near-step), the total and every within-group term are positive in none; the between-group term is positive in none under affine fitness and in 3 to 346 of 3,000 under the nonlinear maps; in two reviewers' instances it is $+0.017$ with total $-0.364$, and $+0.0092$ with total $-0.115$. Biased transmission $\dot z=-cz(1-z)+\mu_{\rm tr}(1-z)$ settles at $\mu_{\rm tr}/c$. In an illustration with a base hazard of 0.03 a period, learning only from failures suffers an irreversible loss within 400 periods with probability 0, 0.14, 0.46 and 1.0 as the irreversible share of failures is 0, 0.05, 0.2 and 1, against 0, 0.06, 0.22 and 0.70 under adoption in advance: an illustration, not an estimate.

### Proof of Proposition {{#club}}

(1) Contestants who weigh one another fully maximize $\Omega(\mathcal E)-c\mathcal E-n\ell\mathcal E$; the system subtracts $m_o\ell_o\mathcal E$ too; strict concavity orders the solutions. With $\Omega=10\sqrt{\mathcal E}$, $c=1$, $n=4$, $\ell=0.3$, $m_o=20$, $\ell_o=0.3$, the club's intensity is 5.17 against 0.37, and the harm to non-contestants 31.0 against 2.2 [num: CB]. (2) A bloc of $n_b$ equal members acting as one maximizes its members' summed payoff: it receives their summed prize share and bears the shared loss $n_b\ell$ per unit of total intensity, so the game among the $n/n_b$ blocs is the original game with $n/n_b$ players and loss $n_b\ell$. At $r=1$ the bloc intensities are 0.1563, 0.1389 and 0.1316 for two blocs of two, three blocs of two and two blocs of three, as that game predicts [num: SM(2)]. With unequal sizes and $r\ne1$, equal total effort gives a bloc of three $3^{1-r}$ times a single contestant's strength (1.732 at $r=0.5$, 0.577 at $r=1.5$). $\square$
