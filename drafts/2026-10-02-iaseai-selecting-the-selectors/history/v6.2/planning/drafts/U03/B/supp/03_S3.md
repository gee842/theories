## S3. One system

### Hosts harsher than the forgiving one

Real shared conditions, such as trust, institutions, information environments and skills, are closer to hosts that degrade by themselves below a threshold, and they are vectors of coupled components. For a threshold host a load later removed can leave it collapsed, and with two coupled components collapse can begin at half the load at which one would fail while their average reads as healthy. Comparison results transfer in one direction only. A host whose regeneration per unit is no greater than $\rho(1-V)$ at every level, such as $\rho_A(V/A-1)(1-V)$ with $\rho_A=\rho A/(1-A)$, makes every *crossing* result hold a fortiori. *Viability* results do not transfer: Theorem {{#safeguards}} needs a liability margin on such hosts (S10). A host normalized to the same peak regeneration is not harsher in this sense, since above about a third of capacity it regenerates faster.

### Proof of Lemma {{#blind}}

(1) A relative rule satisfies $F(p,\pi+G\mathbf 1)=F(p,\pi)$ at every instant, so shares solve the same equation whatever $G(V(t))$ is; a common term leaves every $\pi_k-\pi_l$, and so the sign of relative growth, unchanged.

(2) An equal additive cost among compliers is a common term among them. Costs $c_k$ differing across carriers change $\pi_k-\pi_l$ by $c_l-c_k$, which a payoff-monotone rule selects on: the compliers are re-sorted by the incidence of the rule, and a cost borne by one carrier alone lowers its payoff against every other.

(3) *Within a type.* An evader of type $k$ pays the expected sanction $\bar F_k$ instead of $c_k$, so its payoff exceeds a complier's of its type by $c_k-\bar F_k$, and under cumulative payoff-monotone selection the evading variant grows relative to the complying variant iff $c_k>\bar F_k$. *Sufficiency.* If $\bar F_k\ge c_k$ for every type that can evade, no evading variant grows against its own compliers; when the inequalities are strict each evading share, relative to its own complying share, tends to zero, so the compliant share tends to one whatever the payoffs across types. *Not necessary across types.* A type whose evasion pays, $c_k>\bar F_k$, is still eliminated if another type's compliers earn more than its evaders do, so the per-type condition is sufficient and not necessary for compliance to prevail. *Falling sanction.* With one type and $\bar F(x)$ continuous and decreasing in the evading share $x$, the evading share follows $\dot x=x(1-x)\big(c-\bar F(x)\big)$. A root $x^\dagger\in(0,1)$ exists iff $\bar F(0)>c>\bar F(1)$, by the intermediate value theorem, and it is unstable because $c-\bar F$ is increasing; full compliance and full evasion are then both stable, and $x^\dagger$ separates them. If $\bar F(1)\ge c$, then $c-\bar F<0$ on $(0,1)$ and compliance is globally stable; if $\bar F(0)\le c$, evasion is. $\square$

*Numerics [num: CI].* With costs $(0.3,0.1)$ relative growth rates are $(-0.1,+0.1)$; with an equal cost 0.2, $(0,0)$; a universal cap on loads $(1,0.6,0.2)$ at 0.4 costs the three carriers $(0.6,0.2,0)$ and reverses their ranking. With $c=0.3$ evaders take over when the expected sanction is 0.25 or 0.27 and vanish when it is 0.35 or 0.4. With detection funded by compliers, $\bar F(x)=q_0(1-x)F$, $q_0=0.8$, $F=0.6$, the tipping share is $1-c/(q_0F)=0.375$: starts at 0.05 and 0.2 return to full compliance; 0.4 and 0.6 go to full evasion. A common liability $\tau=1.5>\nu=1$ drives the high-load share to zero; $\tau=0.5$ drives it to one. *The endpoint cases and unequal incidence* [num: CI, extended from the memo scripts f4(A) and m05(A, B)]: with $c=0.3$, the schedule $0.48(1-x)$ is bistable with tipping share 0.375 and $0.6-0.4x$ with tipping share 0.75; $0.24(1-x)$, which starts below the cost, sends every start to full evasion; $0.5+0.5(1-x)$, which never falls below it, sends every start to full compliance. With two types of compliance cost $(0.1,0.2)$ and sanctions $(1,1)$ the compliant share tends to one and the cheaper type takes the whole of it, which is incidence re-sorting the compliers; with sanctions $(1,0.15)$, where the second type's evasion pays, compliance still tends to one because the first type's compliers out-earn the second type's evaders; with sanctions $(1,0.05)$ evasion takes over. These are Codex's boundary case of unequal incidence and the cross-type case of the lemma.

### Proof of Proposition {{#load}}

With $\phi_k=\phi_l$, $\pi_k-\pi_l=(\nu-\tau_{\rm eff})(e_k-e_l)>0$ and $G$ cancels. Under replicator dynamics $\dot E=\operatorname{Cov}_p(e,\pi)=(\nu-\tau_{\rm eff})\operatorname{Var}_p(e)+\operatorname{Cov}_p(e,\phi)$, so the mean load rises unless restraint is correlated strongly enough with other advantages; a rising mean does not imply that the maximal-load carrier gains share (shares $1/3$, loads $(0,0.9,1)$, other advantages $(0,10,0)$, $\nu=1$: the mean load rises at 1.09 while the maximal-load share falls at 0.99). With equal $\phi$, $\frac{d}{dt}\ln(p_k/p_l)=(e_k-e_l)(\nu-\tau_{\rm eff})$, so $B(t)\to\infty$ sends a race carrier's share to one and $E\to\bar e$. For $\epsilon>0$, eventually $\dot V\le V[\rho(1-V)-h_V(\bar e-\epsilon)]$, so $\limsup V\le(1-h_V(\bar e-\epsilon)/\rho)^+$; let $\epsilon\to0$, and (reach) puts the bound below $V_{\min}$. If $B(\infty)<\infty$, each $\ln(p_k/p_l)$ converges. $\square$

### Proof of Proposition {{#twochan}}, and measuring $\theta$

With $\Delta\bar\pi_{-i}=(n\bar\Delta-\Delta\pi_i)/(n-1)$, $\Delta J_i=\frac{n-1-\theta}{n-1}\Delta\pi_i+\frac{n\theta}{n-1}\bar\Delta$; writing $\Delta\pi_i=\bar\Delta+(\Delta\pi_i-\bar\Delta)$ the coefficient of $\bar\Delta$ is $1+\theta$. (1): each $\pi_j$ appears in $n-1$ averages, so $\sum_iJ_i=(1+\theta)\sum_i\pi_i$. (2): at $\theta=-1$ objectives sum to zero at every profile; at $\theta=n-1$ each objective is the total, so a total-maximizing profile admits no profitable deviation. (3): $\pi_k-\sum_jp_j\pi_j=(1-p_k)[\pi_k-\sum_{j\ne k}\frac{p_j}{1-p_k}\pi_j]$; and $\pi_i+\lambda(\pi_i-\bar\pi)=(1+\lambda\frac{n-1}n)\pi_i-\lambda\frac{n-1}n\bar\pi_{-i}$. (4): a rebated levy is a transfer with $\bar\Delta=0$ and own part $-\tau(n-1)/n$; withholding a promised $\beta\Omega$ from the $n-1$ others gives own part $\beta\Omega$ and $\bar\Delta=0$, hence $\beta\Omega(n-1-\theta)/(n-1)$; if the promised share was to be divided among all $n$, the gain is $\beta\Omega(n-1-\theta)/n$ [num: SM]. (5): $\Delta\pi_i=g-H/n$, others $-H/n$, so $\Delta J_i=g-(1+\theta)H/n$; spite pays iff $\theta<-(n-1)c/\Delta_y$. $\square$ [num: TC, an identity on 4,000 random games.]

**Measuring the weight.** In the contest of Section 3.4 a change in the shared loss enters through the common channel and a rebated levy, or the private cost, through the differential one, so the ratio of the responses of intensity identifies the weight: by Proposition {{#boundary}}(1), $\partial e/\partial\ell$ is $1+\theta$ times $\partial e/\partial c$ [num: TH; SP, where the ratio read from numerical equilibria equals $1+\theta$ to four digits]. If actors perceive only a share $\psi$ of the shared loss, the ratio is $n(1+\theta)\psi/(n-1-\theta)$: what is identified is $(1+\theta)\psi$, and with half perceived a true $\theta=0$ reads as $-0.43$ [num: SM]. Separating $\theta$ from $\psi$ needs a third perturbation that changes the visibility of shared harm without changing its size, or an independent measure of perceived harm. The identification fails exactly where the theory says shared harm goes unseen. Because the working $\theta$ of Proposition {{#standprice}} rises when provision lowers the won share of capacity, the registered observable $1+\theta$ rises with provision: in the checked case, from 0.58 with the whole of capacity won to 0.81 with half of it provided [num: SP]. That is the sign Test Γ must use.

### Full statement and proof of Proposition {{#wow}}

*Let $n$ symmetric developers with $\tau_{\rm eff}=0$ and the host at steady state maximize $u_k=\pi_k+\lambda(\pi_k-\bar\pi)$, with $G(V)=g_VV$, $h_V\bar e<\rho$ and $\Upsilon=g_Vh_V/(\rho\nu)$; write $w_\lambda=1+\lambda(n-1)/n$. (1) Restraint is dominant if $w_\lambda<\Upsilon/n$, depletion if reversed. (2) If $\Upsilon>1$, joint payoff is maximized by full restraint while the equilibrium is full depletion whenever $\lambda>(\Upsilon-n)/(n-1)$, at every $\lambda$ when $n>\Upsilon$. (3) Every developer then loses $\nu\bar e(\Upsilon-1)$. (4) Under (reach) the equilibrium host is below the floor.*

*Proof.* $\pi_k-\bar\pi$ gives $G$ weight zero, so $\partial u_k/\partial e_k=w_\lambda\nu-g_Vh_V/(n\rho)$, independent of others' choices. Joint payoff at a common $e$ has slope $n\nu(1-\Upsilon)$. Payoffs are $\nu\bar e+g_V(1-h_V\bar e/\rho)$ at full depletion and $g_V$ under restraint. $\square$

**{{!Remark:thrrep}} (Thresholds and repetition need observation).** If crossing the floor costs each a common loss $L$, a developer pivotal for crossing restrains when $L>(w_\lambda-\Upsilon/n)\nu\bar e$, and the game becomes coordination at the threshold (Barrett 2013). Joint restraint is sustained by reversion whenever $\delta\ge(w_\lambda-\Upsilon/n)/(w_\lambda-\Upsilon/n+\Upsilon-1)$: evaluating the cutoff for $\Upsilon=3$, $n=4$ gives 0.11 at $\lambda=0$, 0.47 at $\lambda=2$ and 0.95 at $\lambda=50$. Uncertainty about the threshold turns coordination back into a dilemma (Barrett and Dannenberg 2012), and noisy monitoring makes reversion costly (Green and Porter 1984).

### Proof of Proposition {{#boundary}}

*Splitting the objective.* Write $\pi_i=w_i\Omega_e+w_i\Omega_s-ce_i-\ell\mathcal E$ with $w_i=e_i^r/\sum_je_j^r$. As a function of $e_i$ with the others fixed, true welfare $W_i$ of {{eq:welfare}} is a contest objective with effective prize $\big[\Omega_e(n-1-\kappa)+\Omega_s(n-1-\kappa_s)\big]/(n-1)$ and unit cost $c+(1+\kappa)\ell$, since the others' divisible winnings fall by what $i$ wins and the others' positional winnings count with weight $\kappa_s\le0$; the objective $J_i$ with a single $\theta$ is the case $\kappa=\kappa_s=\theta$, a contest with prize $\Omega(n-1-\theta)/(n-1)$ and unit cost $c+(1+\theta)\ell$.

(1) That contest has, for $r\le n/(n-1)$, the symmetric pure equilibrium $e(\theta)$ of the statement (Pérez-Castrillo and Verdier 1992). At a symmetric profile $\pi=\Omega/n-e(c+n\ell)$ and $W=(1+\kappa)\pi$ with the positional part cancelling at equal shares, so welfare falls with $e$ and rises with $\theta$.

(2) The welfare-maximizing response is the best response under exactly the weights $\kappa$ on the divisible and $\kappa_s$ on the positional payoffs of the others, because $W_i$ is that objective. Lowering the divisible weight below $\kappa$ raises the marginal value of intensity by $\Omega_e\,\partial w_i/\partial e_i/(n-1)+\ell>0$, so the chosen intensity rises and, where the maximizer is unique and the intensity changes, welfare falls. On the positional part, $\kappa_s\le0$ makes a rival's positional gain count at least fully as one's own loss, so relative concern about it is the accurate weighting, and no $\theta$ above it improves welfare.

(3) A finite-population evolutionarily stable intensity maximizes $\pi_i-\bar\pi_{-i}$ (Schaffer 1988), the case $\theta=-1$, in which the shared loss carries weight $1+\theta=0$. At $r=1$ the resulting intensity dissipates the whole prize (Hehenkamp, Leininger and Possajennikov 2004), so with any shared loss $\ell>0$ every actor's payoff is negative. For $r>1$ asymmetric equilibria with lower total intensity also exist in a band below $n/(n-1)$; the symmetric one is used throughout. $\square$ [num: RH for the closed forms and a grid best response; CB.]

**Which preferences evolve.** It depends on the population: with unobserved preferences in large random populations, material self-interest (Ok and Vega-Redondo 2001; Dekel, Ely and Yilankaya 2007); with observed preferences and at least three actors in this contest, a commitment to a narrower objective (Güth and Yaari 1992; Heifetz, Shannon and Spiegel 2007); with high observability and assortative matching, cooperative preferences (Alger and Weibull 2013). Local competition favours spite (Alger, Weibull and Lehmann 2020), and an observable commitment to aggression is itself a race (Dixit 1987; Fershtman and Judd 1987).

### Proof of Proposition {{#standprice}}

*Decomposition.* A change $\Delta\pi$ in today's payoffs has a common part $\bar\Delta$ and a relative part $y_i=\Delta\pi_i-\bar\Delta$, and each carrier reinvests $\kappa_j\Delta\pi_j$ into stock $j$. To first order the two parts evolve separately: the prize depends on totals, and a relative shift leaves totals unchanged.

*The relative part.* Carrier $i$'s stocks rise by $\kappa_jy_i$ and the others' fall by the same in total. Relative deviations of stocks follow the Jacobian $\mathcal J=\mathcal D+\frac1n\mathbf u\mathbf v^\top$ of S2, with $\mathcal D=\operatorname{diag}(1-d_j-\delta_{f,j})$ and $\frac1nu_jv_j=(1-\beta)\kappa_jr_j\,\Omega/S^{j\ast}$ at the reference state. At a fixed-prize steady state $d_jX^{j\ast}=\kappa_j\Omega/n+k_{0j}$, so $\kappa_j\Omega/S^{j\ast}=\omega_jd_j$ and $\Gamma_\delta$ is the value of Proposition {{#loopgain}}(3); on a balanced-growth path the same expression is evaluated along the path. Applying the secular argument to $\delta\mathcal J$, as in the proof of Proposition {{#loopgain}}(3), the present value of carrier $i$'s own extra winnings is $\lambda_{\rm rel}y_i$ with $\lambda_{\rm rel}=\Gamma_\delta/(1-\Gamma_\delta)$, finite iff $\Gamma_\delta<1$. The prize is unchanged by a relative shift, so the others' winnings fall by $\lambda_{\rm rel}y_i$ in total.

*The common part.* Every carrier's stock $j$ rises by $\kappa_j\bar\Delta$, so the total $S^j$ rises by $n\kappa_j\bar\Delta$, and the prize by $\sum_kg_k\,n\kappa_k\bar\Delta$, of which each carrier receives $1/n$ at equal standing. The per-carrier common deviations $c^j$ therefore follow $c^{j\prime}=(1-d_j)c^j+\kappa_j\sum_kg_kc^k$, with Jacobian $\mathcal J_c=\operatorname{diag}(1-d_j)+\boldsymbol\kappa\mathbf g^\top$: diffusion does not act on a deviation common to all, and each carrier's winnings rise by $\mathbf g^\top c_t$ per period. With $A=I-\delta\operatorname{diag}(1-d_j)$ and $\Gamma^{\rm c}_\delta=\delta\mathbf g^\top A^{-1}\boldsymbol\kappa=\sum_j\delta\kappa_jg_j/(1-\delta(1-d_j))$, the Sherman–Morrison identity gives the present value $\delta\mathbf g^\top(I-\delta\mathcal J_c)^{-1}\boldsymbol\kappa\,\bar\Delta=\lambda_{\rm com}\bar\Delta$ with $\lambda_{\rm com}=\Gamma^{\rm c}_\delta/(1-\Gamma^{\rm c}_\delta)$, finite iff $\Gamma^{\rm c}_\delta<1$.

*Matching.* The future change in $i$'s own winnings is $F_i=\lambda_{\rm rel}y_i+\lambda_{\rm com}\bar\Delta$ and the others' average is $\bar F_{-i}=\lambda_{\rm com}\bar\Delta-\lambda_{\rm rel}y_i/(n-1)$, so the future change in $J_i=\pi_i+\theta_0\bar\pi_{-i}$ is $(1+\theta_0)\lambda_{\rm com}\bar\Delta+\frac{n-1-\theta_0}{n-1}\lambda_{\rm rel}y_i$. Adding today's change from Proposition {{#twochan}},
$$\Delta J_i^{\rm total}=(1+\theta_0)(1+\lambda_{\rm com})\,\bar\Delta+\frac{n-1-\theta_0}{n-1}(1+\lambda_{\rm rel})\,y_i.$$
Matching to $(1+\theta)\bar\Delta+\frac{n-1-\theta}{n-1}y_i$ up to a positive factor gives {{eq:thetagamma}}, and $1+\lambda=1/(1-\Gamma)$ for each gain gives its second form. The map $\theta\mapsto(1+\theta)/(n-1-\theta)$ is increasing on $[-1,n-1)$, so $\theta<\theta_0$ iff $(1+\lambda_{\rm com})/(1+\lambda_{\rm rel})<1$ iff $\lambda_{\rm rel}>\lambda_{\rm com}$. In closed form, with $\varrho=\frac{(1+\theta_0)(1-\Gamma_\delta)}{(n-1-\theta_0)(1-\Gamma^{\rm c}_\delta)}$, $\theta=\big(\varrho(n-1)-1\big)/(1+\varrho)$.

(1) With a fixed prize $\mathbf g=0$, so $\Gamma^{\rm c}_\delta=0$ and $\varrho=(1+\theta_0)(1-\Gamma_\delta)/(n-1-\theta_0)$, whose solution is $1+\theta_\Gamma=(1+\theta_0)\,n(1-\Gamma_\delta)/\big(n-(1+\theta_0)\Gamma_\delta\big)$, equivalent to the stated formula; it falls to $-1$ as $\Gamma_\delta\to1^-$. The weight of a shared change is $1+\theta$, and the four quantities named are the four parts of Proposition {{#twochan}} evaluated at $\theta_\Gamma$.

(2) With one stock, $\Omega=gS$, $k_0=\delta_f=0$: $\kappa\Omega/S^\ast=\kappa g$, so $\Gamma_\delta=\delta(1-\beta)r\kappa g/(1-\delta(1-d))$ and $\Gamma^{\rm c}_\delta=\delta\kappa g/(1-\delta(1-d))$, whose ratio is $(1-\beta)r$ at every $\delta$. On the balanced path the mean stock grows by $G=1-d+\kappa g$ a period and a relative deviation by $m=1-d+(1-\beta)r\kappa g$, so the share deviation multiplies by $m/G$, which exceeds one iff $(1-\beta)r>1$: the condition of Proposition {{#compound}}(1) without provision and diffusion.

(3) *Beyond the domain.* Let $\Lambda^\ast$ be the Perron root of $\mathcal J$; in the single-channel case $\Lambda^\ast=1+(d+\delta_f)(\Gamma-1)$ (Remark {{#tdec}}), and $\Gamma_\delta<1$ iff $\delta\Lambda^\ast<1$. Write the nonlinear relative deviation along the Perron direction as $y_{t+1}=f(y_t)$ with $f(0)=0$, $f'(0)=\Lambda^\ast$, own extra winnings $w(y)$ with $w'(0)>0$, and $PV(\varepsilon)=\sum_{t\ge1}\delta^tw(y_t)$ from $y_0=\varepsilon$. When $\delta\Lambda^\ast>1$, choose $\Lambda''<\Lambda^\ast$ with $\delta\Lambda''>1$: while the deviation stays small, $y_t\ge c\Lambda''^t\varepsilon$, so the sum up to the saturation time $T=\ln(\eta/\varepsilon)/\ln\Lambda''$ is at least of order $\varepsilon(\delta\Lambda'')^T=\varepsilon^{a}$ with $a=\ln(1/\delta)/\ln\Lambda''<1$, and the tail after $T$ is of the same order $\delta^T$. Hence $PV(\varepsilon)\asymp\varepsilon^a$ with $a=\ln(1/\delta)/\ln\Lambda^\ast$ in the limit, and $PV(\varepsilon)/\varepsilon\to\infty$: the value of standing is continuous at equal standing with infinite slope, and no finite weight $\theta$ represents the actor. When $\delta\Lambda^\ast<1$ the same bounds give $PV(\varepsilon)/\varepsilon\to\Gamma_\delta/(1-\Gamma_\delta)$, slowly when $\Lambda^\ast>1$, of order $\varepsilon^{-\ln(\delta\Lambda^\ast)/\ln\Lambda^\ast}$; at $\delta\Lambda^\ast=1$ the ratio grows like $\ln(1/\varepsilon)$. $\square$

*Numerics.* The nonlinear stock model against both closed forms [num: SP]: in five steady-state and balanced-growth cases with sharing, provision and diffusion the simulated prices of a relative and of a common unit match $\Gamma_\delta/(1-\Gamma_\delta)$ and $\Gamma^{\rm c}_\delta/(1-\Gamma^{\rm c}_\delta)$ to a relative error of $2.5\times10^{-5}$ (for instance 2.213115 against 2.213115 with a fixed prize, 4.764587 against 4.764706 and 0.473684 against 0.473684 with a growing prize); in a balanced-growth case with $\Gamma_\delta=1.066$ the simulated relative price is 226 while the formula gives $-16.2$, which is the cusp of (3). The two-period contest [num: SP, an identity, first order by construction at a symmetric point]: the directly computed symmetric first-period intensity equals $e(\theta)$ of the general form to a relative error of $3\times10^{-8}$ in six cases, while the fixed-prize formula misses it by up to 15% when the prize grows; and in fourteen cases with $n\in\{2,4\}$, $\theta_0\in\{-0.5,0,0.3,0.5,1,1.9\}$, $\beta\in\{0,0.2,0.3,0.5,0.6\}$ and fixed, mixed and proportional prizes, $\theta$ read off the equilibrium matches the formula to $7.6\times10^{-8}$ and the sign rule, narrower iff $\lambda_{\rm rel}>\lambda_{\rm com}$, holds in all fourteen, with ties at $(1-\beta)r=1$. If only gross winnings convert, the equilibrium reads a narrower $\theta$ than the net-payoff formula ($-0.31$ against $-0.16$ at $r=1$, $\theta_0=0$), so the premise is load-bearing [num: SP]. The fixed-prize table [num: NB]: with $n=4$, $\theta_\Gamma$ at $\theta_0=0$ is $-0.20$, $-0.43$, $-0.69$, $-0.87$ and $-0.99$ at $\Gamma_\delta=0.25,0.5,0.75,0.9,0.99$; at $\theta_0=2$ it is still $-0.08$ at $\Gamma_\delta=0.9$. The cusp [num: PD]: in Codex's case ($n=2$, $r=2$, $d=0.2$, $\delta=0.9$, so $\Gamma=2$ and $\Gamma_\delta=9/7$) the printed multiplier is $-4.5$ while the discounted advantage is positive at every $\varepsilon$ and scales as $\varepsilon^{0.578}$, the predicted exponent $\ln(1/\delta)/\ln\Lambda^\ast=0.5779$; inside the domain but above $\Gamma=1$ ($\delta=0.8$, $\Gamma_\delta=0.889$) the ratio $PV/\varepsilon$ rises to the formula's 8 from below, 7.16 at $\varepsilon=10^{-4}$ and 7.99 at $10^{-12}$; at $\delta\Lambda^\ast=1$ it grows by a constant 8.4 per two decades of $\varepsilon$.

*Strategic response.* With the others' conduct fixed, the exact finite-jump price equals $\Gamma_\delta/(1-\Gamma_\delta)$ even at the paper's jump sizes, so it is the premise, not the linearization, that the strategic case changes [num: MP]. When rivals' intensities respond to the state, S2 shows that two elasticities replace $r$: the full-equilibrium one decides the stability of equal standing, and the partial-equilibrium one, own intensity held and rivals responding, prices standing, so the first-order form of {{eq:thetagamma}} survives with $\Gamma_\delta$ read from the second. In the static contest with effort the partial-equilibrium elasticity is exactly $2(n-1)/n$ times the structural $r$ at $n=2,3,4,6,10$, and a two-period contest with responding second-period effort has the price $2(n-1)/n$ times the fixed-strategy value [num: MP]. In the two-actor Markov-perfect compounding contests computed, the price at parity exceeds the fixed-strategy value by 1.12 to 4.75 times at the paper's depreciation and jump sizes, by 1.05 to 3.15 times with small jumps, and by 7.87 times at $\delta=0.97$; at a structural $\Gamma=0.6$ the equilibrium gain is 1.96, so equal standing is unstable in equilibrium while the structural benchmark says stable; a laggard holding a quarter of the stock prices standing at 57% of the structural value at $\Gamma=0.3$ and 25% at $\Gamma=0.6$, and below zero with a shared loss, while a leader at three quarters prices it at 153%; total effort in a tug-of-war is highest at parity and falls with the lead [num: MP]. The sign of the correction is not universal: with a strong catch-up motive the equilibrium elasticity falls to 0.993 of the structural one [num: MP]. In the same class, from a step-shaped initial value function, value iteration converges to a second fixed point, a winner-take-all equilibrium whose price at parity is 12.5 times the structural one at $\Gamma=0.2$, while at coarser rounds the step start returns to the smooth equilibrium [num: MP]; this multiplicity, and the reading that the belief that a lead is decisive selects the equilibrium in which it is, are held for outside review.

*The two errors.* With $\theta_0=\kappa$ and a fixed prize, $\theta_\Gamma(\kappa)<0$ iff $n\kappa<(n-1)(1+\kappa)\Gamma_\delta$, that is iff $\Gamma_\delta>n\kappa/\big((n-1)(1+\kappa)\big)$: for $n=4$, $\kappa=0.4$ the threshold is 0.381, and $\theta_\Gamma(0.4)$ is $+0.15$, $-0.15$, $-0.53$, $-0.80$ at $\Gamma_\delta=0.25,0.5,0.75,0.9$ [num: SP]. For $\kappa=1$ and $n=2$ the threshold is 1, so a two-actor contest never carries an accurate held model below the isolated self inside the domain.

*Bounds.* First order and with the others' conduct fixed; in the full stochastic game the state also moves the others' strategies, and the computed amplification is listed for outside review. A myopic actor has $\lambda_{\rm rel}=\lambda_{\rm com}=0$ and keeps its held model, but selection still favours $\theta=-1$ (Proposition {{#boundary}}(3)).

**{{!Proposition:growprize}} (A prize that grows with capacity) [thm].** Where the prize grows with total capacity, looking ahead narrows the working self-model exactly where leads compound, and diffusion opens a gap between the two. *(1) Two periods. In a two-period contest in which first-period net payoffs convert into capacity at rate $\kappa$ and the second-period prize is $\Omega_2=\Omega_{20}+g\sum_jK_j$ with a share $\beta$ divided equally, at equal standing $\lambda_{\rm rel}=\delta\kappa(1-\beta)r\Omega_2/(nK)$ and $\lambda_{\rm com}=\delta\kappa g$, so $\lambda_{\rm rel}/\lambda_{\rm com}=(1-\beta)r\,\Omega_2/(nKg)$, which is $(1-\beta)r$ when $\Omega_{20}=0$. (2) Infinite horizon. With one stock, $\Omega_t=gS_t$, depreciation $d$, diffusion $\delta_f$ and no provision, the total grows by $G=1-d+\kappa g$ a period and a relative deviation by $m=1-d-\delta_f+(1-\beta)r\kappa g$; where $\delta G<1$ and $\delta m<1$,*
$$\lambda_{\rm rel}=\frac{\delta(1-\beta)r\kappa g}{1-\delta m},\qquad \lambda_{\rm com}=\frac{\delta\kappa g}{1-\delta G},\qquad \frac{\lambda_{\rm rel}}{\lambda_{\rm com}}=\frac{(1-\beta)r\,(1-\delta G)}{1-\delta m},$$
*and the working self-model narrows iff $(1-\beta)r>1+\delta\delta_f/\big(1-\delta(1-d)\big)$, while equal standing is unstable iff $(1-\beta)r>1+\delta_f/(\kappa g)$. Without diffusion both conditions are $(1-\beta)r>1$, at every horizon and every $\delta$. (3) Fixed prize at a stationary total. Then $\Gamma^{\rm c}_\delta=0$ and the working self-model narrows for every $\Gamma_\delta>0$, including where $\Gamma<1$ and equal standing is stable.*

*Proof.* (1) Future winnings are $w_i=(1-\beta)\Omega_2p_i+\beta\Omega_2/n$ with $p_i=K_i^r/\sum_jK_j^r$. At equal standing $\partial p_i/\partial K_i=r(n-1)/(n^2K)$, $\partial p_i/\partial K_j=-r/(n^2K)$ and $\partial\Omega_2/\partial K_j=g$, so a reinvestment $x_j=\kappa\Delta\pi_j$ changes $w_i$ by $(1-\beta)\Omega_2r(x_i-\bar x)/(nK)+g\bar x$; discounting gives the two returns, and the matching of the main proof gives {{eq:thetagamma}}. (2) A relative unit raises $s_i=K_i/S$ by $\kappa/S$; each period the deviation is multiplied by $m/G$ and own winnings rise by $g(1-\beta)r\kappa\,m^{t-1}$ in period $t$, which sums to $\lambda_{\rm rel}$; a common unit raises every stock by $\kappa$, so $S$ by $n\kappa$, and each carrier's winnings by $g\kappa G^{t-1}$ in period $t$, which sums to $\lambda_{\rm com}$. The ratio exceeds one iff $(1-\beta)r(1-\delta G)>1-\delta m$, which rearranges to $(1-\beta)r>1+\delta\delta_f/(1-\delta(1-d))$; instability is $m>G$, which is $\kappa g\big((1-\beta)r-1\big)>\delta_f$. (3) is Proposition {{#standprice}}(1) with Proposition {{#loopgain}}(1). $\square$

*Numerics [num: SP].* Infinite horizon, no depreciation, $\kappa=1$: the simulated ratio of present values equals the closed form to $10^{-4}$ in six $(r,\beta)$ pairs, 2.53846, 0.68750, 0.62264, 1.00000, 2.08108 and 0.92958, and exceeds one exactly when $(1-\beta)r>1$. Two periods with a proportional prize, $\theta_0=0.5$: $\theta$ is 0.65 at $r=0.7$, 0.50 at $r=1$ and 0.37 at $r=1.3$, which is the reviewer's finding that the sign turns on decisiveness. With a proportional prize and no diffusion, narrowing and the instability of equal shares coincide in 24 of 24 simulated cases; with $\delta_f=0.03$ the simulated instability threshold is $r=1.2000$ against $1+\delta_f/(\kappa g)=1.2000$ and the narrowing threshold $r=1.1421$ against $1+\delta\delta_f/(1-\delta(1-d))=1.1421$, so an objective narrows at $r=1.17$ while equal shares are stable. With a fixed prize at a stationary total ($n=3$, $r=1.5$, $k_0=0.1$), $\Gamma=0.938$ and equal standing is stable while the objective narrows.

### Proof of Lemma {{#onegroup}}

Under any partition into groups $g$, the Price equation gives $\bar w\Delta\bar z=\operatorname{Cov}_g(\bar w_g,\bar z_g)+\operatorname E_g\operatorname{Cov}_{j\in g}(w_j,z_j)+\operatorname E[w\Delta z]$. Let the trait cost its bearer $c>0$ and give every member a benefit $bz_{\rm pop}$ equal across members, so $\pi_j=\pi_0+bz_{\rm pop}-cz_j$. A payoff-monotone rule orders growth rates as payoffs, so fitness is $w_j=\psi(\pi_j)$ with $\psi$ increasing on the payoffs present, and $w_j=h(z_j)$ for the same decreasing $h$ throughout the population. By Chebyshev's association inequality a decreasing function of $z$ has non-positive covariance with $z$, so the total selection term $\operatorname{Cov}(w,z)$ and every within-group term are non-positive under every partition. When $\psi$ is affine, $\bar w_g=\psi(\pi_0+bz_{\rm pop}-c\bar z_g)$ is decreasing in $\bar z_g$ and the between-group term is $-c\psi'\operatorname{Var}_g(\bar z_g)\le0$. When $\psi$ is not affine, $\bar w_g$ depends on the spread of $z$ within the group as well as on its mean, by Jensen's inequality, so $\operatorname{Cov}_g(\bar w_g,\bar z_g)$ can take either sign; the decomposition is an identity, so the total stays non-positive and the within-group term absorbs the difference. The trait can rise only if the transmission term is positive, by biased transmission, or if a rule changes the bearer's relative payoff. If the benefit goes only to the bearer's group, $\pi_g$ contains $bz_g$, and under the partition that matches its reach the between-group term can be positive. $\square$

*Numerics [num: PR].* With 600 individuals in 30 units and five partitions (one group, units, five blocs, fifty random groups, individuals), the between-group term under a global benefit is $0$, $-0.0019$, $-0.00002$, $-0.0011$ and $-0.0123$; under a benefit local to units it is $+0.0097$ at the unit partition. Biased transmission $\dot z=-cz(1-z)+\mu_{\rm tr}(1-z)$ settles at $\mu_{\rm tr}/c$. In an illustration with a base hazard of 0.03 a period, learning only from failures suffers an irreversible loss within 400 periods with probability 0, 0.14, 0.46 and 1.0 as the irreversible share of failures is 0, 0.05, 0.2 and 1, against 0, 0.06, 0.22 and 0.70 under adoption in advance: an illustration, not an estimate. *Nonlinear fitness* [num: PR, extended from the memo scripts m05(C) and f4(B)]: in 3,000 random partitioned populations under each of six increasing transforms of payoff (affine, exponential, logarithmic, cubic, logistic and near-step), the total selection term and every within-group term are positive in none; the between-group term is positive in none under the affine transform and in 3 to 346 of 3,000 under the nonlinear ones; the reviewers' instances give between-group terms of $+0.0092$ and $+0.0169$ with totals of $-0.1150$ and $-0.3637$. This is Codex's boundary case of nonlinear fitness.

### Full statement and proof of {{club}}

**{{!Proposition:club}} (Clubs and blocs) [thm].** Inclusion that stops at the edge of a club over-produces the harm it does not bear, and blocs replay the race one level up. *(1) Let the contestants' activity yield them a strictly concave common value $\Omega(\mathcal E)$ of total intensity, let each contestant bear a shared loss $\ell$ per unit of $\mathcal E$, and let $m_o$ non-contestants each bear $\ell_o$ per unit. Contestants who weigh one another fully choose the total intensity that maximizes $\Omega(\mathcal E)-c\mathcal E-n\ell\mathcal E$, which exceeds the intensity that also counts $m_o\ell_o\mathcal E$ whenever $m_o\ell_o>0$, and the harm to non-contestants is larger. (2) If the $n$ contestants form $k$ equal blocs of size $s_b=n/k$, each inclusive within and acting as one, the game among blocs is the original game with $n=k$ and shared loss $s_b\ell$ per unit of a bloc's intensity, so every result about the race holds among blocs.*

*Scope.* Unequal blocs with $r\ne1$ make the bloc game asymmetric: at equal total effort a bloc of three has $3^{1-r}$ times a single contestant's strength, 1.73 at $r=0.5$, 1 at $r=1$ and 0.58 at $r=1.5$ [num: SM]. *Proof.* (1) Strict concavity orders the two maximizers, since the system's objective subtracts the further linear term $m_o\ell_o\mathcal E$. (2) A bloc of $s_b$ equal members acting as one holds the members' summed prize share and bears $s_b\ell$ per unit of its own intensity relative to the others', so the game among $k$ blocs is the original game with $n=k$ and loss $s_b\ell$. $\square$ *Numerics.* With $\Omega=10\sqrt{\mathcal E}$, $c=1$, $n=4$, $\ell=0.3$, $m_o=20$, $\ell_o=0.3$: the club's intensity is 5.17 against 0.37, and the harm to non-contestants 31.0 against 2.2 [num: CB]. Bloc intensities 0.1563, 0.1389 and 0.1316 for $(k,s_b)=(2,2),(3,2),(2,3)$, as the formula with $s_b\ell$ predicts and the formula with $\ell$ alone does not [num: SM].
