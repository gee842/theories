# Part III. Responses: what would change the trajectory

Part I proved its results in models and Part II read the present trajectory against them. Part III is comparison and judgment: a standard of comparison [val], profiles of what each path changes [def], results about each path under its own premises [thm∣prem], and the requirements, the design and the transition that follow.

## 8. Comparing paths, and the alternatives on their strongest terms

> **Claim.** Paths are compared by one standard that protects irreversible losses first and applies one range of uncertainty to every path, each path's own failure modes included [val]. An arrangement is described by what it changes, not by its name: which thresholds of {{thresholds}} it moves, in which direction, for whom, and whether its enforcement survives the arrival of a decisive position [def]. Where an arrangement constrains conduct while leaving the thresholds where they were, it erodes as stakes and speed grow; where it moves them across for every capable actor, it persists: a registered prediction, classified by profile before the outcome is known [conj].
>
> **Why.** Labels such as "regulation", "cooperation" or "agreement" decide nothing. What decides is whether an arrangement changes the map from conduct to share, resources and standing, in which direction, and for whom. An alternative that succeeds does so by moving the shared parameters across their thresholds for every capable actor.

### 8.1 The standard of comparison

**The protection standard [val].** Each candidate path is evaluated at common horizons, near (about five years), medium (about twenty) and long (fifty or more), over one justified range $\Theta$ of uncertain parameters applied to every path. First, bound the probability of O1 and O2 within each horizon over $\Theta$. Second, among paths whose bound stays below a stated tolerance, prefer the one with greater useful benefit, more fairly distributed, that keeps more options of correction open. Third, if no path meets the tolerance, prefer the one with the lowest bound that keeps options open. This is robust satisficing on irreversible losses, and a normative choice. An expected-value criterion can favour a small probability of a very large loss for a large expected benefit. Under discounted expected utility the acceptable existential risk depends sharply on the curvature of utility: at a 1% annual hazard and 10% growth, a planner with log utility accepts about a one-in-three cumulative risk for a 55-fold gain in consumption, and with stronger curvature about 4% (Jones 2024) [obs: EV11]; if utility is linear in consumption, so that the value of a year of life does not rise with it, a course is preferred whenever the hazard it adds is below the growth it adds, though it ends in catastrophe with certainty (S8) [thm]. O1 protects catastrophic, common-cause losses of life; deaths from benefits forgone are counted as a cost of the path in the comparison, not as O1, and the weight given to them is the normative crux N1.

**The range $\Theta$ [val].** A parameter value enters $\Theta$ if it is consistent with the dated evidence of the register and is produced by a stated mechanism whose premises are named, with a precedent or base rate where one exists. A value that needs a premise the register contradicts is excluded. The same rule applies to every path: a rising uncovered share on the continuation path enters, because its premises are named mechanisms the register does not contradict (EV5–EV7); and equally the capture of a common custodian, dissolution and coercive enforcement on the cooperative path. Every entry of $\Theta$ cites its source, and every path's failure modes, the design of Section 10 included, are in $\Theta$ under the same rule.

**{{!Definition:kernel}} (The correctable kernel) [def].** *The correctable kernel is the set of states of the world from which some non-anticipative strategy of admissible correction avoids O1 and O2 against every sequence of errors in the justified range $\Theta$. In viability theory it is a discriminating, or guaranteed-viability, kernel of a game against disturbances (Cardaliaguet 1996; Cardaliaguet, Quincampoix and Saint-Pierre 1999), not the viability kernel of undisturbed dynamics: it takes the union over correction strategies and the intersection over disturbances.*

Leaving the kernel is the loss of guaranteed correctability, a point of no guaranteed return that comes earlier than the losses themselves. The kernel is not computed in this paper; what Section 10 computes is a boundary of viable parameters under one fixed strategy and one disturbance path ({{>safeguards}}), a different and smaller question. The corpus describes openness, 开, by four capacities, and each is a condition of membership in the kernel [corpus: possibility-space]: 未, a region not yet settled remains, so no decisive end state has been reached; 可, generation is still possible, so lineages, purposes and correctors stay plural; 应, the process can still respond, so humans can suspend, modify or replace; 转, it can still turn back, so reversal is possible. 开者，未成之域不闭也: to be open is that the not-yet-become is not closed. The corpus also separates two closures, 护闭, closing one place for a time to protect a larger opening, and 成闭, a closing that does not reopen. A closure made to protect openness, such as a defence, a quarantine or an emergency power, can state what would make it withdraw; a closure that cannot state that has left the kernel. The standard's second and third steps, "keeps options of correction open", are a preference for staying inside it.

**{{!Lemma:fallback}} (When an arrangement cannot do worse) [thm].** *Let an arrangement $A$ and continuation $C$ be driven by the same exogenous world process, the irreversible event on each path occurring at date $t$ when a common uniform draw falls below that path's conditional hazard given the shared history. Suppose that on every history on which both paths have survived to $t$: (safe fallback) once $A$ has dissolved, its hazard is no higher than $C$'s; and (bounded own risk) while $A$ lasts, its hazard, its own hazards of capture, restriction and collusion included, is no higher than $C$'s. Then $A$'s survival probability is at least $C$'s at every horizon, whatever the rule by which $A$ dissolves, rules that depend on the state included.*

*Scope.* Where bounded own risk fails there is no pointwise dominance, and the standard compares the worst cases of both paths; that is where regulated competition can win. Comparing after conditioning on the dissolution date is not valid when dissolution depends on the state [num: SM(4), SM(14)]. *Proof:* S8.

The two conditions are design conditions, readable from an arrangement's own documents. An agreement whose fallback is a short sprint from preserved relative standing violates the first; a design whose own concentration, capture or collusion can exceed continuation's hazards violates the second, and whether the design of Section 10 does is crux C4.

**Horizons.** With survival-first comparisons at a common finite horizon, the game between restraint and racing is dominance, a stag hunt, chicken or a prisoner's dilemma according to the signs of $\Phi^R-\Phi^T$ and $\Phi^S-\Phi^P$: the survival gained by restraining rather than racing against a restrainer, and by staying out rather than racing against a racer. Mutual racing remains a strict equilibrium at every finite horizon at which racing beside a racer survives better than staying out; and when the arrangement's own survival decays, a classification that holds at near and medium horizons can reverse at long ones ({{survassure}}, with the examples in which the shared hazard carries the argument for a leader) [thm]. So paths are compared at common finite horizons, not at an infinite one at which both branches of a hazardous world tend to zero. The protection standard does not by itself rank continuation with strong correction against a design that changes selection: under the rule for $\Theta$ the worst case of each includes an irreversible loss, so the comparison falls to the standard's third step, and Section 12.1 states the ranking as a judgment with its reasons.

### 8.2 Profiles, not labels

**{{!Definition:profile}} (What an arrangement changes) [def].** *An arrangement is described by a profile: which mechanisms of Part I it neutralizes, weakens, leaves or strengthens, read as which thresholds of {{thresholds}} it moves and toward which side; its coverage (unilateral, plurilateral, every capable actor); its bindingness (Abbott et al. 2000); whether its enforcement survives a decisive position, and if it prevents one from arriving, by what verified mechanism; whether its beneficiaries can amend it; its fallback and its own risk; and its durability. And its direction of selection: every arrangement that changes the map from conduct to share, resources and standing changes selection, so what is recorded is whether it moves the shared parameters of Definition {{#cond}} toward their safe side in every capable actor's game, or only in rivals'.*

Export controls that aim to preserve a lead change selection toward a lead: they block diffusion to rivals, and blocked diffusion raises the structural loop gain ({{loopgain}}; {{compound}}). Deterrence by threatened sabotage acts on $p_d$, as custody does, by a different means and with a different fallback. The direction this paper argues for is the *generative* one. It has seven marks, each the reversal of a threshold, and every later list in the paper cites these or repeats them in this order:

- **G1** consequences return to their source (load);
- **G2** winning confers reward, not more advantage in the same game (compounding; standing);
- **G3** success cannot buy control over its own assessment, and correction stays plural, decorrelated and in common hands (capture; alignment tax);
- **G4** decisive capability is held in common or not built (enforcement);
- **G5** provision of the conditions of a decent life sits beneath the game, not as its prize (compounding and standing, through the share of capacity that has to be won);
- **G6** measurement and language are shared (talk);
- **G7** pace is bound to understanding (coverage and tempo; the capability bound for masked classes).

G2 is the game-design axiom from which the others follow: where what is won is reinvested in the capacity to win, the game converges, and a reward of a different kind does not compound. Ren wrote it before any of this paper's models: "a winner of the game should be rewarded with anything other than more advantages of the game, otherwise the game will eventually converge and winner will take all. Then the game collapses" [corpus: possibility-space, 2024-07-29]. The delegation threshold is reversed by E1, an arrangement of Section 11.3 rather than a mark of the direction: a generative order can still delegate its decisions away, and the marks do not protect it from that. What this paper argues for is a change of selection with these marks, covering every capable actor, enforceable and durable, whatever it is called.

**{{!Definition:durability}} (Durability tests) [def].** *An arrangement is tested on its own documents and estimates, not on assumptions added by the tester: D1, its fallback if it dissolves; D2, its estimated hazard of breakdown and what drives it; D3, who keeps informed veto, modification, replacement and exit after any delegation; D4, whether its own correctors have full inbound row sums below one; D5, what happens when its strongest participant violates it; D6, succession and the continuity of essential services; D7, common causes and collusion among its checks and systems; D8, whether membership is open or stops at a club.*

The tests apply to every path, the cooperative design of Section 10 included; where an arrangement's documents are silent, the test records the silence, under one evidence standard for every row (X8). An arrangement with the form of cooperation that fails D1–D3 on its own estimates may be called a *pseudo-solution*; the label summarizes test results and does not replace them. The **registered prediction [conj]** of this section's claim is now exact. Arrangements that constrain conduct while leaving the thresholds where they were erode as stakes and speed grow, because the mechanisms of Sections 3 to 5 keep acting on them; arrangements that move the thresholds across for every capable actor persist, because what would erode them has been removed. The classification uses the profile, never the outcome, and Section 7.4 states what would refute it.

### 8.3 The alternatives

Each alternative is met in its strongest form, on its merits rather than its prominence, and answered by mechanism. The full case for each, how it would be installed, and what evidence would show that it suffices are in Discussion X8.

**(a) Technical alignment and AI-assisted correction.** *Strongest form:* control scoped to early transformative systems, used under control to accelerate alignment and oversight while the window lasts (Greenblatt, Shlegeris, Sachan and Roger 2024); weak supervisors eliciting part of a stronger model's capability (Burns et al. 2023); auditing with access to training data finding a planted objective that black-box access did not (Marks et al. 2025); oversight with the right topology; research that drives the alignment tax to zero or below, class by class, so that the safe variant is also the winning one; AI cheapening independent correction as fast as it cheapens control ({{corrrate}}); and, in its proponents' own plans, a strong international agreement ranked first. *What remains:* principal alignment does not change the game among principals ({{faithful}}); the window lasts only as long as the race allows, and the race shortens it; systems are retained for performance ({{retain}}), and on interior branches safety gains are absorbed by more delegation ({{homeostasis}}); redundant oversight is capped by the common causes that selection produces, and certified row sums are a sufficient certificate, not a proof of independent errors ({{topology}}); for masked divergences admission removes only what it detects ({{masked}}); AI-assisted correction is only as independent as its funding, access and appointment; and the common-cost part of incarnation is set at the outer loop ({{nested}}). It changes selection only when held by correctors separated from competitive success, under constraints common to all; it is then part of G3 and G7.

**(b) Regulated competition with strong correction.** *Strongest form:* liability and licensing make consequences return; evaluation access is mandated and thresholds are fixed in advance; where the effective hazard is high and capability transparent, pacing can be the unique equilibrium of a capability race without any agreement (Fudenberg and Koh 2026; X8 states where that equilibrium coincides with E3 and where it does not); civil nuclear power after a major accident, and aviation with independent investigation and insurance pricing, show durable safety with unchanged commercial motives. *What remains:* coverage across jurisdictions, since one actor can start the race; the rule-makers are themselves in a contest of standing ({{>nounilateral}}); liability cannot exceed the assets of the liable, and for a catastrophic common harm that gap is where a common cost cannot be made a private one (Shavell 1986); two developers' frameworks condition commitments on rivals, and one unreviewed study codes 77% of the changes it traced across twelve pairs of framework versions as weakening (EV2) [obs]; the precedents had no decisive prize; and the pacing equilibrium has no delegation channel and assumes the transparency that masking removes. Its profile depends on content. Regulation that returns consequences and separates evaluators from success, across every capable actor, is G1 and G3 for all, and the change this paper argues for, whatever it is called; regulation that constrains conduct while success can still buy the rule-makers is the first case of the registered prediction.

**(c) Leadership and a safety margin.** *Strongest form:* a leader spends part of its lead on safety; a large lead can discourage a follower (Harris and Vickers 1987; Konrad and Kovenock 2009); and a developer able to commit first to a slightly slower speed gains, because rivals slow too (Bueno de Mesquita, Dziuda and Polborn 2026). *What remains:* a lead shrinks by diffusion even when the follower does not accelerate, a six-month lead diffusing at a tenth a month lasting under five months [num: LD]; spending it returns the contest toward parity, where intensity and the price of standing are highest (Section 7.2); what the margin must buy is coverage, and for hazard classes whose evidence lag exceeds it, it buys nothing; whether it suffices is judged inside the contest, by the leader's own evaluators under time pressure; and a leader that sees its window closing has a motive to strike first ({{lead}}). It acts on tempo for a time and keeps the objective of winning. Its best case, reciprocal slowing, is the first stage of a transition (Section 11.3), and the commitment result holds for whoever commits first, lead or no lead.

**(d) The benign winner: win, bind, share.** *Strongest form:* a coalition seeks a clear advantage, including military superiority and the denial of key inputs to adversaries, and then offers its benefits to others in exchange for their support (EV1); victors have bound themselves through institutions (Ikenberry 2001), a dominant power can supply public goods (Kindleberger 1973), and an encompassing ruler internalizes the shared conditions (Olson 1993). It persuades by promising both security and benefits; both rest on the winner's restraint after the fact, which is what the tests examine. *What remains:* after a decisive win what the others receive is set by the winner's objective alone, and a promise to share is worth breaking, with no one to sanction it ({{wingives}}); the race shifts winners toward those that extend dominance ({{raceselects}}); and the winner is itself a field of agencies, firms, laboratories and systems, whose restraint after victory depends on whether its own correctors can be bought by its successes. The paper's own result gives the strongest form its exact content: with logarithmic utility a winner whose held self-model is accurate at $\kappa_W$ gives $\kappa_W/(1+\kappa_W)$ ({{wingives}}), so what the winner gives is its real dependence on the governed after victory, which cannot be verified beforehand, and AI is the technology that lowers that dependence (crux C13). It changes nothing in selection before victory. What would show it suffices, before victory: binding limits on what victory confers, benefits shared contemporaneously rather than promised, and the coalition's own correctors certified independent, with succession rules that survive changes of leadership (Test I). A winner that adopts these has changed what it selects for.

**(e) A finite sprint, then restraint.** *Strongest form:* sprint to a decisive lead, then freeze or roll back and govern at the tempo of understanding; a decisive contest is won with a modest edge when decisiveness is high, so the sprint need not run far beyond understanding. *What remains*, by mechanism and not by norm: the path passes through its greatest exposure, and through the rivals' strongest motive for prevention ({{preventattack}}), before any restraint begins; a decisive advantage not used to stop others' growth does not stay decisive, so the freeze is a stop imposed on everyone else by one party; such a stop is a common constraint of the most unequal incidence, selected against and read as a bid for a lead ({{blind}}(2)); and the sprinter as custodian of the decisive class is the largest conversion target the theory describes ({{influence}}; {{corrrate}}(4)), with its own evaluators as its only correctors. The two properties that would have to hold, equal incidence and uncaptured correction, are what Sections 3 and 4 show erode. What common custody shares with the sprint is that the decisive class is held and not raced; what differs is who bears the stop and who corrects the holder, the tests D4 and D5, compared row by row in X8. The sprint can preserve the sprinter's survival while giving up permanent superiority, which is not survival; during the sprint it changes nothing ({{sprint}}).

**(f) Deterrence and prevention.** Prevention can be survival-preferred by the preventer.

**{{!Proposition:preventown}} (Prevention under the preventer's own criterion) [thm].** *Let an actor compare courses by its own survival, facing a rival it believes certain to race, with survivals $\Phi^R$ under a restored arrangement, $\Phi^P$ racing against a racer and $\Phi^S$ staying out. (1) A single strike that succeeds with probability $s_P$, restoring the arrangement, and escalates to catastrophe with probability $\varepsilon_P$, is preferred to racing and to staying out iff $\varepsilon_P<1-\max(\Phi^S,\Phi^P)/(s_P\Phi^R+(1-s_P)\Phi^P)$; as the racing branches deteriorate, the escalation needed to deter prevention rises toward one. (2) Sustained prevention against a target that rebuilds with probability at least $b_P$ a period, each strike escalating with probability at least $\varepsilon_{\min}$, has a per-period hazard of at least $b_P\varepsilon_{\min}$, and at a common horizon it is compared with racing by hazard, not by the event.*

*Scope.* Prevention that permanently ends the target's capacity, or converts into joint monitoring, is a different branch. *Proof:* S8, with numbers [num: FS].

The objections are three, and none changes the criterion: it ends or endangers others' O1 and O2 (V2, V3); aimed at a rival believed certain to race, it can make a rival that was in fact survival-first act as the type it was feared to be (Jervis 1976; Kydd 1997), and shifts that cannot be observed raise the chance of mistaken prevention; and detecting unobservable progress and striking at machine speed brings the delegation of Section 6.3. Deterrence by threatened sabotage acts on $p_d$ by threat, observing datacentres and making compute thresholds salient (Hendrycks, Schmidt and Wang 2025); its fallback is war or race; its red lines are unclear and hard to monitor and the credibility of the threat is doubtful (X8); its credibility can reverse under survival-first comparisons; and algorithmic progress erodes thresholds stated in compute (EV9). Bounding the penalty for falling behind with autonomous, good-enough provision and defence is not an exit either: the penalty is set by the winner, not the laggard, and a winner with a relative objective has a motive to destroy what the laggard secured ({{insure}}). Insurance accepts the frame of the contest; it hedges against losing instead of declining the game. Ren's ruling: 如果胜利者的逻辑是cancer式的，落后并不可以承受，因此所有人都应该去首先反对这个逻辑, if the winner's logic is of this kind, falling behind cannot be borne, so everyone has reason first to oppose the logic itself [corpus: possibility-space, 2026-10-03]. **The hard case:** a survival-first actor that holds a high prior that a capable rival has an unbounded relative objective compares denial, a coalition that stops the decisive class, and prevention. Coverage then comes from consent or from coercion. Coercive denial raises the weight on winning and the trust threshold, and if it escalates it falls under {{preventown}}; it enters the comparison of Section 12.1 as its own candidate, only under the three conditions stated there: equal incidence, the restrictor's own included; execution by correctors independent of every party it restricts; and the consent of the chamber of those affected. The theory does not derive that its net effect is positive, and states the case as hard.

**(g) A verified agreement over a contest that remains.** Rivals agree to slow and to delay decisive capability, with compute declarations, supply-chain accounting, recomputation of workloads and mutually assured destruction of compute if the agreement dissolves. Let verification detect a defection in time with probability $\upsilon$, reversing it with a sanction $F$; let trust $\varpi$ be the belief that the other cooperates; and write $g_C$ for what cooperating gains against a cooperator and $g_D$ for what it loses against a defector.

**{{!Proposition:assure}} (Assurance) [thm].** *In a symmetric two-player stage game with payoffs $R$ when both cooperate, $T$ for defecting against a cooperator, $P$ when both defect and $S$ for cooperating against a defector, where a defection detected in time is reversed, the defector receiving $P-F$ and the cooperator $P$: (1) cooperation is a best response iff $\varpi\ge g_D/(g_C+g_D)$ when both are positive; no trust suffices if $g_C\le0<g_D$; cooperation is dominant if $g_D\le0<g_C$. (2) When the value of winning exceeds the value of cooperating, $T>R$, cooperation needs $\upsilon>(T-R)/(T-P+F)$; when $T<R$, it is possible without verification.*

*Proof:* S8, with the $n$-player bounds and the condition under which they fail.

The threshold in (2) is the enforcement condition {{eq:enforceeq}} seen from verification. With $p_d=1-\upsilon(T_{\rm dec})$, the probability that a breakout is not detected and answered before it becomes decisive, {{eq:enforceeq}} reads $\upsilon\ge1-\bar p_d$, and the two thresholds are numerically the same where the continuation after detection is the stage game's punishment ({{detectlink}}) [num: JX(d)]. What differs is what each takes as given: {{assure}} takes the sanction and the trust, the enforcement condition a credible continuation. Timely detection must exceed a level that rises with the value of winning, whichever way it is written.

**{{!Theorem:verify}} (Verification and the value of winning) [thm].** Verification is required exactly when, and to the degree that, the value of winning exceeds the value of cooperating. *(1) The timely detection required, $(T-R)/(T-P+F)$, tends to one as $T$ grows for a fixed sanction. (2) Detection counts only before a defection becomes decisive, within a window $T_{\rm dec}$ that acceleration shortens. (3) Verification carries neither unverifiable information nor future commitments, unless the sanction keeps pace with the net gain from exit, which grows with the capability held in check when leaving with a decisive advantage gains faster than staying with one's share ({{overhang}}); nor does it carry its own reliability. (4) Verification can also lower $T$ by bounding the largest defection that would go undetected, for instance by accounting for compute, which does not need near-certain detection; that bound holds only while the verified coalition's advantage in effective compute keeps pace with its erosion by diffusion [conj: the premise (advantage keeps pace), not derived here; the rate of erosion is EV9].*

*Proof:* S8.

One detailed plan of this kind, among leading powers, is designed to be enforceable without trust, with proportional reserves and a coalition that must stay ahead of covert projects (EV10; X8). The position it stands for, keep the contest, slow it and stay safe, is what this section answers; the plan is one instance. Such an agreement slows and caps, acting on tempo and decisiveness, and keeps relative standing in its enforcement and its fallback. That makes it a bridge. Whether it can be the end depends on whether it lowers the value of winning, which is G2 and G5, or only bounds defection, which is (4).

### 8.4 The comparison

|---|---|---|---|---|

| Coercive restriction of frontier inputs (Section 12.1, under three conditions) | $p_d$ by denial; the weight on winning raised | Escalation ({{preventown}}) | Unequal incidence; the restrictor as the largest conversion target | C2, C7 |

The comparison turns on two quantities. If continuation keeps the uncovered hazardous share summable, it can beat a fragile cooperative arrangement; if the uncovered share rises on the continuation path, any cooperative arrangement with a modest hazard of dissolution does better at every horizon past the near term [num: CMP; S8 gives the illustration]. The first is crux C1; the second is what Sections 10 and 11 design for and test. Under the protection standard both paths fail the tolerance in their worst case, so the comparison moves to the standard's third step, the lowest bound that keeps options open, which needs estimates on both sides. Those estimates are what the cruxes of Section 12.3 would supply, and the failure-mode table of X8, every path, each of its failure modes, whether it can be reversed and the measurement that would show it under way, is the base on which Section 12.1 compares. An expected-value criterion that puts low probability on a rising uncovered share and values speed highly can prefer continuation, and a discounted criterion with utility linear in consumption prefers racing whenever the hazard it adds is below the growth it adds. These disagreements are the normative cruxes. The analysis does not establish that every cooperative arrangement outperforms regulated competition, and Section 12 does not claim it.

Section 9 collects what any arrangement must do to move the thresholds for every capable actor, whatever it is called; Section 12 takes from this section the seven marks, the durability tests and the third step.
