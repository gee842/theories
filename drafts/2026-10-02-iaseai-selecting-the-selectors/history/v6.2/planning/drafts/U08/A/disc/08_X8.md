## X8. The alternatives in full

Each alternative is given its best case, how it would be installed, what remains, what it changes, and what evidence would show that it suffices. Section 8.3 of the core summarizes each; the formal results are in the core and S8. The chapter ends with the tables Section 12.1 compares on: the policies now in force profiled in advance, the failure modes of every path with their reversals and measurements, and the durability tests under one evidence standard.

### Technical alignment and AI-assisted correction

**Best case.** The strongest form is not "technical alignment alone". Control is scoped to early transformative systems, used under control to accelerate alignment and oversight research while the window lasts (Greenblatt, Shlegeris, Sachan and Roger 2024): control protocols tested against red teams raise safety at a given level of usefulness in the settings studied; weak supervisors can elicit part of a stronger model's capability (Burns et al. 2023); and in auditing games, teams with access to training data found a planted hidden objective that a team with black-box access alone did not, which supports mandated access (Marks et al. 2025). Oversight can be built redundantly or adversarially by debate, and formal methods certify invariants without predicting trajectories. Research that lowers the alignment tax, class by class, until the safe variant is also the winning one would remove the common constraint's reason for existing in that class ({{nested}}). AI may cheapen independent correction as fast as control ({{corrrate}}), and correction people cannot follow can still lower residual risk ({{opaquecorr}}). Its proponents' own plans rank a strong international agreement with a long slowdown first, and treat technical work as what one does when that is unavailable.

**What remains.** Principal alignment does not change the game among principals ({{faithful}}). The window lasts only as long as the race allows, and the race shortens it. Systems are retained for performance ({{retain}}), and on interior branches safety gains are absorbed by more delegation ({{homeostasis}}). Redundant oversight is capped by common causes, which selection produces, and certified row sums are a sufficient certificate, not a proof of independent errors ({{topology}}). For masked divergences, which change under evaluation or appear only in some contexts, admission removes only what it can detect, so for that class admission has to be stated in capability rather than propensity, with access to weights, activations and training data for auditors ({{masked}}; EV6). AI-assisted correction is only as independent as its funding, access and appointment. The common-cost part of incarnation is set at the outer loop ({{nested}}).

**What it changes.** Tempo, observation and residual harm per unit. It becomes part of a change of selection, G3 and G7, when held by correctors separated from competitive success, under constraints common to all. **What would show it suffices:** the safety of control protocols at a fixed audit budget not falling across generations; catch rates of planted defects at fixed human hours not falling as capability rises, controlled for evaluation awareness (C3a, C12); independent evaluators keeping certificates while depending on the evaluated for access; residual risk per hazard class falling over generations (C1).

### Regulated competition with strong correction

**Best case.** Competition in products and services continues; liability and licensing make consequences return; evaluation access is mandated and thresholds fixed in advance; and where the effective hazard is high and capability transparent, pacing can be the unique equilibrium of a capability race without any agreement (Fudenberg and Koh 2026). There are precedents of durable safety with unchanged commercial motives: an industry that knows one accident can end it for all, as civil nuclear power came to after a major accident; and aviation, with independent accident investigation, liability and insurance pricing. This is the bounded competition a cooperative order can contain (V5).

**Where the pacing equilibrium meets E3, and where it does not.** In the model of Fudenberg and Koh (2026) developers advance capability against a safety frontier that advances linearly; the regions of the parameter space are racing only, coexistence, and pacing only, and with high effective hazard and transparent capability pacing is the unique Markov perfect equilibrium, with no agreement among the developers. Where its conditions hold, this is E3 reached by equilibrium rather than by arrangement: pace bound to understanding, because the hazard of outrunning the frontier is seen and borne by every developer that would outrun it. The coincidence is exact under the model's premises and fails where this paper's premises differ from them, in four places. First, the effective hazard that selects the pacing region is the hazard as the developers see it; where hazards manifest late (reach) or are masked, the seen hazard is below the real one by exactly the gap on which the silent route runs ({{slowroute}}), so a path can lie in the racing region of the real parameters while its participants compute the pacing region. Second, transparency of capability is a premise, and masking, internal deployment and the open–closed gap are what remove it (EV5, EV6). Third, the model has no decisive position, no absorbing state beyond sanction; where one exists, outrunning the frontier is not only a hazard borne by the racer but a prize that ends the game, and the enforcement condition ({{enforce}}) rather than the hazard decides. Fourth, the model has neither a delegation channel nor a conversion channel: the position of the safety frontier is measured by someone, and success that buys the measurement moves the frontier instead of the pace ({{influence}}). The question the theory puts to this position is therefore not whether pacing equilibria exist, which they do, but whether the present path lies in their region. The measurements that answer it are the ones Section 7.3 already lists: late-manifesting hazard classes and their evidence lags (C1), the open–closed gap and internal deployment (C5), and the conversion data of T4 (C2). Where they place the path in the pacing region for a class, E3 for that class needs no agreement, and the paper says so; where they do not, pacing has to be made an equilibrium by the arrangements of Section 11.3.

**Installation.** Each jurisdiction regulates its own developers; coverage must reach every capable actor, since one actor can start the race; the rule-makers are themselves in a contest of standing, so no one of them imposes the liability alone ({{nounilateral}}); rule-making is subject to Section 4.

**What remains.** Liability cannot exceed the assets of the liable for catastrophic harm (Shavell 1986), and for a common harm the judgment-proof gap is where a common cost cannot be turned into a private one. Regulation in one market leaves the load elsewhere. Two developers' frameworks condition commitments on rivals, one of them stating that it cannot commit to mitigations unilaterally because of a collective action problem while keeping a discretion to pause; one unreviewed study codes 77% of the changes it traced across twelve pairs of framework versions as weakening (EV2). Public evaluation bodies have shifted their mandates toward industry contact under voluntary access (EV3). The precedents had no decisive prize; the pacing equilibrium has no delegation channel and assumes transparency.

**What it changes.** This depends on content. Regulation that returns consequences and separates evaluators from success, across every capable actor, is G1 and G3 for all and the change this paper argues for, whatever it is called. Regulation that constrains conduct while success can still buy the rule-makers is predicted to erode as stakes and speed grow. **What would show it suffices:** competitor clauses not invoked; thresholds held when rivals advance; the mandates of evaluation bodies widening as the stakes rise; findings executed against leaders (C2).

### Leadership and a safety margin

**Best case.** A leader spends part of its lead on safety: it slows, evaluates and hardens before the follower catches up. A large lead can discourage the follower (Harris and Vickers 1987; Konrad and Kovenock 2009); a laggard far behind under moderate hostility does not gamble on risky technology (Stafford, Trager and Dafoe 2022); and a developer able to commit first to a slightly slower speed gains, because rivals slow too, a local result that does not depend on holding a lead (Bueno de Mesquita, Dziuda and Polborn 2026). One developer's framework includes such a "leading" scenario (EV2).

**What remains.** {{lead}}: leads diffuse even when the follower does not accelerate; spending a lead returns the contest toward parity, where effort and the price of standing are highest (Aghion, Harris, Howitt and Vickers 2001; Section 7.2); the margin must buy coverage, and for hazard classes with an evidence lag above the margin it buys nothing; it is judged inside the contest; and a leader seeing its window close has a motive to strike first. **What it changes.** Tempo for a time; it keeps the objective of winning. Its best case, a leader slowing and inducing rivals to slow, is a step toward reciprocal restraint, the first stage of a transition. **What would show it suffices:** leads not spent on speed; rivals slowing in response; margins long relative to the evidence lags of the hazard classes.

### The benign winner: win, bind, share

**Best case.** The strongest form is that a winner wins, binds itself and shares. After great wars, victors have bound themselves through institutions to lower others' fear and their own costs of enforcement (Ikenberry 2001); a dominant power can supply public goods a field of equals would not (Kindleberger 1973; Gilpin 1981), and unipolarity can be stable (Wohlforth 1999); an encompassing ruler internalizes the shared conditions (Olson 1993): in the model of Section 3.5 a single developer restrains whenever restraint is worth more to the field than it costs, so the failure lies in the race, not in the existence of a winner. The holder of the first nuclear monopoly did not extend its dominance to everything it could reach. In AI the strategy has been proposed, in its authors' terms, as humanitarian and defensive: a coalition seeks a clear advantage, including military superiority and the denial of key inputs such as chips and semiconductor equipment to adversaries, and then offers its benefits to others in exchange for their support, so that adversaries give up competing (EV1). It persuades by promising both security and benefits; both rest on the winner's restraint after the fact, which is what the commitment tests examine. Most strong international institutions were built by victors, a real challenge to a path that assumes institutions built among rivals at rough parity.

**What remains.** The commitment tests: after a decisive win what the others receive is set by the winner's objective alone, and a promise to share is worth breaking ({{wingives}}); the race shifts winners toward those that extend dominance ({{raceselects}}); and the winner is itself a field of agencies, firms, laboratories and systems, whose restraint after victory depends on whether its own correctors can be bought by its successes. The paper's own result sharpens the strongest form rather than dismissing it. With logarithmic utility a winner whose held self-model is accurate at $\kappa_W$ gives $\kappa_W/(1+\kappa_W)$ ({{wingives}}), and a self-model accurate about real coupling is what Section 3 says survives; so the benign winner is exactly a winner that still depends on the governed after victory. The historical cases had that property: their advantages were not decisive, their restraint rested on institutions others could check, and their rule needed the cooperation of the ruled (Acemoglu and Robinson 2012). The general crux, C13, is whether AI removes that dependence, by supplying the labour, the force and the persuasion that the governed used to supply; a technology that does so lowers $\kappa_W$ for whoever holds it, and the benign winner's strongest form rests on the one quantity the race cannot verify beforehand and the technology tends to lower.

**What it changes.** Nothing in selection before victory; after victory, everything depends on the winner's internal field. **What would show it suffices**, before victory: binding limits on what victory would confer; benefits shared contemporaneously rather than promised; the coalition's own correctors, constitutional correctors included, certified with row sums below one, with succession rules that survive changes of leadership (Test I). A winner that adopts these has changed what it selects for.

### A finite sprint, then restraint

**Best case.** An actor sprints to a decisive lead and then stops compounding: it freezes or rolls back, and governs at the tempo of understanding; decisive contests are won with a modest edge when decisiveness is high, so the sprint need not run far beyond understanding. The objection that the sprinter's stop ends others' O2 is met by the reply that this paper's own E2 and common custody also stop everyone building the decisive class, and that the first nuclear monopoly was not extended; the reply deserves an answer by mechanism.

**What remains**, by type. *Empirical exposure:* the path passes through its greatest exposure, and through the rivals' strongest motive for prevention, before any restraint begins ({{preventattack}}). *Mechanism:* a decisive advantage not used to stop others' growth does not stay decisive, so the freeze is a stop imposed on everyone else by one party. That stop is a common constraint of the most unequal incidence: it is selected against and read as a bid for a lead ({{blind}}(2)), so the others' best reply under relative accounting is prevention or imitation, not compliance. And the sprinter as custodian of the frozen class is the largest conversion target the theory describes, since the gain from converting its evaluators scales with its share ({{influence}}; {{corrrate}}(4)), with no corrector outside it. *Normative:* maintaining the advantage requires stopping others' growth, which ends their O2 (V2, V3). The two properties a sprint would need, equal incidence and uncaptured correction, are what Sections 3 and 4 show erode. Under a survival-first comparison a sprint can preserve the sprinter's survival while giving up permanent superiority, which is not survival ({{sprint}}). **What it changes.** Nothing during the sprint.

**The sprinter's freeze against common custody, on D4 and D5.** Both hold the decisive class unraced; the rows below are what differ, and they are the whole difference.

| | The sprinter's freeze | Common custody (Section 10.5; X10) |
|---|---|---|
| Who bears the stop | Everyone but the holder; the holder keeps the frozen class | Everyone, the custodian's members included; release by $k$ of $n$ with non-aligned members among the $k$ |
| D4, who corrects the holder | The holder's own evaluators | An evaluation network of different lineage and funding, with row sums certified outside the holder; a chamber of the affected; appeal |
| D5, when the strongest violates | No one outside the holder; the holder's judgment of itself | Dependence caps and funded standby make restriction of the strongest possible without stopping services; the coercive fallback only under the custody rules and Section 12.1's three conditions; the hard case remains and is said to remain |
| What ends it | The holder's choice | A verifiable indicator per capability class; dissolution into separately held functions on coverage or certificate failure, with protections against domination entrenched |

The comparison does not make custody easy. It makes exact what the sprint lacks: incidence that includes the holder, and a corrector the holder did not appoint.

### Deterrence and prevention

Prevention can be survival-preferred for the preventer ({{preventown}}). The objections are three, and none is a change of criterion: normative, since it ends or endangers others' O1 and O2; epistemic, since aimed at a rival believed certain to race it destroys, when the rival is in fact survival-first, a game in which restraint was possible, and can lead the rival to act as the type it was feared to be (the spiral model; Jervis 1976; Kydd 1997), while shifts that cannot be observed raise the chance of mistaken prevention (Debs and Monteiro 2014); and technical, since detecting unobservable progress and striking at machine speed brings the delegation of Section 6.3. Deterrence by threatened sabotage aims to make a decisive sprint not worth attempting. *Best case:* large datacentres can be observed and compute thresholds made salient (Hendrycks, Schmidt and Wang 2025). *What remains:* red lines that are unclear and hard to monitor, and doubtful credibility of the threat of sabotage, make deterrence of this kind unstable unless they are refined (Abecassis 2025); while the value of winning stays large, timely detection and credibility approach their limits ({{verify}}); among nuclear-armed rivals credibility is the first problem, and under survival-first comparisons it can reverse; pushing AI into military capability strengthens the delegation pressure; verification that supports it can also supply targets (Coe and Vaynman 2020); and algorithmic progress erodes thresholds stated in compute (EV9). *What it changes:* it acts on $p_d$ by threat, and its fallback is war or race.

**The hard case.** Let a survival-first actor hold the prior $\mu$ that a capable rival holds an unbounded relative objective. When $\mu$ is low, its best course is to propose verifiable limits, and to take narrow E1 unilaterally and verifiably in its own strategic loops, the costly signal of {{signal}}. When $\mu$ is high, it compares denial, a coalition that stops the decisive class, and prevention. Coverage of every capable actor then comes from consent or from coercion. Denying the inputs to the decisive class is the coercive form of a stop; it raises the weight on winning and the trust threshold, and if it escalates it falls under {{preventown}}. It is compared, not recommended: Section 12.1 admits it as its own candidate only under three conditions, each a mechanism result, equal incidence of the stop including on the restrictor ({{blind}}(2)), execution by correctors certified independent of the restrictor ({{influence}}), and the consent of the chamber of the affected (V3, R7), with its escalation risk counted in $\Theta$. Even then the theory does not derive that its net effect is positive and does not endorse it. If neither consent nor a coercion of positive net effect is available, no actor has a secure position: that is the hard case, stated as such. Insuring against falling behind is not an exit ({{insure}}).

### A verified agreement over a contest that remains

**The position, and one instance of it.** The position is to keep the contest, slow it and stay safe: rivals agree to slow and to delay decisive capability, with compute declarations, supply-chain accounting, recomputation of workloads, research transparency, and mutually assured destruction of compute if the agreement dissolves; it works without trust and bounds the largest covert project that could escape detection. The theory answers the position, by {{assure}}, {{verify}} and {{overhang}}; a plan is one instance, cited so that the tests can be applied to documents rather than to a type. One detailed plan of this kind, a deal among leading powers to delay superintelligence to about 2040 (Larsen et al. 2026; EV10), adds inference-only retrofits of datacentres and hardened reserves of about two per cent of pre-deal compute, and aims to end winner-take-all dynamics through many frontier developers, transparency, broad diffusion and a citizens' dividend. Its own scenario estimates do not show near-certain dissolution: one author's judgment puts decline in years five to ten at about four per cent a year, half dissolution and half impairment, and cumulative decline over ten years near half. Its handoff to AI systems is a conditional decision that weighs further alignment research against other risks, and it keeps an option to extend the pause.

**What remains.** Verification is required exactly to the degree that the value of winning exceeds the value of cooperating, and the net gain from exit can grow with the capability held in check ({{verify}}; {{overhang}}); the detection it needs is the same quantity the enforcement condition needs, seen from the other side ({{detectlink}}). **What it changes.** It slows and caps, acting on tempo and decisiveness, and keeps relative standing in its enforcement, through proportional reserves and a coalition that must stay ahead of covert projects; the conversion of success into control over correction is addressed only through transparency. That makes it a bridge. Whether it can be the end depends on the durability tests and on whether it lowers the value of winning, G2 and G5, or only bounds defection. Common custody faces the same verification limits as any agreement; what differs is its fallback, and whether it lowers the value of winning, a claim with the same standing as any agreement's.

The register has no base rate for the survival and capture of international regulatory bodies, which is the analogue class for the design's D2; EVIDENCE records the gap. Until it is filled, the design's D2 cell is empty by the same rule that fills the agreement's cell with a scenario judgment, and neither is an observed frequency.

**Where the criteria disagree.** Under the protection standard the worst case includes a rising uncovered share on the continuation path, because its premises are named mechanisms the register does not contradict (EV5–EV7); it equally includes the capture of a common custodian on the cooperative path, because its correctors' certificates can fail ({{certificate}}). Both fail the tolerance in their worst case, and the comparison moves to the standard's third step, which needs estimates on both sides. An expected-value criterion that puts low probability on a rising gap and values speed highly can prefer continuation; a discounted criterion with utility linear in consumption prefers racing whenever the hazard it adds is below the growth it adds, and with curvature in utility it stops (S8). A well-corrected continuation can outperform a fragile cooperative arrangement; the analysis does not establish that every cooperative arrangement outperforms regulated competition.
