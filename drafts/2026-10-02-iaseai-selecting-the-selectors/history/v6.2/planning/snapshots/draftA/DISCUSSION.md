### The vocabulary of winning, and its critics

The doctrine of winning has at least two strands. *Within firms and public institutions:* an essay of 1970 argued that the social responsibility of business is to increase its profits within the rules of the game, which include law and ethical custom (Friedman 1970); agency theory recast the firm around aligning managers with owners (Jensen and Meckling 1976); a leading business association declared shareholder primacy the purpose of the corporation in 1997 and revised that statement in 2019; and market criteria were carried into institutions generally (Harvey 2005). In that turn self-interest moved from an assumption inside models to a norm of conduct and a criterion of design [obs, interpretive; EV15]. The doctrine's strongest form is that the residual claimant bears the residual risk, so that with complete contracts and externalities priced by law, maximizing the residual maximizes surplus; that separation fails where externalities cannot be separated from production decisions (Hart and Zingales 2017), which can be read as the unpriced coupling $\kappa$ [interpretive]. An early defence of commerce was that interests would tame the passions for glory and domination (Hirschman 1977); read that way, the pressure of winning is the passion for domination returning in the dress of interest, not the commercial tradition itself. *Between states:* doctrines of national survival and technological rivalry, from the Melian dialogue (Thucydides) and the Legalist programme of enriching the state and strengthening its army to the national Darwinism of late- and early-industrializing states alike and the technology races of the Cold War.

Both strands appear across civilizations, and so do their critics: Smith on sympathy; Darwin on the social instincts; Kropotkin on mutual aid; Polanyi on markets embedded in society; Ostrom on governing the commons; and, in China, Mozi's impartial care and condemnation of offensive war, and Kang Youwei's *Book of Great Unity*. Translated into doctrines of national survival in states that industrialized late, the strand was answered there too. Yan Fu rendered Huxley's 1893 lecture, whose point was that ethical progress consists in resisting the cosmic process, as a doctrine of 物竞天择, "things contend and heaven selects" (Yan 1898; Schwartz 1964); others set mutual aid against struggle and imagined the removal of boundaries. These histories illustrate how the pressure is narrated; they are not an account of any civilization's essence.

**The tradition behind V4.** One strand of the Chinese tradition the author draws on justifies authority by improving people's lives and contributing to a better historical trajectory. Like every tradition, that one also contains strands that justified power by strength. The theory takes the ideal as a positive account of what power is for; it does not assume that every institution realizes it, that any other tradition has one uniform motive, or that power necessarily corrupts.

### Ψ in full

*Its borrowed authority is a misreading.* "Survival of the fittest" was Spencer's phrase (1864), adopted by Darwin from the fifth edition of the *Origin*; Spencer himself expected societies to move from a militant to an industrial type. Darwinian arguments were later recruited for laissez-faire, and equally for the racism, eugenics and imperialism of reformers who rejected laissez-faire (Hofstadter 1944, who popularized the label; Leonard 2009); the label was largely a critics' term (Bannister 1979), and the borrowing belongs to no single strand of thought. *It misdescribes human beings.* In experiments in fifteen small-scale societies the canonical self-interest model failed in every society, and cooperation rose with market integration (Henrich et al. 2001); about half the subjects of a public-goods experiment were conditional cooperators (Fischbacher, Gächter and Fehr 2001); users govern commons without either a sovereign or privatization where institutions fit the resource (Ostrom 1990), though those commons were bounded and small, and scaling their design principles to global commons is itself an open problem (Stern 2011). Markets are institutions of cooperation among strangers; the target is the reading of them as a contest of all against all (EV14). Students of economics defected more often in a prisoner's dilemma, a difference later work attributes mainly to who chooses the subject (Frank, Gilovich and Regan 1993). *Held and attributed.* Realism's strongest reply to verifiable conduct is that conduct verified today does not settle uncertainty about a rival's future intentions (Copeland 2000b; Mearsheimer 2001), which is why the arrangements of Sections 10 and 11 verify capabilities and custody, which can be checked, rather than intentions.

**Status.** The desire for status, meaning respect, admiration and voluntary deference from others and distinct from power, is a fundamental human motive, observed across cultures, genders, ages and personalities (Anderson, Hildreth and Howland 2015). Status is relative, so it is never completed. Rank has two routes (Henrich and Gil-White 2001): dominance, gained through fear and control, and prestige, deference freely conferred on skill and success, and both yield real influence (Cheng et al. 2013). The drive is for respect; dominance is a substitute route to rank. Prestige also compounds (Merton 1968), which is why the condition on a generative order is that it not convert into control over the shared conditions or their correctors (EV16).

### The strongest objection from biology

Within-group cooperation was often favoured by competition between groups; Darwin's account of the social instincts rested on competition between tribes, and parochial altruism, cooperation inside and hostility outside, may have co-evolved with intergroup conflict (Choi and Bowles 2007). The answer is Lemma 3.11: a benefit common to all is never favoured by selection at any level of grouping, while a benefit confined to one group can be favoured between groups. At the scale of one host the benefits of the rules that keep it viable are of the first kind. Two routes to integration that need no rival group remain, and the core keeps both: polycentric trial among sub-units, as in nested commons governance and experiments within federations, which works wherever failure is not final; and reputation and indirect reciprocity, which change within-group payoffs without design (Panchanathan and Boyd 2004). What the core claims is narrower than in v6.0: failures that are global and irreversible cannot be learned by trial (Price 1972; Hamilton 1975).

### International relations

The logic of Section 3.8 has two sources: actors hold it, and selection on actions rewards its behaviour; and structure produces it, since anarchy, decisive advantage and the absence of a commitment device produce outcomes of the same kind among actors who do not hold it (Propositions 7.3, 5.4). An actor holding it does not stop compounding, because no level of standing makes more stop rewarding; on a continuation branch where the premises of Section 5.3 hold, Theorem 5.13 applies to its own capability too. To oppose the logic is to change the structure and the selection environment, not to oppose any actor: credible, verifiable limits on what anyone would do with decisive advantage; no decisive advantage without a commitment device; and common knowledge that the logic endangers its holder. In the vocabulary of international relations this is defensive realism with one more variable (Jervis 1978; Glaser 1994/95, 2010): a hazard in development that endangers its holder acts as a negative payoff to the offense, as a weapon that endangers its holder does, and the Biological Weapons Convention is the closest precedent. To the extent that the offense has the advantage and offense and defense are hard to tell apart, AI sits in the doubly dangerous quadrant of the offense–defense framework, where cooperation is hardest. The outcomes of the enforcement condition correspond to constructivism's three cultures of anarchy (Wendt 1999): enmity, where only stage equilibria hold; rivalry, where cooperation is enforced; friendship, where it is built into objectives. The theory adds the selection that moves a system between them, and the decisiveness that collapses the middle one. Balancing by others against a rising actor (Waltz 1979; Walt 1987) is a saturation mechanism independent of technology, and Proposition 5.16(2) is its counterpart in talk.

### The alignment literature

The insufficiency of single-principal alignment follows from the multiplicity thesis of Critch and Krueger (2020), developed in work on robust agent-agnostic processes (Critch 2021), societal-scale risks (Critch and Russell 2023), natural selection among AI systems (Hendrycks 2023), cooperative AI (Dafoe et al. 2020), multi-agent risk, which already lists selection pressures as a risk factor (Hammond et al. 2025), and gradual disempowerment (Kulveit et al. 2025). Section 6.2 is an elementary illustration of that thesis. What is added is the mechanism by which the game reproduces itself and its correctors: selection on relative standing that is blind to what all share, with no misalignment needed; the second-order selection for control over correction; and the nested reward loop with its alignment-tax premise. Multi-agent failures include miscoordination and collusion among AI systems as well as conflict among principals, and the stress tests count them.

### What is absorbed, and what is new

Much of the anatomy is known and absorbed: multipolar traps; realism's account of competition under anarchy (Waltz 1979; Mearsheimer 2001; Glaser 2010; Jervis 1976); Goodhart's and Campbell's laws; regulatory capture (Stigler 1971; Laffont and Tirole 1991; Dal Bó 2006); increasing returns and lock-in (Arthur 1989); the systems archetypes "success to the successful" and "escalation" (Senge 1990; Sterman 2000); self-help as an institution (Wendt 1992); security communities (Deutsch et al. 1957); the translation invariance of relative selection (Weibull 1995), which is Lemma 3.3(1); Powell's (1991) account of relative gains; the "generator functions" of catastrophic risk; and the AI literature above. The theory adds:
1. the reflexive closure as a specified selection mechanism, with its certificates, rate law, keystones and pause design (Section 4);
2. the interconnection of the conditions: one loop gain that decides compounding and prices standing, and the shared parameters that link it to enforcement, correction and talk (Propositions 2.2, 3.9);
3. the enforcement condition and the scissors: how winning moves each of its terms (Proposition 5.4);
4. the compounding condition with provision not won in the contest, the formal content of trees (Proposition 2.3);
5. the two-channel accounting identity (Proposition 3.5);
6. how common constraints survive: not the invisibility itself, which is absorbed, but its connection to incidence, evasion and the capture of enforcement (Lemma 3.3(2)(3));
7. AI as instrument and as weak and strong incarnation, with the alignment-tax premise and the nested loop (Section 6);
8. at the scale of one host, what can be learned by trial and what must be anticipated, and where understanding acts (Lemma 3.11).

**Endogenous measurement and preference [conj].** The masking model is the simplest form of epistemic closure: reality exists and the sensor is biased. Deeper forms are listed for research (TESTS, Study 4): capture of interpretation; capture of the concepts in which a failure could be expressed, so that the variable itself disappears from what the system can represent; and capture of preference, in which people no longer regard the state as harm. Each is a further step of the reflexive closure; none is modelled here.

## X2. Substrates

The pressure needs only selection on relative success, convertible winnings and, for its closure, the ability of the successful to reshape their selection. The following are demonstrations; none is the theory's ground.

- **Cells.** A cancer lineage evades the signals that limit proliferation, recruits the surrounding tissue into supporting its expansion, and evades the immune response that would remove it (Hanahan and Weinberg 2011; Aktipis et al. 2015). Growth is not the fault line: large animals, with far more cells, show no higher cancer risk than small ones, because suppression scales with size (Peto's paradox; Caulin and Maley 2011).
- **Firms.** Markets with increasing returns lock in early leads (Arthur 1989). The doctrine that the purpose of business is profit "so long as it stays within the rules of the game" (Friedman 1970) meets its limit where the successful can buy the rules. The same school analysed that possibility (Stigler 1971), and its remedy was to shrink discretionary rule-making that could be captured. The disagreement is about the remedy: a planetary technology with unpriced coupling has no option of making no rules, and where common rules are absent, the winners' systems make them in fact. The doctrine's own argument that managers spending others' money on social goals tax without representation supports public, independent correction, not voluntary self-regulation by firms.
- **States.** In security competition a gain for one is a threat to another, and falling behind is read as fatal.
- **Federations.** Members compete for capital and residents, and a race to the bottom in standards follows; the strongest can capture the centre; common standards and fiscal equalization change what the federation selects for.
- **Science.** Metrics allocate positions, and the positions set the metrics.
- **Training loops.** A policy is selected by a reward a developer sets, and the developer is selected by a contest; pairwise preference comparisons see only differences between responses.
- **Another planet.** Nothing in Definition 2.1 refers to a culture, a political system or a species. A civilization on another planet, with other drives and institutions, would face the same pressure wherever selection is relative, winnings convert and the successful can edit the selection.

### Technical alignment and AI-assisted correction

**Best case.** The strongest form is not "technical alignment alone". Control is scoped to early transformative systems and used, under control, to accelerate alignment and oversight research while the window lasts (Greenblatt, Shlegeris, Sachan and Roger 2024): control protocols tested against red teams raise safety at a given level of usefulness in the settings studied. Weak supervisors can elicit part of a stronger model's capability (Burns et al. 2023); in auditing games, teams with access to training data found a planted hidden objective that a team with black-box access alone did not, which supports mandated access (Marks et al. 2025). Oversight can be built redundantly or adversarially by debate, and formal methods certify invariants without predicting trajectories. AI may cheapen independent correction as fast as control, so that AI-assisted correction keeps pace (Proposition 4.5), and correction people cannot follow can still lower residual risk (Proposition S6.4). Research can drive the alignment tax to zero or below, class by class, until the safe variant is also the winning one; once published it is common to all, so the safeguards it makes cheap spread without coordination, and the common constraint loses its reason to exist in that class (Section 6.4; Proposition 6.7). Its proponents' own plans rank a strong international agreement with a long slowdown first, and treat technical work as what one does when that is unavailable.

**What remains.** Principal alignment does not change the game among principals (Proposition 6.1). The window lasts only as long as the race allows, and the race shortens it. Systems are retained for performance (Lemma 6.5), and progress that lowers the hazard per unit of racing or delegation is partly absorbed by more of both (Proposition S6.2). Redundant oversight is capped by common causes, which selection produces, and certified row sums are a sufficient certificate, not a proof of independent errors (Proposition 6.9). AI-assisted correction is only as independent as its funding, access, appointment and lineage. One part of the tax no technique removes: where countering a divergence means waiting for evidence that arrives after a manifestation window, its cost is time, which relative selection with decisive advantage always prices (Proposition 5.12). For masked divergences, which change under evaluation or appear only in some contexts, admission removes only what it detects; admission for that class has to be stated in capability, enforced in common, with access to weights, activations and training data for auditors, and beyond the threshold E2 or E3 for the class (Remark 6.8; EV6). Which divergences are tolerated is set at the outermost loop (Proposition 6.7).

**What it changes.** Tempo, observation and residual harm per unit, and the alignment tax where the research that lowers it is published. It becomes part of a change of selection, G3 and G7, when held by correctors separated from competitive success, under constraints common to all. **What would show it suffices:** the safety of control protocols at a fixed audit budget not falling across generations; catch rates of planted defects at fixed human hours not falling as capability rises, controlled for evaluation awareness (C3a); auditors with fixed access still finding planted and emergent hidden objectives as capability rises (C12); independent evaluators keeping certificates while depending on the evaluated for access; the tax measured at zero or below, class by class (C9); residual risk per hazard class falling over generations (C1).

### Regulated competition with strong correction

**Best case.** Competition in products and services continues; liability and licensing make consequences return; evaluation access is mandated and thresholds fixed in advance; and where the effective hazard is high and capability transparent, pacing can be the unique equilibrium of a capability race without any agreement (Fudenberg and Koh 2026). There are precedents of durable safety with unchanged commercial motives: an industry that knows one accident can end it for all, as civil nuclear power came to after a major accident; and aviation, with independent accident investigation, liability and insurance pricing. This is the bounded competition a cooperative order can contain (V5).

**Installation.** Each jurisdiction regulates its own developers; coverage must reach every capable actor, since the race is set by the most race-inclined actor (Proposition 5.1); the rule-makers are themselves in a contest of standing, so no one of them imposes the liability alone (Proposition 9.2); rule-making is subject to Section 4.

**What remains.** Liability cannot exceed the assets of the liable (Shavell 1986): for catastrophic and common harms that is the point at which a common cost cannot be turned into a private one, which is why liability alone does not settle the alignment tax. Regulation in one market leaves the load elsewhere. Two developers' frameworks condition commitments on rivals, one of them stating that it cannot commit to mitigations unilaterally because of a collective action problem while keeping a discretion to pause; one unreviewed study codes 77% of the changes it traced, in twelve pairs of consecutive framework versions, as weakening (EV2). Public evaluation bodies have shifted their mandates toward industry contact under voluntary access (EV3). The precedents had no decisive prize.

**The pacing equilibrium, compared with E3.** The strongest formal result for this path deserves more than a clause. As the register reads it, from the abstract only (EV11), Fudenberg and Koh (2026) study a capability race against a linearly advancing safety frontier and find regions of racing only, of coexistence and of pacing only; with a high effective hazard and transparent capability, pacing can be the unique Markov perfect equilibrium, with no agreement among the developers. Where that holds, it is E3 reached as an equilibrium of bounded competition: capability paced to what can be made safe, because the hazard of outrunning the frontier is seen and borne by whoever would outrun it. This paper does not oppose it, and a cooperative order would contain it. The correspondence that follows is this paper's reading, and it turns on the result's two conditions, which are where the mechanisms of Part I bear.
- *The effective hazard* is the part of a hazard that the objective an actor acts on counts, and that it sees. Relative accounting weighs a shared hazard by $1+\theta$ and drops it at $\theta=-1$ (Proposition 3.5), and compounding narrows the working objective as the discounted loop gain rises (Proposition 3.9), so the effective hazard falls exactly where the gain rises. Where harm manifests late, or is masked, the hazard seen is below the real one by the gap on which the silent route runs (Proposition 4.13): a path can lie in the racing region of the real parameters while its participants compute the pacing region.
- *Transparency* fails where the conditions of winning predict that it fails: the quiet deviation is the one that binds enforcement (Proposition 5.4), captured evaluation masks the harm (Proposition 4.8), masked divergences hide under evaluation (Remark 6.8), and internal deployment and the open–closed gap hide capability (EV5, EV6).
- *What the comparison adds.* Where outrunning the frontier can yield a decisive position, the prize ends the game and the enforcement condition, not the hazard, decides (Proposition 5.4). Speed can also be bought outside the frontier by delegation (Section 6.3), and the frontier's position is measured by someone whom success can buy (Proposition 4.3).

So the pacing equilibrium is not an alternative to the marks of Section 8.2 but an argument for two of them: G1, consequences return to their source, keeps the effective hazard high; G6, measurement and language are shared, supplies the transparency. Where both hold for every capable actor, regulated competition and the generative direction coincide. The question this result puts is therefore not whether pacing equilibria exist, which in that model they do, but whether the present path lies in their region, class by class. The measurements that answer it are those of Section 7.3 and the cruxes of Section 12.3: late-manifesting hazard classes and their evidence lags (C1), the open–closed gap and internal deployment (C5), and the conversion data of T4 (C2). Where they place a class in the pacing region, E3 for that class needs no agreement, and the paper says so; where they do not, pacing has to be made an equilibrium by the arrangements of Section 11.3. The correspondence should be checked against the full paper in outside review.

**What it changes.** This depends on content. Regulation that returns consequences and separates evaluators from success, across every capable actor, is G1 and G3 for all, changes the basin, and is the change this paper argues for, whatever it is called. Regulation that constrains conduct while success can still buy the rule-makers is predicted to erode as stakes and speed grow. **What would show it suffices:** competitor clauses not invoked; thresholds held when rivals advance; the mandates of evaluation bodies widening as the stakes rise; findings executed against leaders (C2); effective liability above the private advantage of load for every capable carrier.

### Leadership and a safety margin

**Best case.** A leader spends part of its lead on safety: it slows, evaluates and hardens before the follower catches up. A large lead can discourage the follower (Harris and Vickers 1987; Konrad and Kovenock 2009); a laggard far behind under moderate hostility does not gamble on risky technology (Stafford, Trager and Dafoe 2022); and a developer able to commit first to a slightly slower speed gains, because rivals slow too, a local result that does not depend on holding a lead (Bueno de Mesquita, Dziuda and Polborn 2026). One developer's framework includes such a "leading" scenario (EV2).

**What remains.** Remark S8.2: leads diffuse even when the follower does not accelerate; the margin must buy coverage, and buys nothing for hazard classes whose evidence lags exceed it; it is judged inside the contest; and a leader seeing its window close has a motive to strike first. Spending the lead returns the contest toward parity, where effort is highest in contests of step-by-step innovation (Aghion, Harris, Howitt and Vickers 2001) and, where a lead discourages the follower, the price of standing peaks in the contests computed (Section 7.2). **What it changes.** Tempo for a time; it keeps the objective of winning. Its best case, a first commitment to slow that rivals answer by slowing, is a step toward reciprocal restraint, the first stage of a transition. **What would show it suffices:** leads not spent on speed; rivals slowing in response; margins long relative to the evidence lags of the hazard classes.

### The benign winner: win, bind, share

**Best case.** The strongest form is that a winner wins, binds itself and shares. After great wars, victors have bound themselves through institutions to lower others' fear and their own costs of enforcement (Ikenberry 2001); a dominant power can supply public goods a field of equals would not (Kindleberger 1973; Gilpin 1981), and unipolarity can be stable (Wohlforth 1999); an encompassing ruler internalizes the shared conditions (Olson 1993). In the model of Section 3.5 a single developer restrains whenever restraint is worth more to the field than it costs, so the failure lies in the race, not in the existence of a winner. The holder of the first nuclear monopoly, through the period of superiority to 1954, did not extend its dominance to everything it could reach (X11). In AI the strategy has been proposed, in its authors' terms, as humanitarian and defensive: a coalition seeks a clear advantage, including military superiority and the denial of key inputs such as chips and semiconductor equipment to adversaries, and then offers its benefits to others in exchange for their support, so that adversaries give up competing (EV1). It persuades by promising both security and benefits; both rest on the winner's restraint after the fact, which is what the commitment tests examine. By this paper's own results the form is strongest where the winner still needs the governed: a winner whose self-model is accurate toward what can be divided, $\theta=\kappa_W$, gives them $\kappa_W/(1+\kappa_W)$ under logarithmic utility (Proposition 5.8; Proposition 3.6(2)). Most strong international institutions were built by victors; that is the record this path appeals to, and the record a transition among rivals near parity has to answer (X11).

**What remains.** The commitment tests. After a decisive win what the others receive is set by the winner's objective alone, and a promise to share is worth breaking (Proposition 5.8). What the race cannot verify beforehand is $\kappa_W$ after victory: a winner, like any institution, stays answerable because it depends on the labour, consent and judgment of the people it governs, and AI can substitute for each (Kulveit et al. 2025). The historical cases had advantages that were not decisive, and their restraint rested on institutions others could check and on that dependence (Acemoglu and Robinson 2012); whether AI removes it is crux C13. Where conduct before victory and after it go together, the race shifts winners toward those that extend dominance (Proposition 7.3). And the winner is itself a field of agencies, firms, laboratories and systems, whose restraint after victory depends on whether its own correctors can be bought by its successes; an AI aligned to the winner is not thereby aligned to the people it governs (Section 6.3).

**What it changes.** Nothing in selection before victory; after victory, everything depends on the winner's internal field. **What would show it suffices**, before victory: binding limits on what victory would confer; benefits shared as they arise rather than promised; the coalition's own correctors, constitutional correctors included, certified with row sums below one, with succession rules that survive changes of leadership (Test I); and a measured dependence of the coalition on the people it would govern that AI does not erode (C13). A winner that adopts these has changed what it selects for.

### A finite sprint, then restraint

**Best case.** An actor sprints to a decisive lead and then stops compounding: it freezes or rolls back, and governs at the tempo of understanding. Decisive contests are won with a modest edge when $r$ is large, so the sprint need not run far beyond understanding. A winner that freezes or rolls back keeps positive survival (Proposition 5.14), and after a decisive win no contest remains to price standing, so the pressure of Section 3.5 no longer acts on the winner.

**What remains, by kind of objection.**
- *Exposure*, an empirical objection: the path passes through its greatest exposure before any restraint begins, and through the rivals' strongest motive for preventive attack (Proposition 5.9); whether that exposure is survivable is crux C1 for the hazards of development and C5 for decisiveness.
- *Maintenance*, a normative objection: a decisive advantage not used to stop others' growth does not stay decisive, and using it so ends their O2, which V2 and V3 protect (Remark S8.3). Under a survival-first comparison a sprint can preserve the sprinter's survival; what the freeze gives up is permanent superiority, which survival does not need.
- *Mechanism*, the paper's own answer, which needs neither of the others. The freeze is a stop imposed by one actor on the rest, a constraint of the most unequal incidence a stop can have; those it binds are re-sorted by it and evade it wherever evasion pays their type (Lemma 3.3(2), (3)), so it holds only by a sanction the sprinter supplies, and it reads to every rival as a lead being kept. And it is held by the largest conversion target there is: the return to influence over correctors scales with the square of share (Proposition 4.3), and cultivated conversion with a high moment of shares (Proposition 4.5(4)), so whatever is to check the freeze is what the sprinter's success can most cheaply buy.

**Against common custody.** E2 and common custody also stop every actor from building the decisive class, and a coalition that stops outsiders need not end their O2 (the non-proliferation row of X11). So the stop is not the difference. Incidence and holder are, and the durability tests show them, read by the same standard on both sides:

| | Sprint, then freeze | Common custody (Section 10.5; X10) |
|---|---|---|
| Who bears the stop | Every actor except the sprinter, which keeps the frozen class; selected to be evaded (Lemma 3.3) | Everyone, the custodians' own capability included, by design; release by $k$ of $n$ with non-aligned members among the $k$; whether incidence is equal in effect is to be measured |
| D4 Who corrects the holder | The sprinter's own evaluators, held by the largest carrier and the largest conversion target (Proposition 4.3) | Functions held separately, funded by formula and appointed insulated from success, with evaluators of different lineage and funding certified outside the holder, a chamber of the affected and an appeal, by design; the arrangement is itself a large conversion target, and its certificates are not yet measured (C4) |
| D5 When the strongest violates | The strongest is the custodian; its violation answers to no one | Dependence caps and funded standby, so that restricting the strongest need not stop services; execution first by its host jurisdiction, then a coercive fallback compared, not settled (Section 12.1); the hard case remains |
| What ends it | The holder's choice | A verifiable indicator per capability class; dissolution into separately held functions on coverage or certificate failure, with protections against domination entrenched |

The comparison does not make custody easy. It makes exact what the sprint lacks: incidence that includes the holder, and a corrector the holder did not appoint.

**What it changes.** Nothing during the sprint; after it, a stop of the most unequal incidence held by the largest conversion target.

### Deterrence and prevention

Prevention can be survival-preferred for the preventer (Proposition 8.5). The objections are three, and none is a change of criterion. *Normative:* it ends or endangers others' O1 and O2. *Epistemic:* aimed at a rival believed certain to race, it destroys, when the rival is in fact survival-first, a game in which restraint was possible, and can lead the rival to act as the type it was feared to be (the spiral model; Jervis 1976; Kydd 1997), while shifts that cannot be observed raise the chance of mistaken prevention (Debs and Monteiro 2014). *Technical:* detecting unobservable progress and striking at machine speed brings the delegation of Section 6.3.

Deterrence by threatened sabotage aims to make a decisive sprint not worth attempting. *Best case:* large datacentres can be observed and compute thresholds made salient (Hendrycks, Schmidt and Wang 2025). *What remains:* red lines that are unclear and hard to monitor, and doubtful credibility of the threat of sabotage, make deterrence of this kind unstable unless they are refined (Abecassis 2025); while the value of winning stays large, timely detection and credibility approach their limits (Theorem 8.7); among nuclear-armed rivals credibility is the first problem, and under survival-first comparisons it can reverse; pushing AI into military capability strengthens the pressure to delegate (Proposition 6.2); verification that supports deterrence can also supply targets (Coe and Vaynman 2020); and algorithmic progress erodes thresholds stated in compute (EV9). *What it changes:* it acts on $p_d$ by threat, and its fallback is war or race.

### The hard case

Let a survival-first actor hold the prior $\mu$ that a capable rival holds an unbounded relative objective. When $\mu$ is low, its best course is to propose verifiable limits, and to take narrow E1 unilaterally and verifiably in its own strategic loops, the costly signal of Proposition 11.2. When $\mu$ is high, it compares denial, a coalition that stops the decisive class, and prevention. Coverage of every capable actor then comes from consent or from coercion. Denying the inputs to the decisive class is the coercive form of a stop; it raises the weight on winning and the trust threshold, and if it escalates it falls under Proposition 8.5. The theory does not derive that its net effect is positive and does not endorse it. Section 12.1 admits the coercive restriction of frontier inputs to its comparison only under three conditions of its own, and even there compares it rather than recommends it. If neither consent nor a coercion of positive net effect is available, no actor has a secure position: that is the hard case, stated as such. Insuring against falling behind is not an exit (Remark S8.4): it accepts the frame of the contest, and against a winner of the logic Section 3.8 describes, falling behind cannot be made survivable.

### A verified agreement over a contest that remains

**Best case.** Rivals agree to slow and to delay decisive capability, with compute declarations, supply-chain accounting, recomputation of workloads, research transparency, and mutually assured destruction of compute if the agreement dissolves; it works without trust and bounds the largest covert project that could escape detection. One detailed plan of this kind, a deal among leading powers to delay superintelligence to about 2040 (Larsen et al. 2026; EV10), adds inference-only retrofits of datacentres and hardened reserves of about two per cent of pre-deal compute, and aims to end winner-take-all dynamics through many frontier developers, transparency, broad diffusion and a citizens' dividend. Its own scenario estimates do not show near-certain dissolution: one author's judgment puts decline in years five to ten at about 4% a year, half dissolution and half impairment, and cumulative decline over ten years at about 48% (EV10). Its handoff to AI systems is a conditional decision that weighs further alignment research against other risks, and it keeps an option to extend the pause. The plan is one instance of a position, keep the contest, slow it and stay safe; it is cited so that the tests can be applied to documents rather than to a type, and the answer below is to the position, not to the plan's figures.

**What remains.** Verification is required exactly to the degree that the value of winning exceeds the value of cooperating; its threshold is the deterrence condition of the inspection game (Avenhaus, von Stengel and Zamir 2002), the enforcement condition seen from verification (Remark 5.5); and the net gain from exit can grow with the capability held in check (Theorem 8.7; Proposition S8.5). The bound on covert projects holds only while the coalition's compute advantage keeps pace with the diffusion of algorithmic efficiency, which is not derived (EV9).

**What it changes.** It slows and caps, acting on tempo and decisiveness, and keeps relative standing in its enforcement, through proportional reserves and a coalition that must stay ahead of covert projects; the conversion of success into control over correction is addressed only through transparency. An agreement that preserves relative positions preserves the relative term of every payoff, and with it the motive to exit (Proposition S8.5(3)). That makes it a bridge. Whether it can be the end depends on the durability tests and on whether it lowers the value of winning, as G2 and G5 do, or only bounds defection. Common custody faces the same verification limits as any agreement; what differs is its fallback, and whether it lowers the value of winning, a claim with the same standing as any agreement's.

### Where the criteria disagree

Under the protection standard the worst case includes a rising uncovered share on the continuation path, because its premises are named mechanisms the register does not contradict (EV5–EV7); it equally includes the capture of a common custodian on the cooperative path, because its correctors' certificates can fail (Proposition 4.10), and the other failure modes of the table above. Both fail the tolerance in their worst case, and the comparison moves to the standard's third step, which needs estimates on both sides. An expected-value criterion that puts low probability on a rising gap and values speed highly can prefer continuation; a discounted criterion with utility linear in consumption prefers racing whenever the hazard it adds is below the growth it adds, and with curvature in utility it stops (S8). A well-corrected continuation can outperform a fragile cooperative arrangement; the analysis does not establish that every cooperative arrangement outperforms regulated competition.

## X10. The design in full

One configuration is developed far enough to expose its assumptions. It is one candidate among those Section 12.1 compares under one range of uncertainty: a polycentric complex of mutually recognized institutions, a verification agreement together with shared provision, regulated competition meeting the requirements of Section 9, an agreement of the type of Section 8.3(g) and the benign winner are others, and the coercive restriction of frontier inputs enters that comparison only under conditions of its own (Section 12.1). The text does not claim the configuration is superior; which design is best is part of crux C4. It is called here a *compact*: an arrangement for shared AI provision and correction, made of separately held functions rather than one institution. Each clause below is written so that a party under assessment cannot turn it against the assessment, and the hardest functions, execution and the fallback, carry the financing, appointment and certificates that the others carry.

### Functions, held separately

Each function is held by a different body, separately financed and separately corrected, so that correction lies outside what it corrects. On a planet, "outside" means outside the function: a different source of funds, a different lineage, and certificates anyone can recompute from public data. Every body has a rule of financing, a rule of appointment, a corrector, and a condition that ends or reverts it, so that each can answer the withdrawal question of the guardian test (Remark 10.5), and every body whose findings or decisions have force carries a full inbound row-sum certificate (Proposition 4.10(2)), recomputed on a schedule from the data of the disclosure registry. Three funds are fed by formula from the levy below: a *service fund* for provision, a *correction fund* for standard-setting, evaluation, disclosure, the chamber and the appeal, each by its own formula line, and an *execution fund* for execution and custody. The rules body is financed by treaty contributions outside the levy, so that the body that sets the formula receives nothing from it.

**Separation rule.** No body both revises the levy formula and appoints evaluators, and neither of those executes findings. Each has its own row-sum certificate. Supermajorities and the chamber's consent apply to the slow loops, amendment and the formula; the fast loop, restricting a specific capability, stays fast, with review after the fact, because every veto point adds delay, and the robustness margin of Proposition 5.12 depends on it.

**Monocentric by function, nested by option.** Each function is still held by one body. That is a separation of powers at one scale, monocentric function by function, and not polycentricity in the sense of overlapping jurisdictions at several scales that adjust to one another, which is a different candidate in Section 12.1; "many hands" applies inside evaluation only. The compact can instead be nested: each function held at national, regional and planetary levels, above a common floor of admission, custody and disclosure that no level may lower, with mutual recognition of certificates between levels, and with the fast loop placed at the level where the capability is held. Nesting adds redundancy of correction and local knowledge. It also multiplies the bodies whose certificates must hold and the seams through which a party under assessment can play one level against another, every level is a further conversion target, and the common floor must then be entrenched at every level. Nested governance of a planetary commons is itself an open problem (Stern 2011). The requirements of Section 9 constrain functions, not the number of centres; the choice is open.

**The chamber.** It is a corrector, and Section 4 applies to it. Its members are drawn by a rule no participant controls, they rotate, its inbound row sum is certified like any evaluator's, and its removal conditions are in the table. The regress of guardians ends not at a trusted body but at node-by-node certificates that many parties can recompute from public data; what remains of the regress falls on standard-setting, which has its own row, and on disclosure, without which no certificate can be recomputed.

**Injection is the injector's violation.** Row sums count conversion into an evaluator from the party it assesses (Proposition 4.10(2); Proposition S10.2), so an assessed party could push an evaluator's row sum above one by sending it funds, staff or data, and so strip its findings of executive force; the same move repeated could manufacture the certificate failures that trigger dissolution. The compact closes both. Conversion into an evaluator from a party it assesses, outside the evaluator's disclosed channels, is a violation by that party; it does not suspend findings against that party; the assessment is reassigned to an evaluator of different lineage; and certificate failures attributable to a party under assessment count toward no end condition.

**Recusal, and the clock.** A participant under assessment does not vote on revisions of the purpose under which it is assessed. Assessments are opened by the evaluation network on stated evidence and lapse if not concluded within their deadline. The deadline stops while the assessed party withholds access, data or personnel that the assessment requires, the time withheld is recorded as evidence, and interim measures confined to the capability at issue remain available on the evidence already in hand, so delay by the assessed cannot make an assessment lapse.

**Held-out metrics.** A held-out metric published at registration is no longer held out: it becomes a target (Section 5.4). Metrics are committed in advance by a cryptographic hash and revealed only at evaluation, so that anyone can check that the metric was fixed beforehand and no one can train on it.

**Disclosure.** Certificates are only as good as the data on conversion into correctors, and omission is the cheapest capture: a channel left unreported counts for nothing in a row sum. The disclosure registry keeps mandatory, audited records of funding, staff, data and access flowing into every corrector, from every party, the drafting parties included, with a sanction on the omitting party above its gain from omission (Lemma 3.3(3)). Without it the certificates of the design could not be recomputed by anyone, so it is the layer on which every other certificate rests, and it gets what every corrector gets: separate funding, an audit of a different lineage, and its own certificate.

**The levy formula is itself a prize.** The levy's *base* is wider than the activity the compact constrains: total compute, or treaty shares set by economic size, with a surcharge on frontier compute above a threshold. A base confined to frontier compute would shrink under exactly the stopping (E2) and slowing (E3) the compact exists to be able to impose, and would make its correctors stakeholders in the activity they constrain; the wider base does not shrink, while the surcharge still prices the frontier. The *correction fund* is indexed to the coverage each hazard class requires, the $d^\ast$ of Proposition 4.5, not to the field's capability and not to perceived harm, so that correctors do not become stakeholders in capability growth and do not lapse after a recovery. Because $d^\ast$ is computed from measured conversion, payers gain by moving influence into channels that are not measured, and correctors who received the fund would gain by overstating it. It is therefore estimated, as a conservative upper bound, by a body that receives none of the correction fund, such as the rules body, whose financing lies outside the levy; checked against direct measurements that do not depend on the conversion data, the correlation of misses on common test sets and the catch rate of planted defects at fixed human hours; and allowed to fall by at most a registered rate per period, so that an apparent recovery lowers the fund only slowly. The formula is fixed for a registered period; revision needs the supermajority and the chamber, and revisers are separated from beneficiaries and carry row-sum certificates, because by Proposition 4.3 the formula is a conversion target.

**Frontier contributions by formula.** In a common frontier, cooperation is an equilibrium only while the pooled effort stays above a threshold that rises steeply with the value of winning (Proposition 10.1), and effort in the pool is a good shared by every member, which members choosing one by one under-supply (S10). Contributions to the common frontier are therefore paid by formula from the same base as the levy, not subscribed by each participant.

**Providers: voice without vote.** Providers earn revenue and contracts by usefulness and have no rights to appoint evaluators, set thresholds, exclude entrants or raise their cost of entry, or amend the rules. They have voice: notice and comment on every rule and standard, technical input to evaluation, and standing to contest a finding through the appeal, with their own conversion into every body disclosed. The experience of commons is that users excluded from revising their rules withhold local knowledge and compliance, and here the strongest of them also hold an exit option (Ostrom 1990); the result of Section 4 is that a party able to revise its own assessment captures it (Proposition 4.3), and in this field the regulated are the largest conversion targets the theory describes. The compact takes the second risk as the larger and draws the line at voice without vote. The tension is real, and it does not end at the line: a voice that is heard more when its holder is larger is itself a channel of conversion, which disclosure and the row sums have to count. The chamber, the appeal and the open channels of comment are where the tension is managed, not dissolved.

**End conditions.** If coverage falls below the participation threshold, or a stated number of the functions' certificates fail for a stated period for reasons not attributable to any party under assessment, the compact dissolves into its separately held functions, each continuing under its own rule. Protections against unilateral domination are entrenched. Every power that confers authority states what would end it (Remark 10.5).

**Against Ostrom's principles.** Boundaries (capability thresholds and hazard classes), monitoring and graduated sanctions are present. Proportionality of benefits and costs is partial: the largest payers have no vote, and strong types take part only for shares near their race payoffs (Proposition S10.3). Collective choice is answered by voice without vote. Conflict resolution is central, through one appeal, and neither cheap nor local, which nesting would answer. Recognition by an outside authority has no analogue at the scale of the planet; the host jurisdiction's first execution is the design's only link to the sovereignty of its participants. Nesting is the option above. The principles were drawn from commons that lasted at smaller scales (Ostrom 1990), and their transfer to a global commons is itself in question (Stern 2011).

**Other provisions.** A charter with three purposes: useful provision for all participants' populations; protection against O1 and O2; effective correction. Membership open to every state and provider above a capability threshold on non-discriminatory terms, participation made individually rational by access to shared services, a share of the dividend and capacity building (Proposition S10.3). Participation benefits, voting rights and protection against unilateral domination kept apart (Section 10.3). Baseline access to useful services with continuity guarantees; portable interfaces, escrow of systems providing essential services, and support for each participant to audit, operate and replace them. Narrow E1 first, for decisions whose delegation would lock with near certainty: human authorization and a minimum time for deliberation for decisions on the use of force and on nuclear command, drilled parallel capacity, an evidence channel independent of AI, and doctrine open to mutual inspection; broad E1 as the scope (Section 11.3). Admission constraints common to every system, before consequential deployment, internal deployment included, for divergences with common costs and a positive tax, with decision policies retained by performance only inside them; for masked classes, admission by capability threshold with control protocols that do not assume the system's cooperation (Remark 6.8). Commonly funded, openly published alignment and control research. Pace: no building of the decisive class while readiness is unmet; capability thresholds by evaluation, and input thresholds in effective compute that tighten with algorithmic progress. Graduated remedies: notice; restriction of a specific capability; suspension of specific deployments; never suspension of essential services; emergency powers narrow, time-limited, reviewable and stating what would end them. Bounded competition continues inside: providers compete on usefulness, cost and reliability, and jurisdictions keep plural local purposes.

### Hard cases

**An adverse finding against a weaker participant.** Pending appeal, only the capability at issue may be restricted; essential services continue through alternative providers; the appellant sees the evidence under confidentiality, the panel decides within the deadline, and compensation follows if the finding is overturned. The power to suspend could become containment; the safeguards are the narrow scope of interim measures, the burden of evidence on the compact, and the appellant's standing.

**An adverse finding against the leading provider.** Suspending it could interrupt services for everyone, so formally equal procedures would be unequal in effect. The share cap, escrowed systems and funded standby capacity mean that restricting the leader's hazardous capability need not stop services. The author's book gives the rule for this case: the greater the power, the stricter the regulation it must receive, because it can shape everyone's conditions (力愈大者，受调当愈严，以其能塑众人之境也) [corpus: shengsheng, 2026-07-14 第十四章]. The compact's version is that the leader's row in every certificate is weighted by its share, since the return to influence rises with the square of share (Proposition 4.3). Execution falls first to the leader's host jurisdiction. If that fails, what remains is the coercive restriction of inputs to the decisive class, the same form whose endorsement Section 8.3 withholds. The design does not settle it: it enters only as the candidate of Section 12.1, under the conditions stated there, with its escalation risk counted in $\Theta$, and the theory does not derive that its net effect is positive. Where neither consent nor such a restriction is available, the case is the hard case of Section 8.3. This is the hardest case, and the design does not dissolve it. It reduces the asymmetry of dependence that would otherwise make enforcement against the strongest impossible, and it gives the body that would execute a restriction a financing, an appointment and a certificate that do not run through the party restricted.

**A coalition of insiders.** Rival elites agree on mutual secrecy and immunity while weakening public correction. Four channels answer to people outside the coalition: the chamber, protected reporting, evaluators funded by formula, and publication of findings by default after a fixed delay. The disclosure registry makes the coalition's own conversion visible, and omission is the violation.

**An unjustified restriction.** The compact is used to contain a developing participant. It has standing to challenge, the burden is on the compact to show evidence of hazard, restrictions carry sunsets, and the compact has obligations to build capacity.

**Succession and withdrawal.** A change of leadership triggers review, not suspension. A withdrawal by a large participant makes the constraints borne by the rest unilateral and can trigger further exits; the participation threshold and the conditions of market access are what keep coverage above it (Section 9, "coverage can unravel"). Because the decisive class was not built and benefits and knowledge were diffused, a withdrawing party starts any race far from decisive capability and with a lower value of winning.

**Loss of an essential service.** Redundancy, escrow and standby capacity carry the service.

### Federations, at both orders

Competition among member units for capital and residents can become a race to the bottom, and the strongest members can capture the centre. At the first order, common minimum standards bind every member, and fiscal equalization gives members capacity they do not have to win, both widening the effective self of members that value their absolute payoff and supplying the $k_0$ of the loop gain; for members that act strategically, pooling raises the working self-model rather than only slowing the race (Proposition 10.2(1)). At the second order, equal representation of small members and supermajorities for constitutional change keep the centre from being bought by the largest, a precedent for voting that does not follow compute. A global minimum tax on corporate profits is a recent instance of one rule for all, and of how coverage unravels: the largest participant's groups were exempted from two of its rules in recognition of that participant's own minimum-tax rules (EVIDENCE EV18), the coverage problem of Section 9's participation threshold arising at the participant whose coverage matters most. The same federal form can select for winning or for contribution; what decides is what it rewards.

### Narrow E1 and the value of commitment

Deterrence theory holds that removing human discretion from retaliation makes a threat credible: a commitment that cannot be revoked is worth more than one that can (Schelling 1960). Its stronger form is that semi-automated retaliation could relieve the pressure to launch on warning. Five answers follow from the core.
1. The commitment value comes from removing the possibility of human overrule, which is the lock of Lemma 6.3 and, in its domain, a loss of O2.
2. Survivable, human-controlled forces relieve the pressure to launch on warning without removing judgment.
3. An automated commitment cannot tell a false alarm from an attack. Several false alarms of early-warning systems are documented. On 26 September 1983 a satellite early-warning system reported launches that were sunlight reflected from high clouds, and the duty officer judged the alarm false and did not report an attack (Podvig 2002; Hoffman 2009); the register records the event and does not claim that a report would have launched retaliation (EV18).
4. Automation adds a common risk, escalation at machine speed on a false alarm, that relative selection does not see (Lemma 3.3), so the contest under-prices it.
5. Among actors who price in the lock, keeping AI out of these loops is a strict equilibrium, and doing so verifiably is a costly signal (Proposition 11.2).

The strict equilibrium of Proposition 6.4(1) needs a certain lock, $q_L=1$, and it survives while the lock fails with a probability below the bound of Remark S6.1. The narrow domain meets that bound by construction: a decision irreversible within the time for deliberation cannot be overruled in time once it is delegated. In tactical domains, where the speed advantage is large and the lock uncertain, delegation can be an equilibrium (Proposition 6.4(2), (3)), and bounded, reversible defensive automation falls under the test of O2 of Section 6.6.

### Feasibility

Each actor can take part of E1 alone, and taken verifiably it is the signal of Proposition 11.2. E2 and E3 are the most verifiable among rivals, because chips, fabs, datacentres and compute can be observed. Algorithmic progress erodes thresholds stated in compute: with pre-training efficiency doubling about every eight months (90% interval about two to twenty-two months, on data to 2023), a nominal cap must tighten roughly two and a half to three times a year at the central estimate, and from about 1.5 to about 64 times a year across the interval (EV9), a rate condition verification must meet. Hardware-enabled mechanisms for verifying compute use are proposed and prototyped, not deployed (EV17). E1 is the hardest to verify, since it concerns software and internal procedure, and the most self-enforcing among actors who price in the lock, which is why it can begin as a norm: declared doctrine with inspected exercises.

Staying out of the contest alone while others race can be worse than joining. In the survival accounting of Proposition S8.1, take two actors, no hazard but the loss of the ability to overrule and the rival's decisive advantage, with $q_{\rm I}=p_{\rm dec}=1-\varepsilon$, and a decisive winner that extends its dominance, $p_{\rm ext}=1$. Joining gives $\Phi^P=\varepsilon\big(1-(1-\varepsilon)/2\big)=\varepsilon(1+\varepsilon)/2$. Staying out gives $\Phi^S=1-p^S_{\rm dec}$: that is $\varepsilon$, better than joining, if staying out leaves the rival's decisive chance where it was, and $\varepsilon^2$, worse than joining for every $\varepsilon<1$, if it raises that chance to $1-\varepsilon^2$ [thm, by substitution; num: TR]. That is why E1 must be held in common and begins with the decision loops in which the lock makes it an equilibrium.

|---|---|---|---|
| The Acheson–Lilienthal report (1946) | International ownership of the dangerous activity was proposed | Custody of the dangerous activity as the object of agreement | It was proposed, not adopted, while one holder had a monopoly; frontier capability in AI is held by several, with short leads (EV5) |
| The Baruch plan (1946) | It added that sanctions on violators would not be subject to veto; the other side opposed this and on-site inspection, and the leader would keep its monopoly until control was in place (Bundy 1988) | Custody must precede decisiveness, and enforcement against violators is part of the bargain | The sequence offered, custody after the leader's monopoly, was read by the other side as a bid to keep it; a leader that keeps its capability until control is in place offers the sequence the others refuse |

The Cold War ended largely by the internal collapse of one side, accompanied by negotiated reductions in intermediate forces, conventional forces and strategic arms, and its hazards fell with learning (Nye 1987), the case in which residual risk becomes summable. Rivals survived where nature or balancing supplied saturation, and gave up capabilities by agreement when reciprocal limits made the extra advantage worth less. Balancing by others is a saturation mechanism that does not depend on the technology, and whether it operates for AI is part of crux C5. Whether advantage in AI saturates is not known (Section 7.3), so Section 11.5 states the consequence conditionally: if it is unsaturated, as the racers' own premise holds, none of these precedents supplies the saturation, and safety has to come from changing what is selected before advantage becomes decisive.

### Crisis windows and standing

Arrangements of this kind are often adopted when a crisis opens a window, not through negotiation among the strong at a time of their choosing (Gunderson and Holling 2002; Olsson et al. 2006). A design prepared in advance, with its functions and protections worked out and its activation committed, is what a crisis can adopt; unprepared responses tend to strengthen the contest, through emergency powers without sunsets and measures aimed at rivals. A measure adopted in a crisis without a stated end condition runs the ratchet of Section 11.4 backward: held past its need, it becomes the lock-in the next reform must repay. Smaller jurisdictions, users and people who depend on services controlled by others need standing on every track, because they cannot leave the shared exposure; the chamber of X10 and the service guarantees are how they get it.
