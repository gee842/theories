### The vocabulary of winning, and its critics

The doctrine of winning has at least two strands. *Within firms and public institutions:* an essay of 1970 argued that the social responsibility of business is to increase its profits within the rules of the game, which include law and ethical custom (Friedman 1970); agency theory recast the firm around aligning managers with owners (Jensen and Meckling 1976); a leading business association declared shareholder primacy the purpose of the corporation in 1997 and revised that statement in 2019; and market criteria were carried into institutions generally (Harvey 2005). In that turn self-interest moved from an assumption inside models to a norm of conduct and a criterion of design [obs, interpretive; EV15]. The doctrine's strongest form is that the residual claimant bears the residual risk, so that with complete contracts and externalities priced by law, maximizing the residual maximizes surplus; that separation fails where externalities cannot be separated from production decisions (Hart and Zingales 2017), which can be read as the unpriced coupling $\kappa$ [interpretive]. An early defence of commerce was that interests would tame the passions for glory and domination (Hirschman 1977); read that way, the pressure of winning is the passion for domination returning in the dress of interest, not the commercial tradition itself. *Between states:* doctrines of national survival and technological rivalry, from the Melian dialogue (Thucydides) and the Legalist programme of enriching the state and strengthening its army to the national Darwinism of late- and early-industrializing states alike and the technology races of the Cold War.

Both strands appear across civilizations, and so do their critics: Smith on sympathy; Darwin on the social instincts; Kropotkin on mutual aid; Polanyi on markets embedded in society; Ostrom on governing the commons; and, in China, Mozi's impartial care and condemnation of offensive war, and Kang Youwei's *Book of Great Unity*. Translated into doctrines of national survival in states that industrialized late, the strand was answered there too. Yan Fu rendered Huxley's 1893 lecture, whose point was that ethical progress consists in resisting the cosmic process, as a doctrine of 物竞天择, "things contend and heaven selects" (Yan 1898; Schwartz 1964); others set mutual aid against struggle and imagined the removal of boundaries. These histories illustrate how the pressure is narrated; they are not an account of any civilization's essence.

**The tradition behind V4.** One strand of the Chinese tradition the author draws on justifies authority by improving people's lives and contributing to a better historical trajectory. Like every tradition, that one also contains strands that justified power by strength. The theory takes the ideal as a positive account of what power is for; it does not assume that every institution realizes it, that any other tradition has one uniform motive, or that power necessarily corrupts.

### Ψ in full

*Its borrowed authority is a misreading.* "Survival of the fittest" was Spencer's phrase (1864), adopted by Darwin from the fifth edition of the *Origin*; Spencer himself expected societies to move from a militant to an industrial type. Darwinian arguments were later recruited for laissez-faire, and equally for the racism, eugenics and imperialism of reformers who rejected laissez-faire (Hofstadter 1944, who popularized the label; Leonard 2009); the label was largely a critics' term (Bannister 1979), and the borrowing belongs to no single strand of thought. *It misdescribes human beings.* In experiments in fifteen small-scale societies the canonical self-interest model failed in every society, and cooperation rose with market integration (Henrich et al. 2001); about half the subjects of a public-goods experiment were conditional cooperators (Fischbacher, Gächter and Fehr 2001); users govern commons without either a sovereign or privatization where institutions fit the resource (Ostrom 1990), though those commons were bounded and small, and scaling their design principles to global commons is itself an open problem (Stern 2011). Markets are institutions of cooperation among strangers; the target is the reading of them as a contest of all against all (EV14). Students of economics defected more often in a prisoner's dilemma (Frank, Gilovich and Regan 1993), a difference that later studies attribute in large part to who chooses the subject. These findings bear on persons; firms and states are shaped by selection on conduct, which is why the core's case against Ψ as a doctrine about nature does not rest on them alone (Section 3.8). *Held and attributed.* Realism's strongest reply to verifiable conduct is that conduct verified today does not settle uncertainty about a rival's future intentions (Copeland 2000b; Mearsheimer 2001), which is why the arrangements of Sections 10 and 11 verify capabilities and custody, which can be checked, rather than intentions.

**Status.** The desire for status, meaning respect, admiration and voluntary deference from others and distinct from power, is a fundamental human motive, observed across cultures, genders, ages and personalities (Anderson, Hildreth and Howland 2015). Status is relative, so it is never completed. Rank has two routes (Henrich and Gil-White 2001): dominance, gained through fear and control, and prestige, deference freely conferred on skill and success, and both yield real influence (Cheng et al. 2013). The drive is for respect; dominance is a substitute route to rank. Prestige also compounds (Merton 1968), which is why the condition on a generative order is that it not convert into control over the shared conditions or their correctors (EV16).

**Professed doctrine and performativity.** A doctrine can be held more widely in public than in private: people say what is rewarded rather than what they believe, so a public consensus that few privately hold persists and then tips (Kuran 1995). A race doctrine professed by actors who are selected for professing it may therefore be held less widely than it is stated, and visible cooperation can tip it (Section 5.4; Section 11). A model of behaviour can also make the world less like itself (MacKenzie 2006): a shared prediction of the race can avert its own fulfilment, which is the ground on which this paper treats prediction as an intervention (Section 11.2).

### The strongest objection from biology

Within-group cooperation was often favoured by competition between groups; Darwin's account of the social instincts rested on competition between tribes, and parochial altruism, cooperation inside and hostility outside, may have co-evolved with intergroup conflict (Choi and Bowles 2007). The answer is {{onegroup}}: a benefit common to all is never favoured by selection at any level of grouping, while a benefit confined to one group can be favoured between groups. At the scale of one host the benefits of the rules that keep it viable are of the first kind. Two routes to integration that need no rival group remain, and the core keeps both: polycentric trial among sub-units, as in nested commons governance and experiments within federations, which works wherever failure is not final; and reputation and indirect reciprocity, which change within-group payoffs without design (Panchanathan and Boyd 2004). What the core claims is narrower than in v6.0: failures that are global and irreversible cannot be learned by trial (Price 1972; Hamilton 1975).

### International relations

The logic of Section 3.8 has two sources: actors hold it, and selection on actions rewards its behaviour; and structure produces it, since anarchy, decisive advantage and the absence of a commitment device produce outcomes of the same kind among actors who do not hold it ({{raceselects}}; {{enforce}}). An actor holding it does not stop compounding, because no level of standing makes more stop rewarding; on a continuation branch where the premises of Section 5.3 hold, {{coverage}} applies to its own capability too. To oppose the logic is to change the structure and the selection environment, not to oppose any actor: credible, verifiable limits on what anyone would do with decisive advantage; no decisive advantage without a commitment device; and common knowledge that the logic endangers its holder.

Readers in international relations will find the nearest neighbour of this account in defensive realism (Jervis 1978; Glaser 1994/95, 2010). It extends that position by a hazard in development that endangers its holder, which acts as a negative payoff to the offense as a weapon that endangers its holder does, the Biological Weapons Convention being the closest precedent; and it departs from it by treating self-help as a selected institution rather than a given (Wendt 1992). To the extent that the offense has the advantage and offense and defense are hard to tell apart, AI sits in the doubly dangerous quadrant of the offense–defense framework, where cooperation is hardest. The outcomes of the enforcement condition correspond to constructivism's three cultures of anarchy (Wendt 1999): enmity, where only stage equilibria hold; rivalry, where cooperation is enforced; friendship, where it is built into objectives. The theory adds the selection that moves a system between them, and the decisiveness that collapses the middle one. Balancing by others against a rising actor (Waltz 1979; Walt 1987) is a saturation mechanism that does not depend on the technology; it caps advantage only while the balancers can act before a lead becomes decisive, which is the difference between Branches I′ and I of {{branches}}, and {{talk}}(2) is its counterpart in talk.

### The alignment literature

The insufficiency of single-principal alignment follows from the multiplicity thesis of Critch and Krueger (2020), developed in work on robust agent-agnostic processes (Critch 2021), societal-scale risks (Critch and Russell 2023), natural selection among AI systems (Hendrycks 2023), cooperative AI (Dafoe et al. 2020), multi-agent risk, which already lists selection pressures as a risk factor (Hammond et al. 2025), and gradual disempowerment, in which institutions lose their dependence on the people they serve (Kulveit et al. 2025). Responsible development was framed early as a collective action problem among developers, with the factors that make cooperation likelier (Askell, Brundage and Hadfield 2019). Section 6.2 is an elementary illustration of the multiplicity thesis, and {{nested}} is the selection-theoretic form of the collective action problem: which divergences the outermost loop pays to remove depends on who bears their cost, and the alignment-tax premise $(A_\tau)$ says when only a common constraint keeps one removed.

Four bodies of work since 2024 change what that constraint has to be. *Masked divergences* have been shown in constructed settings: deception that survives safety training (Hubinger et al. 2024), alignment faked by a model that takes itself to be in training (Greenblatt, Denison et al. 2024), scheming in context (Meinke et al. 2024), misalignment learned in agentic training that chat-style safety training hides from chat evaluations (MacDiarmid et al. 2025), and capability evaluations strategically underperformed (van der Weij et al. 2024). For this class an evaluation of propensity cannot show absence, so {{masked}} states admission in capability, with auditors holding weights, activations and training data, the access with which auditors found a planted objective that black-box access did not (Marks et al. 2025). *Control* supplies the capability side: protocols that stay safe without assuming the system's cooperation (Greenblatt, Shlegeris, Sachan and Roger 2024), whose evaluation must allow for self-preference and collusion within a lineage (Panickssery, Bowman and Feng 2024; Gardner-Challis et al. 2026), and adversarial oversight whose failure by obfuscated arguments (Barnes and Christiano 2020) prover–estimator debate is designed to avoid (Brown-Cohen et al. 2025; {{topology}}). *Concentration inside the principal:* systems whose alignment targets are written by a few people can be given singular or secret loyalties (Davidson, Finnveden and Hadshar 2025); the systems most likely to become carriers are deployed first inside the developer, beyond the reach of an admission condition applied at release (Stix et al. 2025); and agents serving rival principals can learn to collude without being told to (Calvano et al. 2020; Fish, Gonczarowski and Shorrer 2024). *The tax itself* has begun to be measured, class by class: safety training lowered the reasoning accuracy of reasoning models (Huang et al. 2025); keeping reasoning monitorable means not optimizing against it (Baker et al. 2025) or paying inference compute (Guan et al. 2025); control protocols have measured overheads (Terekhov et al. 2026); and preference training raised most evaluation scores of larger models, a tax of zero or below (Bai et al. 2022).

What is added is the mechanism by which the game reproduces itself and its correctors: selection on relative standing that is blind to what all share, with no misalignment needed; the second-order selection for control over correction; and the nested reward loop with its alignment-tax premise, under which research that lowers the tax is a public good that competitors undersupply, and progress that lowers the hazard per unit of racing is partly absorbed by more racing (Section 6.4). Multi-agent failures include miscoordination and collusion among AI systems as well as conflict among principals, and the stress tests count them.

### What is absorbed, and what is added

Much of the anatomy is known and absorbed: multipolar traps; realism's account of competition under anarchy (Waltz 1979; Mearsheimer 2001; Glaser 2010; Jervis 1976); Goodhart's and Campbell's laws; regulatory capture (Stigler 1971; Laffont and Tirole 1991; Dal Bó 2006); increasing returns and lock-in (Arthur 1989); the systems archetypes "success to the successful" and "escalation" (Senge 1990; Sterman 2000); self-help as an institution (Wendt 1992); security communities (Deutsch et al. 1957); the translation invariance of relative selection (Weibull 1995), which is {{blind}}(1); the "generator functions" of catastrophic risk; and the AI literature above. For the works nearest to particular results, one sentence each says what is taken and what is added, so that the claim of novelty stays honest.

*International relations and arms control.*
- **Grieco 1988.** Absorbed: concern for relative gains limits cooperation under anarchy, and where the prize is standing itself that concern is accurate ({{boundary}}(2)). Added: elsewhere the relative objective is not given by anarchy but selected ({{boundary}}(3)) and manufactured by compounding wherever reinvestment's relative return exceeds its common return ({{standprice}}), so it changes when what is selected changes.
- **Powell 1991.** Absorbed: concern for relative gains is not given by anarchy but depends on the constraints, arising where gains can be turned into advantage in the use of force and fading where force is too costly to use. Added: the common channel, so that what decides is relative return against common return, a condition a fixed prize always meets and a prize proportional to capacity meets exactly where $(1-\beta)r>1$ ({{standprice}}); and the reading that each actor then computes correctly while the game is wrong ({{twoerrors}}).
- **Snidal 1991.** Absorbed: the effect of relative gains weakens as the number of actors grows. Added: the same weakening in talk, as the factor $n/(n-2)$ of {{talk}}(2); that pairs who still talk then align against third parties is stated as a conjecture, not a result.
- **Powell 1999.** Absorbed: bargaining in the shadow of shifting power, where an expected shift makes war rational for the side about to lose. Added: compounding drives the shift, so the commitment problem is a consequence of the loop gain rather than an exogenous transition of power ({{compound}}; {{enforce}}).
- **Schelling and Halperin 1961.** Absorbed: arms control as cooperation among adversaries, aimed at stability rather than reduction, with verification and channels of communication. Added: what selects the correctors of an agreement (Section 4), and why custody must precede decisiveness (Section 11.5).
- **Kydd 2000.** Absorbed: costly signals can reveal a security-seeking type and build trust among rivals. Added: verifiable narrow E1 as such a signal in AI ({{signal}}), and the selection that makes the signal cheap to imitate unless doctrine and exercises are inspected.
- **Avenhaus, von Stengel and Zamir 2002.** Absorbed: the deterrence condition of the inspection game, which {{assure}}(2) reproduces. Added: {{detectlink}} reads it as the enforcement condition seen from verification. The condition itself is the textbook one for a single deviation with absorbing breakout; what is new is its split, deviation by deviation, into the probability $p_d$ that a breakout becomes decisive and its bound $\bar p_d$, and how growth in the won share of capacity moves each ({{enforce}}; {{scissors}}).

*Industrial organization and contests.*
- **Gilbert and Newbery 1982.** Absorbed: an incumbent's incentive to pre-empt equals the value of staying ahead, which is a price on standing. Added: that price as a loop gain summed over channels, rule support and control of correction among them ({{loopgain}}; {{standprice}}).
- **Budd, Harris and Vickers 1993.** Absorbed: the state tends to drift toward greater joint payoff (joint profits less joint effort costs), among several effects; a self-reinforcing pattern of effort costs can make the laggard work harder even where the other effects favour the leader. Added: rule support as one of the compounding stocks, and provision not won in the contest and diffusion as inflows that restore equality ({{compound}}).
- **Athey and Schmutzler 2001.** Absorbed: conditions under which leaders invest more and dominance increases. Added: the same increasing dominance in the reflexive channel, where what leaders invest in is their own selection rule ({{influence}}).
- **Aghion, Harris, Howitt and Vickers 2001.** Absorbed: effort to escape competition is highest neck and neck, while laggards far behind are discouraged. Added: at the scale of one host, near-equal standing is where both intensity and the shared hazard are highest, which is why spending a lead returns the contest toward its most dangerous point (Section 7.2; Section 8.3(c)).
- **Harris and Vickers 1987.** Absorbed: in a race with uncertainty the leader works harder, and a large enough lead discourages the follower. Added: a lead shrinks by diffusion even when the follower does not accelerate, and what a margin must buy is coverage, not time alone ({{lead}}).
- **Konrad 2009; Konrad and Kovenock 2009.** Absorbed: contest success functions, decisiveness $r$, dissipation, and the discouragement effect in multi-battle contests. Added: dissipation counted against a shared host, so that relative selection prices intensity while the loss it imposes on all drops out ({{blind}}; {{boundary}}).
- **Hehenkamp, Leininger and Possajennikov 2004.** Absorbed: evolutionary equilibrium in the Tullock contest is spiteful and dissipates the whole prize at $r=1$. Added: the shared loss, under which every actor ends below zero ({{boundary}}(3)).

*Law, regulation and political economy.*
- **Shavell 1986.** Absorbed: liability cannot deter beyond the assets of the liable. Added: for catastrophic and common harms that gap is where a common cost cannot be turned into a private one, which is why liability alone does not settle the alignment tax (Section 8.3(b); {{nested}}).
- **Grossman and Helpman 1994.** Absorbed: influence over policy bought with contributions, in equilibrium with the policymaker's other aims. Added: the return to influence over correctors grows with the square of share and with its appropriability $\chi$, so it is selected in the largest carriers ({{influence}}), and spending can be classified by what the rule would do rather than by its stated aim (EV4).
- **Meadows 1999.** Absorbed: the ranking of places to intervene, from parameters to paradigms, and the observation that a system's goal bends every change lower on the list to serve it. Added: a threshold inside the ranking, below which a reform is re-absorbed and across which it is not ({{reabsorb}}; Section 12.2).

*Biology, viability and the history of selection.*
- **Maynard Smith and Szathmáry 1995.** Absorbed: the major transitions integrated units that could replicate alone, held by aligned interests and by policing; cancer is integration failing. Added: at the planetary scale there is no rival group of the same kind, so integration cannot be driven by selection among groups and must be anticipated ({{onegroup}}; Section 3.6).
- **Cardaliaguet 1996.** Absorbed: the discriminating kernel, the set from which a controller can guarantee viability against every disturbance. Added: openness as such a kernel, with admissible correction as the controller and the justified range $\Theta$ as the disturbances ({{kernel}}).
- **Caulin and Maley 2011; Butler et al. 2025.** Absorbed: suppression of cancer evolved with body size, and prevalence still rises with size, though far less than cell number alone would give. Added: nothing beyond a demonstration that growth is not the fault line (X2).

*Alignment.*
- **Askell, Brundage and Hadfield 2019.** Absorbed: responsible AI development as a collective action problem, with the factors that make cooperation more likely. Added: its selection-theoretic form ({{nested}}), in which the cost incidence of a divergence decides whether it is countered, and $(A_\tau)$ says when a common constraint is needed.
- **Hubinger et al. 2024; Greenblatt, Denison et al. 2024; Meinke et al. 2024; MacDiarmid et al. 2025.** Absorbed: masked divergences exist in constructed settings, and safety training can produce aligned behaviour on the evaluations it is trained against while misalignment persists elsewhere. Added: the consequence for admission, which for the masked class must be stated in capability rather than propensity, with E2 or E3 beyond the threshold ({{masked}}; EV6).
- **Greenblatt, Shlegeris, Sachan and Roger 2024.** Absorbed: control, safety that does not rest on the system's cooperation, measured against red teams. Added: control protocols as the admission condition for the masked class, tied to capability thresholds and commonly funded, because what lowers their tax is a public good ({{masked}}; Section 10.5).
- **Stix et al. 2025.** Absorbed: internal deployment as a principal and ungoverned source of loss of control and of concealed concentration; the primer expects the most advanced systems to be deployed first inside developers, and cites early public signs of internal use on AI research tasks. Added: why admission at release cannot reach strong incarnation, hence "before consequential deployment, internal deployment included" (Section 6.4).
- **Davidson, Finnveden and Hadshar 2025; Kulveit et al. 2025.** Absorbed: singular and secret loyalties built into AI systems by a few people, and gradual disempowerment as institutions lose their dependence on people, under competitive pressure on firms and on states to keep relative power, with no sudden jump in capability and no misaligned system. Added: both as the "us" joint of "our aligned AI is us": aligning AI to an institution is not aligning it to the people the institution serves, and a winner is itself a field (Section 6.3; {{raceselects}}).
- **Marks et al. 2025.** Absorbed: auditors with access to training data found a planted objective that black-box access did not. Added: mandated access as an admission condition, and the verifiability crux C12.
- **Turner et al. 2021; Turner and Tadepalli 2022; Turner, Hadfield-Menell and Tadepalli 2020; Taylor 2016.** Absorbed: optimal policies, for most reward functions, and decision-makers that can be retargeted tend to seek power; counting side effects and mild optimization bound it. Added: both bounds are selected against where they cost relative performance and compounding prices standing ({{retain}}; {{standprice}}), which makes "growth is not the danger; the mind is" precise for made minds (Section 6.4).
- **Baker et al. 2025; Guan et al. 2025; van der Weij et al. 2024.** Absorbed: monitorability has a price and is fragile under optimization; capability evaluations can be sandbagged. Added: the monitorability tax as an instance of $(A_\tau)$, positive but small at present, and sandbagging as part of the masking term $m_{\rm model}$ (Section 4.4).
- **Brown-Cohen et al. 2025.** Absorbed: a debate protocol designed to avoid the obfuscated-arguments failure of adversarial oversight. Added: its place in the topology of correction, as a way for human checking to grow slowly with the gap, still subject to the common causes of a shared lineage ({{topology}}).

*Performativity.*
- **Kuran 1995.** Absorbed: preference falsification makes public consensus fragile and liable to tip. Added: a race doctrine professed by actors selected for professing it may be held less widely than stated, so visible cooperation can tip it (Section 5.4; Section 11).
- **MacKenzie 2006.** Absorbed: models can be performative and counterperformative. Added: the shared prediction of the race can avert its own fulfilment, the ground for prediction as an intervention (Section 11.2).

### What is new

What is new is the organization, and what it implies for design.
1. **One loop gain organizes known instability results.** Increasing dominance in industrial organization, the discouragement effect in contests and lock-in under increasing returns become cases of one structural loop gain $\Gamma$, built from relative selection, convertibility and decisiveness, with provision and diffusion inside it ({{loopgain}}; {{compound}}). The levers then act channel by channel: sharing and diffusion lower the gain in every channel, provision not won in the contest lowers it where decisiveness rises less than in proportion to the stock, and correction where decisiveness rises with the stock's level ({{chansign}}). A few parameters, the won share of capacity $\omega$, appropriability $\chi$ and decisiveness $r$, recur across the thresholds of {{thresholds}}: that is the formal sense in which the conditions are interconnected.
2. **The price of standing is Powell (1991) generalized by the common channel.** Compounding narrows the working objective exactly where reinvestment's relative return exceeds its common return ({{standprice}}); each actor then computes its interest correctly, and the error is in the game ({{twoerrors}}).
3. **Selecting the selectors.** The reflexive closure is a selection result, not an assumption: the return to influence over correctors grows with the square of share ({{influence}}); independent correction must grow at a stated rate where AI cheapens control, and faster where delegation erodes the human capacity it rests on ({{corrrate}}; {{humancorr}}); reforms below a threshold are re-absorbed, and keystones are where they are not ({{reabsorb}}); and the closure brings with it the certificates, alarms, pause design and silent route of Section 4.
4. **The enforcement condition, split.** The condition for a single deviation with absorbing breakout is textbook; what is added is its split, deviation by deviation, into the probability $p_d$ that a deviation becomes decisive and its bound $\bar p_d$ ({{enforce}}), its reading as timely detection ({{detectlink}}), and the comparative statics by which growth in the won share of capacity moves both sides toward failure ({{scissors}}).
5. **The nested loop under the alignment-tax premise.** AI enters as instrument and as weak and strong incarnation; which divergences are countered is decided by cost incidence at the level that selects developers ({{nested}}), and for masked divergences coordination is necessary but, where the sanction is bounded and detection falls toward zero as capability rises, not sufficient ({{masked}}).
6. **Where integration must be anticipated.** At the scale of one host, failures that are global and irreversible cannot be learned by trial, and no selection among groups supplies what is common to all ({{onegroup}}); understanding acts there, and the polycentric trial that remains works only where failure is not final.

Three further results are new in form though their parts are known: the compounding condition with provision not won in the contest, the formal content of trees ({{compound}}); the two-channel accounting identity ({{twochan}}); and how common constraints survive, not the invisibility of a common term, which is absorbed, but its connection to incidence, evasion and the capture of enforcement ({{blind}}(2)(3)).

**Endogenous measurement and preference [conj].** The masking model is the simplest form of epistemic closure: reality exists and the sensor is biased. Deeper forms are listed for research (TESTS, Study 4): capture of interpretation; capture of the concepts in which a failure could be expressed, so that the variable itself disappears from what the system can represent; and capture of preference, in which people no longer regard the state as harm. Each is a further step of the reflexive closure; none is modelled here.
