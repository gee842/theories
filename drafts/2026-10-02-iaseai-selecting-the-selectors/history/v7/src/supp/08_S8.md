## S8. Comparing paths

### Proof of Lemma {{#fallback}}

Couple the two paths by a common uniform draw $\mathsf u_t$ each period: the event occurs on a path at $t$ when $\mathsf u_t$ falls below that path's conditional hazard given the shared world history. On every history on which both have survived to $t$, the two conditions give $A$'s hazard at most $C$'s, so $\{A\text{ fails at }t\}\subseteq\{C\text{ fails at }t\}$. By induction the event "$C$ survives to $H$" is contained in "$A$ survives to $H$", path by path, whatever the rule by which $A$ dissolves, so $\Phi^A(H)\ge\Phi^C(H)$. $\square$

*A step that fails.* Comparing after conditioning on the dissolution date, $\Phi^A(H\mid\tau)\ge\Phi^C(H)$ for every $\tau$, is not valid when dissolution depends on the state. In a two-state Markov world in which bad states make dissolution more likely, the conditional comparison fails at $H=30$ for dissolution at $t=1$ (0.291 against 0.324), while the coupled comparison holds at horizons 10, 30 and 100 [num: SM(4)]; an exact dynamic programme over the world state, the arrangement's status and both survivals, with a common uniform, gives $\Phi^A=0.884$, 0.551 and 0.077 against $\Phi^C=0.813$, 0.461 and 0.062 at those horizons [num: SM(14)]. If $C$'s hazard is a deterministic schedule, the two conditions need only be stated on $A$'s histories. *Example.* Let continuation's hazard be $h_c(t)=0.05(0.2+0.01t)+0.001$, the arrangement's own hazard 0.0015 a period, dissolution 2% a period, and the fallback a restart of continuation ten periods back, with hazard $h_c(\max(t-10,0))$ after dissolution. The arrangement survives with probability 0.976, 0.864 and 0.211 at horizons 10, 30 and 100, against 0.875, 0.575 and 0.026 for continuation [num: FD].

### The discounting comparison

With utility linear in consumption, discount rate $\rho$, a constant race hazard $h$ and growth rates $g_r$ racing and $g_p$ paced (pacing taken as hazard-free), the race is worth $c_0/(\rho+h-g_r)$ and pacing $c_0/(\rho-g_p)$, each finite where its denominator is positive, so the race is preferred iff $h<g_r-g_p$, though it ends in catastrophe with certainty; a rising hazard reverses the preference only if it comes to exceed the growth differential [thm]. This is our derivation. It is the special case of Jones's (2024) trade-off, which runs a growth-raising technology until $v(c^\ast)=g/h$, with $h$ the hazard (his $\delta$) and $v(c)=u(c)/(u'(c)c)$ the value of a year of life relative to consumption, in which the value of a year of life does not rise with consumption. With curvature $\gamma\ge1$ it does, and Jones's planner stops: at a 1% annual hazard and 10% growth, with logarithmic utility after about 40 years, at incomes about 55 times higher and about a one-in-three cumulative risk; with $\gamma=2$, after about 4.5 years at about 4%; at a 2% hazard, with logarithmic utility, at once. Jones also finds that mortality improvements from AI raise the acceptable risk, to about 25% for $\gamma=2$ or 3 in his richer model with a one-time risk, which belongs to crux C10, and that without them a lower social discount rate lowers the acceptable risk toward zero [obs: EV11]. The protection standard does not discount the protected losses at all, by choice (N1).

### Survival at a common horizon

**{{!Proposition:survassure}} (Assurance in survival terms at a common horizon) [thm].** Comparing by survival classifies the game between restraint and racing by two signs, and the classification can change with the horizon. *Survival is computed with per-period conditional probabilities over a common horizon $H$. Let $h_R$ be the hazard under restraint, $q_{\rm I}$ the loss of the ability to overrule by delegation, $q_{\rm II}$ the shared hazard (with superscripts for the branch on which it is evaluated), $q_W$ the hazard of preventive attack, $p_{\rm dec}$ and $p^S_{\rm dec}$ the probability that a racer's advantage becomes decisive when all race and when the actor stays out, and $p_{\rm ext}$ the actor's belief that a decisive winner would extend its dominance over it. With independence, $\Phi^R=1-h_R$, $\Phi^T=(1-q_{\rm I})(1-q^T_{\rm II})(1-q_W)$, $\Phi^P=(1-q_{\rm I})(1-q^P_{\rm II})(1-q_W)\big(1-p_{\rm dec}\frac{n-1}n\big)$ and $\Phi^S=(1-q^S_{\rm II})(1-p^S_{\rm dec}p_{\rm ext})$.*
1. *The advantage of restraint is $\varpi(\Phi^R-\Phi^T)+(1-\varpi)(\Phi^S-\Phi^P)$: dominance if both differences are non-negative; a stag hunt with threshold $(\Phi^P-\Phi^S)/\big((\Phi^R-\Phi^T)+(\Phi^P-\Phi^S)\big)$ if only the first is positive; chicken if only the second is; a prisoner's dilemma if neither.*
2. *Where $\Phi^S\ge\Phi^P$ and $\Phi^R\ge\Phi^T$, an actor that holds the prediction restrains whatever it believes about the others.*
3. *Mutual racing is a strict equilibrium at every horizon $H$ with $\Phi^P_H>\Phi^S_H$.*
4. *For constant per-period hazards $q_B$ on a branch and $q_R$ under the arrangement, $\Phi^B(H)/\Phi^R(H)=\big((1-q_B)/(1-q_R)\big)^H$.*
5. *If an actor gives the premises of Section 5.3 probability $p_{\rm prem}$, and the branches of the race have survivals $T_\neg$, $P_\neg$, $S_\neg$ when those premises fail, the classification at horizon $H$ uses the mixtures, and when the arrangement's own survival also decays the signs of (1) can fail at long horizons.*

*Proof:* the advantage is linear in $\varpi$; (2)–(5) by substitution. $\square$

*Numbers* ($n=2$, $p_{\rm ext}=1$) [num: legacy v4.4, SV, recomputed from the closed forms for V6.2; (5) legacy v5, SR].
- With $q_{\rm I}=0.6$, $q^T_{\rm II}=q^S_{\rm II}=0.3$, $q^P_{\rm II}=0.4$, $q_W=0.2$, $p_{\rm dec}=p^S_{\rm dec}=0.6$ and $h_R=0.1$: $\Phi^R=0.90$, $\Phi^T=0.22$, $\Phi^P=0.13$ and $\Phi^S=0.28$, and restraint is dominant.
- With low delegation ($q_{\rm I}=0.05$, every shared hazard 0.1, $q_W=0.1$, $p_{\rm dec}=p^S_{\rm dec}=0.3$, $h_R=0.1$), the game is a stag hunt with threshold 0.16.
- For a leader that wins with probability 0.8 if it races, so that when both race the rival's decisive win has probability $0.2\,p_{\rm dec}$, and whose exit hands decisive advantage to the rival ($q_{\rm I}=0.2$, $q_W=0.2$, $p_{\rm dec}=0.9$, $p^S_{\rm dec}=0.95$, $h_R=0.1$): a stag hunt with threshold 0.42 when every shared hazard is 0.3, and 0.11 when it is 0.8. For a leader whose exit would hand a rival a decisive win, the shared hazard is what carries the case for restraint.
- With $p_{\rm prem}=0.99$, the arrangement's survival $e^{-0.005H}$, race branches decaying at rate 0.05 when the premises hold and $(T_\neg,P_\neg,S_\neg)=(0.9,0.4,0.1)$ when they fail, the arrangement's survival exceeds the mixed survival of racing against restrainers at $H=10$, 100 and 600, by 0.34, 0.59 and 0.04; at $H=1{,}000$ it does not, and racing becomes dominant.

### The alternatives: results stated briefly in the core

**{{!Remark:lead}} (What a lead buys).** (1) With diffusion at rate $\delta_f$, a lead $\Delta_0$ held by a leader that pauses obeys $\dot\Delta=-\delta_f\Delta-1$ and is exhausted after $\delta_f^{-1}\ln(1+\delta_f\Delta_0)<\Delta_0$: a six-month lead diffusing at 0.1 a month lasts 4.7 months, a one-year lead 7.9 months, a two-year lead 12.2 months [num: legacy v4.5, LD]. (2) The margin is time, but what it must buy is coverage, which rises by about $\dot U\Delta$ while uncovered variety grows; for hazard classes with an evidence lag above $\Delta$ it buys nothing ({{tempo}}). (3) Whether it suffices is judged by the leader's own evaluators, under time pressure. (4) In a static contest the follower raises effort when the leader slows (Dixit 1987); in dynamic races a large lead discourages the follower (Harris and Vickers 1987; Konrad and Kovenock 2009), and a leader seeing its window close has a motive to strike first (Van Evera 1999). A developer able to commit first to a slightly slower speed gains, because the others then slow too, a local and Pareto-improving result that needs no lead (Bueno de Mesquita, Dziuda and Polborn 2026). On their own median estimates, the planners of one detailed scenario judge that spending part of a lead buys about a third (0.32) of the reduction in takeover risk that a coordinated slowdown buys [obs: EV10]. *Proof of (1):* $\dot\Delta=-\delta_f\Delta-1$ with $\Delta(0)=\Delta_0$ reaches zero at $\delta_f^{-1}\ln(1+\delta_f\Delta_0)$, which is below $\Delta_0$ since $\ln(1+x)<x$. $\square$

**{{!Remark:sprint}} (The finite sprint, by mechanism).** A finite sprint trades four quantities: exposure during and after it, zero after a freeze if the hazardous ratio built is at most one ({{freeze}}); the erosion of the frozen edge as rivals developing within understanding catch up; the rivals' responses, since they cannot verify which kind of winner they face; and the maintenance of the advantage, which requires stopping others' growth and so ends their O2. It can preserve the sprinter's survival while giving up permanent superiority, which survival does not need; excluding it as a deviation from a cooperative arrangement needs its net deviation payoff to be negative ({{participate}}). Two properties of the freeze follow from Part I, whatever the sprinter intends. (i) It is a constraint borne by every actor but the one that imposes it, the most unequal incidence a stop can have; by {{blind}}(2) and (3), those it binds are re-sorted by it and evade it wherever evasion pays their type, so it holds only by a sanction kept above every type's gain from evasion, supplied by the sprinter, and under relative accounting the others' best reply is prevention or imitation, not compliance. (ii) Its holder is the largest carrier there is: the return to influence over correctors scales with the square of share ({{influence}}) and cultivated conversion with a high moment of shares ({{corrrate}}(4)), so the sprinter is the largest target for conversion of success into control over whatever is to check the freeze, and no corrector outside it certifies its evaluators. E2 and common custody impose the same stop on the decisive class; they differ in (i), by equal incidence that includes the custodians' own capability, and in (ii), by functions held separately, with correctors to be certified independent of every party. X8 sets the two side by side on D4 and D5.

**Proposition {{#preventown}} in full.** *Facing a rival it believes certain to race, comparing by its own survival: (1) a single strike that escalates to catastrophe with probability $\varepsilon_P$, and otherwise succeeds with probability $s_P$ in returning the field to something like the arrangement and leaves the race as it was if it fails, gives $(1-\varepsilon_P)\big[s_P\Phi^R+(1-s_P)\Phi^P\big]$, and beats both staying out and joining iff $\varepsilon_P<1-\max(\Phi^S,\Phi^P)/\big(s_P\Phi^R+(1-s_P)\Phi^P\big)$; (2) sustained prevention against a target that rebuilds with probability at least $b_P$ a period, each strike escalating with probability at least $\varepsilon_{\min}$, has per-period hazard at least $b_P\varepsilon_{\min}$; (3) at common horizons, sustained prevention with hazard 0.01 beats racing with hazard 0.05: 0.90 against 0.60 at $H=10$, 0.60 against 0.08 at $H=50$, 0.13 against 0.00 at $H=200$.* *Proof:* (1) compare and solve; as $\Phi^S,\Phi^P\to0$ with $s_P\Phi^R>0$ the bound tends to one. When $\Phi^S$ and $\Phi^P$ fall in proportion, by a common factor $\lambda$, $\max(\Phi^S,\Phi^P)/(s_P\Phi^R+(1-s_P)\Phi^P)$ is increasing in $\lambda$, so the bound rises; it need not rise when one branch falls alone: with $\Phi^S>\Phi^P$, lowering $\Phi^P$ lowers it ($\Phi^S=0.5$, $\Phi^R=0.9$, $s_P=0.5$: 0.231 at $\Phi^P=0.4$, 0.167 at 0.3) [num: VF]. (2) {{nonsum}}. (3) $0.99^H>0.95^H$. $\square$ With $\Phi^R=1$, $s_P=0.7$, $\varepsilon_P=0.2$ and the race branches near zero it gives 0.56 [num: legacy v5, FS]. Sustained prevention is compared with racing by its hazard, not by the event: both end in catastrophe over an unbounded horizon, and at a common finite horizon the lower hazard survives better. Shifts that cannot be observed raise the chance of mistaken prevention (Debs and Monteiro 2014); verification that supports deterrence can also supply targets (Coe and Vaynman 2020). An actor that manipulates shared risk as an instrument (Schelling 1966) plays chicken in survival terms, with mixed equilibria and wars of attrition. When the probability that a capable rival holds an unbounded relative objective is low, a survival-first actor's best course is to propose verifiable limits and take narrow E1 verifiably in its own loops, the costly signal of {{signal}}.

**{{!Remark:insure}} (Why insuring against falling behind is not an exit).** Bounding the penalty for falling behind with autonomous, good-enough provision and defence does not leave the contest, for three reasons. The penalty is set by the winner, not the laggard, so the bound is only as reliable as the belief that a winner would not extend its dominance. A winner with a relative objective has a motive to destroy what the laggard secured, since its standing rises by the laggard's loss ({{eq:twochaneq}}). And insurance accepts the frame of the contest: it hedges against losing instead of declining the game, which is the reply that the ruling of Section 3.8 rejects, since against a winner of that logic falling behind cannot be made survivable. What would leave the contest is different in kind. Defence that makes decisive advantage unattainable for everyone lowers $r$ for all and belongs to E2, provided it stays under human oversight. Provision held by no one and supplied beneath the game lowers the won share for every actor (G5), and belongs to a world that has left the contest; the same provision held by one actor against its rivals is a position in it.

### Proofs for the verified agreement

**Proposition {{#assure}} in full.** *Payoffs $R,T,P,S$; a defection detected in time is reversed, the defector receiving $P-F$ and the cooperator $P$; undetected defection yields $T$ and $S$. With $T_\upsilon=\upsilon(P-F)+(1-\upsilon)T$, $g_C=R-T_\upsilon$ and $g_D=(1-\upsilon)(P-S)$: (1) the advantage of cooperating is $\varpi g_C-(1-\varpi)g_D$, with the three cases of the core and, if both are negative, chicken; (2) where $T-P+F>0$, $g_C>0$ iff $\upsilon>(T-R)/(T-P+F)$, and where $T<R$, $g_C>0$ at $\upsilon=0$; (3) cooperation is risk-dominant iff $g_C>g_D$, a property of payoffs selected under global games and adaptive play (Carlsson and van Damme 1993; Kandori, Mailath and Rob 1993; Young 1993); (4) with $n$ players and independent belief $\varpi_1$ about each rival, $\varpi_1^{n-1}\ge\varpi^\ast=g_D/(g_C+g_D)$ suffices only if a cooperator gets at least $S$ when some but not all others defect and the advantage of defecting in any such state is at most $g_D$.* *Proof:* (1) cooperating yields $\varpi R+(1-\varpi)\big(\upsilon P+(1-\upsilon)S\big)$, defecting $\varpi T_\upsilon+(1-\varpi)P$; their difference is $\varpi g_C-(1-\varpi)g_D$. (2) $g_C=(R-T)+\upsilon(T-P+F)$; rearrange. (4) Bound the intermediate states. Without the second bound the condition fails: with advantages $(1,-10,-1)$ by number of defecting others in a three-player game, each rival cooperating with probability $\sqrt{0.5}$ gives $-3.73$ although all others cooperate with probability 0.5 [num: legacy v5, N13]. $\square$ The threshold in (2) is the deterrence condition of the inspection game (Avenhaus, von Stengel and Zamir 2002); what this paper adds is its identity with the enforcement condition and the comparative statics of both under growth. Read with its payoffs as continuation values, cooperation $R=C_i$, a deviation that becomes decisive $T=(1-\delta)(C_i+g_i)+\delta D_i$ and one detected and answered $P-F=(1-\delta)(C_i+g_i)+\delta v_i$, it equals $1-\bar p_{d,i}$ of the enforcement condition, as S5 shows for {{detectlink}} [identity: JX]; the two differ in what they take as given, the sanction $F$ and trust here, a credible continuation computed with breakouts possible there.

**{{!Proposition:overhang}} (Commitment under a growing overhang) [thm].** An agreement that holds growing capability in check needs a sanction that keeps pace with the net gain from exit, and one that preserves relative positions preserves the motive. *Let $\mathcal O(t)$ be the capability an agreement holds in check, $V^{\rm exit}$ and $V^{\rm stay}$ the values of exiting first and of staying, exit occur at hazard $\mathcal H$, a fraction $\mu_x$ of the overhang be destroyed on exit, and the expected loss from exit be $\mathcal L=\mathcal H(1-\mu_x)\Lambda_x(\mathcal O)$ with $\Lambda_x$ increasing. (1) Deterring an irreversible, public exit requires $\upsilon F\ge V^{\rm exit}-V^{\rm stay}$; deterring a reversible covert preparation requires $\upsilon F\ge(1-\upsilon)(V^{\rm exit}-V^{\rm stay})$; the net gain from exit grows with the overhang when leaving with a decisive advantage gains faster than staying with one's share. (2) $\dot{\mathcal L}/\mathcal L=\dot{\mathcal H}/\mathcal H-\dot\mu_x/(1-\mu_x)+\varepsilon_\Lambda\dot{\mathcal O}/\mathcal O$, with $\varepsilon_\Lambda$ the elasticity of $\Lambda_x$, so growth of the overhang faster than the decline of the exit hazard is not sufficient for the loss to rise. (3) An agreement that preserves relative positions preserves the relative term of every payoff, and with it the motive in $V^{\rm exit}$.* *Proof:* (1) expected values. (2) Differentiate $\ln\mathcal L$; counter-case: $\mathcal O=e^{2t}$, $\mathcal H=e^{-1.5t}$, $\Lambda_x=\sqrt{\mathcal O}$ and $\mu_x=0$ give $\mathcal L=e^{-0.5t}$. (3) Proportional caps leave each share $w_i$ unchanged. $\square$

*Theorem {{#verify}}, by part.* (1) By (2) of {{assure}}, the required $\upsilon$ is $(T-R)/(T-P+F)$, which tends to one as $T\to\infty$ with $R$, $P$ and $F$ fixed. (2) Timely detection here means detection followed by an effective response before decisiveness: $p_d=1-\upsilon(T_{\rm dec})$ ({{detectlink}}), $\upsilon$ is non-decreasing in the time allowed, and $T_{\rm dec}$ falls as the loop gain rises ({{tdec}}). (3) Bounding the largest undetected defection bounds $T$, since a defection that cannot become decisive cannot earn a decisive payoff. The bound needs the coalition's effective compute to stay above the decisive ratio, an identity of the definition; effective compute is algorithmic efficiency times compute, so a coalition that caps compute keeps its bound only while its compute advantage grows as fast as diffusion erodes its algorithmic one. Nothing here derives that it does: the clause is the premise (advantage keeps pace), and the rate of erosion is an observation (EV9). Revealing types is the information role of {{participate}}. The Scope collects {{talk}} (unverifiable information), {{overhang}} (future commitments) and Section 4 (the reliability of the verifiers, who are correctors that success can buy). $\square$

*An instance.* One detailed plan among leading powers to slow development and delay superintelligence to about 2040 combines compute declarations, supply-chain accounting, inference-only retrofits and recomputation of workloads, research transparency, mutually assured destruction of compute if the deal declines, and hardened reserves of about two per cent of pre-deal compute; it bounds covert projects rather than requiring near-perfect detection, the route of {{verify}}(3). One author's scenario judgment puts decline in years five to ten at about 4% a year, half dissolution and half impairment, and cumulative decline over ten years at about 48% [obs: EV10]. X8 gives its durability and failure-mode rows beside every other path's, by the same standard.

### An illustration of the two cruxes

Let the per-period hazard on a path be 0.05 times its uncovered share plus a background 0.001, and on the cooperative path add a dissolution hazard times the loss its fallback carries. Survival at horizons 10, 30 and 100: continuation with the uncovered share rising from 0.2 by 0.01 a period, 0.871, 0.567, 0.028; continuation with a summable uncovered share, 0.966, 0.941, 0.873; cooperation with a summable share and dissolution 2% a period with fallback loss 0.5, 0.873, 0.696, 0.319; cooperation with dissolution 0.2%, 0.956, 0.913, 0.789 [num: legacy v5, CMP]. The numbers are illustrative. They show that a well-corrected continuation can beat a fragile arrangement, and that a rising uncovered share makes any modestly durable arrangement better past the near term.

### The failure-mode table: method

The table in X8 on which Section 12.1 compares is built in four steps, the same for every path, the paper's own design included. First, the failure modes are enumerated from two sources: the path's own documents, read for what they say ends or suspends it, and the rule for $\Theta$, which admits every mode that a named mechanism of Part I produces and the register does not contradict. Second, each mode is classified as reversible or not. A mode is reversible when a reversal exists that is named, stops short of O1 and O2, and is executed by someone other than the party whose failure it is; "the arrangement is renegotiated" is a reversal only if the documents say who can compel it, and otherwise it is recorded as a reversal by the parties. A branch is irreversible when correction cannot arrive before a protected loss: a decisive position that removes independent overrule ({{wingives}}), a crossing of the protected floor ({{safeguards}}(3)), an irreversible delegated decision or a contest that denies the recovery interval of {{lockin}}, or catastrophic escalation under {{preventown}}. Capture alone need not be irreversible while an independent corrector survives, and loss of capacity alone need not be irreversible while recovery is possible. The same test applies to every path, including the proposed design: recertification, rotation and standby name possible remedies, and only their timely execution prevents a protected loss. Loss of guaranteed correctability marks exit from the kernel of Definition {{#kernel}}; actual protected loss may follow later. Third, each mode is given the measurement that would show it under way, drawn from the signatures and cruxes of Sections 7.3 and 12.3, so that the table and the trigger of Section 12.1 use one set of indicators. Fourth, every cell names the kind of support behind it, with one standard for every row: the path's own documents, a scenario judgment by its authors, an observation in the register, a result of this paper under its premises, or none. A cell with none says so, and the paper's own design is marked by the same rule: its documents are X10, so its reversals are provided by design and not yet shown to work, and its hazard of breakdown has no estimate and no base rate in the register. The asymmetry of reversibility on which Section 12.1 rests is read off the reversibility column, mode by mode, not from the labels of the paths.
