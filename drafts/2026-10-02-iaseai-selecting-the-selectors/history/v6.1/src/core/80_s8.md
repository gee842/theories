# Part III. Responses: what would change the trajectory

## 8. Comparing paths, and the alternatives on their strongest terms

> **Claim.** Paths are compared by one standard that protects irreversible losses first and applies one range of uncertainty to every path, including the failure modes of cooperative designs. On that comparison, each alternative is described by what it changes, not by its name: the alternatives that succeed do so by moving the shared parameters of Definition {{#cond}} across their thresholds for every capable actor.
>
> **Why.** Labels such as "regulation", "cooperation" or "agreement" decide nothing. What decides is whether an arrangement changes the map from conduct to share, resources and standing, in which direction, for whom, and whether it survives the arrival of a decisive position.

### 8.1 The standard of comparison

**The protection standard [val].** Each candidate path is evaluated at common horizons, near (about five years), medium (about twenty) and long (fifty or more), over one justified range $\Theta$ of uncertain parameters applied to every path. First, bound the probability of O1 and O2 within each horizon over $\Theta$. Second, among paths whose bound stays below a stated tolerance, prefer the one with greater useful benefit, more fairly distributed, that keeps more options of correction open. Third, if no path meets the tolerance, prefer the one with the lowest bound that keeps options open. This is robust satisficing on irreversible losses, and a normative choice: an expected-value criterion can favour a small probability of a very large loss with a large expected benefit, and a discounted criterion can favour a race that ends in catastrophe with certainty when the hazard it adds is below the growth it adds (Jones 2024). O1 protects catastrophic, common-cause losses of life; deaths from benefits forgone are counted as a cost of the path in the comparison, not as O1, and the weight given to them is the normative crux N1.

**The range $\Theta$ [val].** A parameter value enters $\Theta$ if it is consistent with the dated evidence of the register and is produced by a stated mechanism whose premises are named, with a precedent or base rate where one exists. A value that needs a premise the register contradicts is excluded. The same rule applies to every path: a rising uncovered share on the continuation path, because its premises are named mechanisms the register does not contradict (EV5–EV7); and equally the capture of a common custodian, dissolution and coercive enforcement on the cooperative path. Every entry of $\Theta$ cites its source.

**{{!Definition:kernel}} (The correctable kernel) [def].** *The correctable kernel is the set of states of the world from which some non-anticipative strategy of admissible correction avoids O1 and O2 against every sequence of errors in the justified range. In viability theory it is a discriminating, or guaranteed-viability, kernel of a game against disturbances (Cardaliaguet, Quincampoix and Saint-Pierre 1999), not the viability kernel of undisturbed dynamics.*

Leaving the kernel is the loss of guaranteed correctability, a point of no guaranteed return earlier than the losses themselves. The corpus describes openness by four capacities, each a condition of the kernel [corpus: possibility-space]: *not yet exhausted* (未), no decisive end state reached; *still possible* (可), plurality of lineages, purposes and correctors kept; *still responsive* (应), humans able to suspend, modify or replace; *still able to turn back* (转), reversal possible. A closure made to protect openness, such as a defence or an emergency power, can state what would make it withdraw; a closure that cannot state that has left the kernel. The standard's second step is a preference for staying inside it.

**{{!Lemma:fallback}} (When an arrangement cannot do worse) [thm].** Two design conditions guarantee that an arrangement is no worse than continuation at any horizon. *Let both paths be driven by the same exogenous world process, the irreversible event on each occurring at date $t$ when a common uniform draw falls below that path's conditional hazard. Assume, on every history on which both have survived: (safe fallback) once arrangement $A$ has dissolved, its hazard is no higher than continuation $C$'s; and (bounded own risk) while $A$ lasts, its hazard, its own hazards of capture, restriction and collusion included, is no higher than $C$'s. Then $A$'s survival is at least $C$'s at every horizon, whatever the rule, possibly endogenous, by which $A$ dissolves.*

*Scope.* Where bounded own risk fails, there is no pointwise dominance and the standard compares the worst cases of both; that is where regulated competition can win. *Proof:* S8, which also shows that comparing after conditioning on the dissolution date fails when dissolution depends on the state.

An agreement whose fallback is a short sprint from preserved relative standing violates the first condition; a design whose own concentration, capture or collusion can exceed continuation's hazards violates the second (crux C4).

**{{!Proposition:survassure}} (Assurance in survival terms at a common horizon) [thm].** *With survival-first comparisons at a common horizon, the game between restraint and racing is dominance, a stag hunt, chicken or a prisoner's dilemma according to the signs of $\Phi^R-\Phi^T$ and $\Phi^S-\Phi^P$; mutual racing remains a strict equilibrium at every finite horizon at which racing alongside a racer survives better than staying out; and when the arrangement's own survival decays, the classification can change at long horizons.*

*Proof:* S8, with examples in which the shared hazard carries the argument for a leader.

So paths are compared at common finite horizons, not at an infinite one at which both branches of a hazardous world can tend to zero. The protection standard does not by itself rank continuation with strong correction against a design that changes selection; under the rule for $\Theta$ the worst case of each includes an irreversible loss, and Section 12.1 states the ranking as a judgment with its reasons.

### 8.2 Profiles, not labels

**{{!Definition:profile}} (What an arrangement changes) [def].** *An arrangement is described by a profile: which mechanisms of Part I it neutralizes, weakens, leaves or strengthens; its coverage (unilateral, plurilateral, every capable actor); its bindingness (Abbott et al. 2000); whether its enforcement survives a decisive position, and if it prevents one from arriving, by what verified mechanism; whether its beneficiaries can amend it; its fallback and own risk; and its durability. And selection: every arrangement that changes the map from conduct to share, resources and standing changes selection, so what is recorded is the direction, whether it lowers or raises the shared parameters of Definition {{#cond}} in every capable actor's game, or only in rivals'.*

Export controls aimed at preserving a lead change selection, toward a lead. Deterrence by threatened sabotage acts on $p_d$, as custody does, by a different means and with a different fallback. A change of selection in the *generative* direction has four marks: consequences return; success cannot buy control of its own assessment; decisive positions are kept from forming or held in common; measurement and language are shared. What this paper argues for is such a change, covering every capable actor, enforceable and durable, whatever it is called.

**{{!Definition:durability}} (Durability tests) [def].** *An arrangement is tested on its own documents and estimates, not on assumptions added by the tester: D1, its fallback if it dissolves; D2, its estimated hazard of breakdown and what drives it; D3, who keeps informed veto, modification, replacement and exit after any delegation; D4, whether its own correctors have full inbound row sums below one; D5, what happens when its strongest participant violates it; D6, succession and the continuity of essential services; D7, common causes and collusion among its checks and systems; D8, whether membership is open or stops at a club.*

The tests apply to every path, including the cooperative design of Section 10. An arrangement with the form of cooperation that fails D1–D3 on its own estimates may be called a *pseudo-solution*; the label summarizes test results and does not replace them. **Registered prediction [conj]:** arrangements that constrain conduct while leaving the shared parameters as they were erode as stakes and speed grow; those that move the parameters across their thresholds for every capable actor persist. The classification uses the profile, never the outcome.

### 8.3 The alternatives

**(a) Technical alignment and AI-assisted correction.** *Strongest form:* control scoped to early transformative systems, used under control to accelerate alignment and oversight while the window lasts (Greenblatt et al. 2024); weak supervisors eliciting part of a stronger model's capability (Burns et al. 2023); auditing with access to training data finding a planted objective that black-box access did not (Marks et al. 2025); oversight with the right topology; AI cheapening independent correction as fast as control; and, in its proponents' own plans, a strong international agreement ranked first. *What remains:* principal alignment does not change the game among principals; the window lasts only as long as the race allows; systems are retained for performance and safety gains are absorbed on interior branches; redundant oversight is capped by the common causes selection produces; AI-assisted correction is only as independent as its funding, access and appointment; and the common-cost part of incarnation is set at the outer loop. *Profile:* acts on tempo, observation and residual harm per unit; changes selection only when held by correctors separated from success, under constraints common to all.

**(b) Regulated competition with strong correction.** *Strongest form:* liability and licensing make consequences return; evaluation access is mandated and thresholds fixed in advance; where the effective hazard is high and capability transparent, pacing can be the unique equilibrium without any agreement (Fudenberg and Koh 2026); civil nuclear power after a major accident, and aviation with independent investigation and insurance pricing, show durable safety with unchanged commercial motives. *What remains:* coverage across jurisdictions, since one actor can start the race; the rule-makers are themselves in a contest of standing (Proposition {{>nounilateral}}); liability cannot exceed the assets of the liable; written commitments are already set relative to rivals and revised mostly toward weakening (EV2); and the precedents had no decisive prize. *Profile:* depends on content. Regulation that returns consequences and separates evaluators from success, across every capable actor, is the change this paper argues for, whatever it is called; regulation that constrains conduct while success can still buy the rule-makers is predicted to erode as stakes and speed grow.

**(c) Leadership and a safety margin.** *Strongest form:* a leader spends part of its lead on safety; a large lead can discourage a follower, and a leader committed to slowing can lead rivals to slow where the shared hazard is significant (Bueno de Mesquita, Dziuda and Polborn 2026). *What remains:* a lead shrinks by diffusion even when the follower does not accelerate, and spending it returns the contest toward parity, where intensity is greatest; what the margin must buy is coverage, and for hazard classes whose evidence lag exceeds it, it buys nothing; whether it suffices is judged inside the contest by the leader's own evaluators (**{{!Remark:lead}}**, S8). *Profile:* acts on tempo for a time; its best case, reciprocal slowing, is a first stage of the transition.

**(d) The benign winner: win, bind, share.** *Strongest form:* a coalition seeks a clear advantage, including military superiority and the denial of key inputs to adversaries, and then offers its benefits to others in exchange for their support (EV1); victors have bound themselves through institutions (Ikenberry 2001), a dominant power can supply public goods (Kindleberger 1973), and an encompassing ruler internalizes the shared conditions (Olson 1993). Its force comes from the combination of stick and carrot. *What remains:* after a decisive win what the others receive is set by the winner's objective alone, and a promise to share is worth breaking (Proposition {{#wingives}}); the race shifts winners toward those that extend dominance (Proposition {{#raceselects}}); and the winner is itself a field whose restraint depends on whether its own correctors can be bought by its successes. *Profile:* changes nothing in selection before victory. What would show it suffices: binding limits on what victory confers, benefits shared contemporaneously rather than promised, and the coalition's own correctors certified independent with succession rules that survive changes of leadership. A winner that adopts these has changed what it selects for.

**(e) A finite sprint, then restraint.** *Strongest form:* sprint to a decisive lead, then freeze or roll back and govern at the tempo of understanding. *What remains:* the path passes through its greatest exposure, and through the rivals' strongest motive for prevention, before any restraint begins; a decisive advantage not used to stop others' growth does not stay decisive, and using it so ends their O2 (**{{!Remark:sprint}}**, S8). *Profile:* nothing during the sprint.

**(f) Deterrence and prevention.** Prevention can be survival-preferred by the preventer.

**{{!Proposition:preventown}} (Prevention under the preventer's own criterion) [thm].** *Facing a rival it believes certain to race, an actor comparing outcomes by its own survival can prefer a strike that succeeds with probability $s_P$ and escalates to catastrophe with probability $\varepsilon_P$; as the racing branches deteriorate, the escalation needed to deter prevention rises toward one. Sustained prevention against a target that keeps rebuilding has a per-period hazard at least the rebuilding probability times the escalation probability.*

*Scope.* Prevention that permanently ends the target's capacity, or converts into joint monitoring, is a different branch. *Proof:* S8.

The objections are three, and none changes the criterion: it ends or endangers others' O1 and O2 (V2, V3); aimed at a rival believed certain to race, it can make a rival that was in fact survival-first act as the type it was feared to be (Jervis 1976; Kydd 1997); and striking at machine speed brings the delegation of Section 6.3. Deterrence by threatened sabotage acts on $p_d$ by threat, observing datacentres and making compute thresholds salient (Hendrycks, Schmidt and Wang 2025); its fallback is war or race, its credibility can reverse under survival-first comparisons, and algorithmic progress erodes thresholds stated in compute (EV9). Insuring against falling behind is not an exit, because the penalty is set by the winner (**{{!Remark:insure}}**, S8). **The hard case:** a survival-first actor that holds a high prior that a capable rival has an unbounded relative objective compares denial, a coalition that stops the decisive class, and prevention. Coverage then comes from consent or from coercion; coercive denial raises the weight on winning, and if it escalates falls under Proposition {{#preventown}}. The theory does not derive that its net effect is positive, and states the case as hard.

**(g) A verified agreement over a contest that remains.** Rivals agree to slow and to delay decisive capability, with compute declarations, supply-chain accounting, recomputation of workloads and mutually assured destruction of compute if the agreement dissolves. Let verification detect a defection in time with probability $\upsilon$, reversing it with a sanction $F$, and let trust $\varpi$ be the belief that the other cooperates; write $g_C$ for what cooperating gains against a cooperator and $g_D$ for what it loses against a defector.

**{{!Proposition:assure}} (Assurance) [thm].** *Cooperation is a best response iff $\varpi\ge g_D/(g_C+g_D)$ when both are positive; no trust suffices if $g_C\le0<g_D$; cooperation is dominant if $g_D\le0<g_C$. When the value of winning exceeds the value of cooperating ($T>R$), cooperation needs $\upsilon>(T-R)/(T-P+F)$; when $T<R$, it is possible without verification.*

*Proof:* S8, with the $n$-player bounds.

**{{!Theorem:verify}} (Verification and the value of winning) [thm].** Verification is required exactly when, and to the degree that, the value of winning exceeds the value of cooperating. *The required timely detection tends to one as $T$ grows for a fixed sanction. Detection counts only before a defection becomes decisive, a window acceleration shortens. Verification can also lower $T$ by bounding the largest defection that would go undetected, for instance by accounting for compute, which does not need near-certain detection; that bound holds only if the verified coalition's advantage in effective compute grows at least as fast as diffusion erodes it.*

*Scope.* Verification does not carry unverifiable information, future commitments unless the sanction keeps pace with the net gain from exit, which can grow with the capability held in check (**{{!Proposition:overhang}}**, S8), or its own reliability. *Proof:* S8.

One detailed plan of this kind is designed to be enforceable without trust, with proportional reserves and a coalition that must stay ahead of covert projects (EV10; X8). *Profile:* it slows and caps, acting on tempo and decisiveness, and keeps relative standing in its enforcement and fallback. That makes it a bridge; whether it can be the end depends on whether it lowers the value of winning or only bounds defection.

### 8.4 The comparison

| Path | What it changes | Fallback (D1) | Main residual risk | Crux |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Technical alignment and AI-assisted correction alone | Residual harm per unit; tempo | The race | Game among principals; absorption; common causes; the outer loop | C1, C3 |
| Regulated competition within jurisdictions | Selection in part, within one jurisdiction | The race | Coverage across jurisdictions; the rule-makers' contest | C2, EV2 |
| Leadership and margin | Tempo for a time | The race | The margin decays and is judged inside | C5 |
| The benign winner | Nothing before victory | Domination | Others' O2 rests on unverifiable restraint; the winner's internal field | C4, Test I |
| Finite sprint, then restraint | Tempo, after the sprint | Prevention | Peak exposure and prevention during the sprint | C1, C5 |
| Deterrence by sabotage | $p_d$ by threat | War or race | Misjudgment; militarization | C7 |
| Verified agreement over a remaining contest | Tempo and decisiveness, bounded | A short sprint from preserved standing | Fallback and handoff | C5, C7 |
| A design changing selection (Section 10) | The shared parameters, for every capable actor | Separately held functions (Section 10.5) | Its own capture, dissolution and restriction | C4, C9 |

The comparison turns on two quantities. If continuation keeps the uncovered hazardous share summable, it can beat a fragile cooperative arrangement; if the uncovered share rises on the continuation path, any cooperative arrangement with a modest hazard of dissolution does better at every horizon past the near term [num: an illustration in S8]. The first is crux C1; the second is what Sections 10 and 11 design for and test. Under the protection standard both paths fail the tolerance in their worst case, so the comparison moves to the standard's third step, which needs estimates on both sides; those estimates are what the cruxes of Section 12 would supply. An expected-value criterion that puts low probability on a rising uncovered share and values speed highly can prefer continuation, and a discounted criterion prefers racing whenever the hazard it adds is below the growth it adds. These disagreements are the normative cruxes. The analysis does not establish that every cooperative arrangement outperforms regulated competition, and Section 12 does not claim it.
