### The vocabulary of winning, and its critics

The doctrine of winning has at least two strands. *Within firms and public institutions:* an essay of 1970 argued that the social responsibility of business is to increase its profits within the rules of the game, which include law and ethical custom (Friedman 1970); agency theory recast the firm around aligning managers with owners (Jensen and Meckling 1976); a leading business association declared shareholder primacy the purpose of the corporation in 1997 and revised that statement in 2019; and market criteria were carried into institutions generally (Harvey 2005). In that turn self-interest moved from an assumption inside models to a norm of conduct and a criterion of design [obs, interpretive; EV15]. The doctrine's strongest form is that the residual claimant bears the residual risk, so that with complete contracts and externalities priced by law, maximizing the residual maximizes surplus; that separation fails where externalities cannot be separated from production decisions (Hart and Zingales 2017), which can be read as the unpriced coupling $\kappa$ [interpretive]. An early defence of commerce was that interests would tame the passions for glory and domination (Hirschman 1977); read that way, the pressure of winning is the passion for domination returning in the dress of interest, not the commercial tradition itself. *Between states:* doctrines of national survival and technological rivalry, from the Melian dialogue (Thucydides) and the Legalist programme of enriching the state and strengthening its army to the national Darwinism of late- and early-industrializing states alike and the technology races of the Cold War.

Both strands appear across civilizations, and so do their critics: Smith on sympathy; Darwin on the social instincts; Kropotkin on mutual aid; Polanyi on markets embedded in society; Ostrom on governing the commons; and, in China, Mozi's impartial care and condemnation of offensive war, and Kang Youwei's *Book of Great Unity*. Translated into doctrines of national survival in states that industrialized late, the strand was answered there too. Yan Fu rendered Huxley's 1893 lecture, whose point was that ethical progress consists in resisting the cosmic process, as a doctrine of 物竞天择, "things contend and heaven selects" (Yan 1898; Schwartz 1964); others set mutual aid against struggle and imagined the removal of boundaries. These histories illustrate how the pressure is narrated; they are not an account of any civilization's essence.

**The tradition behind V4.** One strand of the Chinese tradition the author draws on justifies authority by improving people's lives and contributing to a better historical trajectory. Like every tradition, that one also contains strands that justified power by strength. The theory takes the ideal as a positive account of what power is for; it does not assume that every institution realizes it, that any other tradition has one uniform motive, or that power necessarily corrupts.

### Ψ in full

*Its borrowed authority is a misreading.* "Survival of the fittest" was Spencer's phrase (1864), adopted by Darwin from the fifth edition of the *Origin*; Spencer himself expected societies to move from a militant to an industrial type. Darwinian arguments were later recruited for laissez-faire, and equally for the racism, eugenics and imperialism of reformers who rejected laissez-faire (Hofstadter 1944, who popularized the label; Leonard 2009); the label was largely a critics' term (Bannister 1979), and the borrowing belongs to no single strand of thought. *It misdescribes human beings.* In experiments in fifteen small-scale societies the canonical self-interest model failed in every society, and cooperation rose with market integration (Henrich et al. 2001); about half the subjects of a public-goods experiment were conditional cooperators (Fischbacher, Gächter and Fehr 2001); users govern commons without either a sovereign or privatization where institutions fit the resource (Ostrom 1990), though those commons were bounded and small, and scaling their design principles to global commons is itself an open problem (Stern 2011). Markets are institutions of cooperation among strangers; the target is the reading of them as a contest of all against all (EV14). Students of economics defected more often in a prisoner's dilemma (Frank, Gilovich and Regan 1993), a difference that later studies attribute in large part to who chooses the subject. These findings bear on persons; firms and states are shaped by selection on conduct, which is why the core's case against Ψ as a doctrine about nature does not rest on them alone (Section 3.8). *Held and attributed.* Realism's strongest reply to verifiable conduct is that conduct verified today does not settle uncertainty about a rival's future intentions (Copeland 2000b; Mearsheimer 2001), which is why the arrangements of Sections 10 and 11 verify capabilities and custody, which can be checked, rather than intentions.

**Status.** The desire for status, meaning respect, admiration and voluntary deference from others and distinct from power, is a fundamental human motive, observed across cultures, genders, ages and personalities (Anderson, Hildreth and Howland 2015). Status is relative, so it is never completed. Rank has two routes (Henrich and Gil-White 2001): dominance, gained through fear and control, and prestige, deference freely conferred on skill and success, and both yield real influence (Cheng et al. 2013). The drive is for respect; dominance is a substitute route to rank. Prestige also compounds (Merton 1968), which is why the condition on a generative order is that it not convert into control over the shared conditions or their correctors (EV16).

**Professed doctrine and performativity.** A doctrine can be held more widely in public than in private: people say what is rewarded rather than what they believe, so a public consensus that few privately hold persists and then tips (Kuran 1995). A race doctrine professed by actors who are selected for professing it may therefore be held less widely than it is stated, and visible cooperation can tip it (Section 5.4; Section 11). A model of behaviour can also make the world less like itself (MacKenzie 2006): a shared prediction of the race can avert its own fulfilment, which is the ground on which this paper treats prediction as an intervention (Section 11.2).

### The strongest objection from biology

Within-group cooperation was often favoured by competition between groups; Darwin's account of the social instincts rested on competition between tribes, and parochial altruism, cooperation inside and hostility outside, may have co-evolved with intergroup conflict (Choi and Bowles 2007). The answer is Lemma 3.11: a benefit common to all is never favoured by selection at any level of grouping, while a benefit confined to one group can be favoured between groups. At the scale of one host the benefits of the rules that keep it viable are of the first kind. Two routes to integration that need no rival group remain, and the core keeps both: polycentric trial among sub-units, as in nested commons governance and experiments within federations, which works wherever failure is not final; and reputation and indirect reciprocity, which change within-group payoffs without design (Panchanathan and Boyd 2004). What the core claims is narrower than in v6.0: failures that are global and irreversible cannot be learned by trial (Price 1972; Hamilton 1975).

### International relations

The logic of Section 3.8 has two sources: actors hold it, and selection on actions rewards its behaviour; and structure produces it, since anarchy, decisive advantage and the absence of a commitment device produce outcomes of the same kind among actors who do not hold it (Proposition 7.3; Proposition 5.4). An actor holding it does not stop compounding, because no level of standing makes more stop rewarding; on a continuation branch where the premises of Section 5.3 hold, Theorem 5.13 applies to its own capability too. To oppose the logic is to change the structure and the selection environment, not to oppose any actor: credible, verifiable limits on what anyone would do with decisive advantage; no decisive advantage without a commitment device; and common knowledge that the logic endangers its holder.

Readers in international relations will find the nearest neighbour of this account in defensive realism (Jervis 1978; Glaser 1994/95, 2010). It extends that position by a hazard in development that endangers its holder, which acts as a negative payoff to the offense as a weapon that endangers its holder does, the Biological Weapons Convention being the closest precedent; and it departs from it by treating self-help as a selected institution rather than a given (Wendt 1992). To the extent that the offense has the advantage and offense and defense are hard to tell apart, AI sits in the doubly dangerous quadrant of the offense–defense framework, where cooperation is hardest. The outcomes of the enforcement condition correspond to constructivism's three cultures of anarchy (Wendt 1999): enmity, where only stage equilibria hold; rivalry, where cooperation is enforced; friendship, where it is built into objectives. The theory adds the selection that moves a system between them, and the decisiveness that collapses the middle one. Balancing by others against a rising actor (Waltz 1979; Walt 1987) is a saturation mechanism that does not depend on the technology; it caps advantage only while the balancers can act before a lead becomes decisive, which is the difference between Branches I′ and I of Proposition 7.2, and Proposition 5.16(2) is its counterpart in talk.

### The alignment literature

The insufficiency of single-principal alignment follows from the multiplicity thesis of Critch and Krueger (2020), developed in work on robust agent-agnostic processes (Critch 2021), societal-scale risks (Critch and Russell 2023), natural selection among AI systems (Hendrycks 2023), cooperative AI (Dafoe et al. 2020), multi-agent risk, which already lists selection pressures as a risk factor (Hammond et al. 2025), and gradual disempowerment, in which institutions lose their dependence on the people they serve (Kulveit et al. 2025). Responsible development was framed early as a collective action problem among developers, with the factors that make cooperation likelier (Askell, Brundage and Hadfield 2019). Section 6.2 is an elementary illustration of the multiplicity thesis, and Proposition 6.7 is the selection-theoretic form of the collective action problem: which divergences the outermost loop pays to remove depends on who bears their cost, and the alignment-tax premise $(A_\tau)$ says when only a common constraint keeps one removed.

Four bodies of work since 2024 change what that constraint has to be. *Masked divergences* have been shown in constructed settings: deception that survives safety training (Hubinger et al. 2024), alignment faked by a model that takes itself to be in training (Greenblatt, Denison et al. 2024), scheming in context (Meinke et al. 2024), misalignment learned in agentic training that chat-style safety training hides from chat evaluations (MacDiarmid et al. 2025), and capability evaluations strategically underperformed (van der Weij et al. 2024). For this class an evaluation of propensity cannot show absence, so Remark 6.8 states admission in capability, with auditors holding weights, activations and training data, the access with which auditors found a planted objective that black-box access did not (Marks et al. 2025). *Control* supplies the capability side: protocols that stay safe without assuming the system's cooperation (Greenblatt, Shlegeris, Sachan and Roger 2024), whose evaluation must allow for self-preference and collusion within a lineage (Panickssery, Bowman and Feng 2024; Gardner-Challis et al. 2026), and adversarial oversight whose failure by obfuscated arguments (Barnes and Christiano 2020) prover–estimator debate is designed to avoid (Brown-Cohen et al. 2025; Proposition 6.9). *Concentration inside the principal:* systems whose alignment targets are written by a few people can be given singular or secret loyalties (Davidson, Finnveden and Hadshar 2025); the systems most likely to become carriers are deployed first inside the developer, beyond the reach of an admission condition applied at release (Stix et al. 2025); and agents serving rival principals can learn to collude without being told to (Calvano et al. 2020; Fish, Gonczarowski and Shorrer 2024). *The tax itself* has begun to be measured, class by class: safety training lowered the reasoning accuracy of reasoning models (Huang et al. 2025); keeping reasoning monitorable means not optimizing against it (Baker et al. 2025) or paying inference compute (Guan et al. 2025); control protocols have measured overheads (Terekhov et al. 2026); and preference training raised most evaluation scores of larger models, a tax of zero or below (Bai et al. 2022).

What is added is the mechanism by which the game reproduces itself and its correctors: selection on relative standing that is blind to what all share, with no misalignment needed; the second-order selection for control over correction; and the nested reward loop with its alignment-tax premise, under which research that lowers the tax is a public good that competitors undersupply, and progress that lowers the hazard per unit of racing is partly absorbed by more racing (Section 6.4). Multi-agent failures include miscoordination and collusion among AI systems as well as conflict among principals, and the stress tests count them.

### What is absorbed, and what is added

Much of the anatomy is known and absorbed: multipolar traps; realism's account of competition under anarchy (Waltz 1979; Mearsheimer 2001; Glaser 2010; Jervis 1976); Goodhart's and Campbell's laws; regulatory capture (Stigler 1971; Laffont and Tirole 1991; Dal Bó 2006); increasing returns and lock-in (Arthur 1989); the systems archetypes "success to the successful" and "escalation" (Senge 1990; Sterman 2000); self-help as an institution (Wendt 1992); security communities (Deutsch et al. 1957); the translation invariance of relative selection (Weibull 1995), which is Lemma 3.3(1); the "generator functions" of catastrophic risk; and the AI literature above. For the works nearest to particular results, one sentence each says what is taken and what is added, so that the claim of novelty stays honest.

*International relations and arms control.*

Added: elsewhere the relative objective is not given by anarchy but selected (Proposition 3.6(3)) and manufactured by compounding wherever reinvestment's relative return exceeds its common return (Proposition 3.9), so it changes when what is selected changes.

### What is new

What is new is the organization, and what it implies for design.
1. **One loop gain organizes known instability results.** Increasing dominance in industrial organization, the discouragement effect in contests and lock-in under increasing returns become cases of one structural loop gain $\Gamma$, built from relative selection, convertibility and decisiveness, with provision and diffusion inside it (Proposition 2.2; Proposition 2.3). The levers then act channel by channel: sharing and diffusion lower the gain in every channel, provision not won in the contest lowers it where decisiveness rises less than in proportion to the stock, and correction where decisiveness rises with the stock's level (Proposition S2.4). A few parameters, the won share of capacity $\omega$, appropriability $\chi$ and decisiveness $r$, recur across the thresholds of Table 7.1: that is the formal sense in which the conditions are interconnected.
2. **The price of standing is Powell (1991) generalized by the common channel.** Compounding narrows the working objective exactly where reinvestment's relative return exceeds its common return (Proposition 3.9); each actor then computes its interest correctly, and the error is in the game (Remark 3.10).
3. **Selecting the selectors.** The reflexive closure is a selection result, not an assumption: the return to influence over correctors grows with the square of share (Proposition 4.3); independent correction must grow at a stated rate where AI cheapens control, and faster where delegation erodes the human capacity it rests on (Proposition 4.5; Proposition 4.6); reforms below a threshold are re-absorbed, and keystones are where they are not (Proposition 4.11); and the closure brings with it the certificates, alarms, pause design and silent route of Section 4.
4. **The enforcement condition, split.** The condition for a single deviation with absorbing breakout is textbook; what is added is its split, deviation by deviation, into the probability $p_d$ that a deviation becomes decisive and its bound $\bar p_d$ (Proposition 5.4), its reading as timely detection (Remark 5.5), and the comparative statics by which growth in the won share of capacity moves both sides toward failure (Proposition 5.17).
5. **The nested loop under the alignment-tax premise.** AI enters as instrument and as weak and strong incarnation; which divergences are countered is decided by cost incidence at the level that selects developers (Proposition 6.7), and for masked divergences coordination is necessary but, where the safeguard tax stays bounded away from zero while the sanction is bounded and detection falls toward zero as capability rises, not sufficient; if the tax also falls, insufficiency depends on expected enforceable sanction falling below that tax (Remark 6.8).
6. **Where integration must be anticipated.** At the scale of one host, failures that are global and irreversible cannot be learned by trial, and no selection among groups supplies what is common to all (Lemma 3.11); understanding acts there, and the polycentric trial that remains works only where failure is not final.
7. **A constructive obligation made explicit.** The generative arrangement is asked to preserve shared viability and executable correction through changes in capability, successors and institutions. This is a specification and research target, not a general preservation result. Proposition S16.1 composes existing field and host arguments through a maintained liability relation to give one explicit invariant region, while Test N asks whether the institutional means of correction endure. The composition is a modest extension; the distinctive larger contribution depends on deriving and testing an implementable arrangement whose protection survives its own selection dynamics.

Three further results are new in form though their parts are known: the compounding condition with provision not won in the contest, the formal content of trees (Proposition 2.3); the two-channel accounting identity (Proposition 3.5); and how common constraints survive, not the invisibility of a common term, which is absorbed, but its connection to incidence, evasion and the capture of enforcement (Lemma 3.3(2)(3)).

**Endogenous measurement and preference [conj].** The masking model is the simplest form of epistemic closure: reality exists and the sensor is biased. Deeper forms are listed for research (TESTS, Study 4): capture of interpretation; capture of the concepts in which a failure could be expressed, so that the variable itself disappears from what the system can represent; and capture of preference, in which people no longer regard the state as harm. Each is a further step of the reflexive closure; none is modelled here.

## X2. Substrates

The pressure needs only relative selection, convertible winnings and decisiveness; its closure needs only that the successful can reshape their selection (Section 2.4). The instances below show the same structure in different materials. They are demonstrations; none is the theory's ground, and each is stated only as far as its evidence goes.

- **Cells.** A cancer lineage evades the signals that limit proliferation, recruits the surrounding tissue into supporting its expansion, and evades the immune response that would remove it (Hanahan and Weinberg 2011; Aktipis et al. 2015). Growth is not the fault line: large animals show far less cancer than their cell numbers alone would predict, plausibly because suppression evolved with size (Peto's paradox; Caulin and Maley 2011), though prevalence does rise with size, and lineages with repeated bursts of accelerated size evolution show less of it (Butler et al. 2025). What decides is whether growth stays integrated with the body that sustains it, the sense in which "cancer" is used in Section 3.8.
- **Firms.** Markets with increasing returns lock in early leads (Arthur 1989). The doctrine that the purpose of business is profit "so long as it stays within the rules of the game" (Friedman 1970) meets its limit where the successful can buy the rules. The same school analysed that possibility (Stigler 1971), and its remedy was to shrink discretionary rule-making that could be captured. The disagreement is about the remedy: a planetary technology with unpriced coupling has no option of making no rules, and where common rules are absent, the winners' systems make them in fact. The doctrine's own argument that managers spending others' money on social goals tax without representation supports public, independent correction, not voluntary self-regulation by firms.
- **States.** In security competition a gain for one is read as a threat to another, and falling behind is read as fatal: the belief that one is in a struggle for survival is the drive of Section 7.1, and the logic Section 3.8 opposes.
- **Federations.** Members compete for capital and residents, and a race to the bottom in standards follows; the strongest can capture the centre; common standards and fiscal equalization change what the federation selects for (X10 takes federations at both orders).
- **Science.** Metrics allocate positions, and the positions set the metrics.
- **Training loops.** A policy is selected by a reward its developer sets, and the developer is selected by a contest; pairwise preference comparisons see only differences between responses, the blindness of Lemma 3.3(1) in miniature. Trained systems that are retained by relative performance and control resources they can reinvest become carriers themselves (Section 6.4).
- **Another planet.** Nothing in Definition 2.1 refers to a culture, a political system or a species. A civilization on another planet, with other drives and institutions, would face the same pressure wherever selection is relative, winnings convert, conversion is decisive and the successful can edit the selection.

## X8. The alternatives in full

Each alternative is given its best case on its merits, how it would be installed, what remains, what it changes, and what evidence would show that it suffices. Section 8.3 of the core states each in one paragraph; the formal results are in the core and S8. The chapter ends with the tables Section 12.1 compares on: the policies now in force, profiled in advance; the main residual risk of each path in the comparison of Section 8.4; the durability of every arrangement by its own documents; and the failure modes of every path, with whether each can be reversed. One evidence standard holds in every row, the paper's own design included.

The constructive target of Section 10.4 applies equally to every alternative: can the arrangement retain effective correction through changes in capability, leadership, providers and rules? A favourable test of today's systems does not establish that persistence; neither does naming a common custodian or independent regulator. The bounded result Proposition S16.1 holds only while its incoming-gain and effective-liability bounds remain in force. Which design can maintain such conditions is C4 and Test N, assessed through actual access, authority and interventions. A competing arrangement that meets the target counts in its favour, even if it preserves competitive motives; the target does not establish the author's preferred arrangement as uniquely sufficient.

### Technical alignment and AI-assisted correction

**Best case.** The strongest form is not "technical alignment alone". Control is scoped to early transformative systems and used, under control, to accelerate alignment and oversight research while the window lasts (Greenblatt, Shlegeris, Sachan and Roger 2024): control protocols tested against red teams raise safety at a given level of usefulness in the settings studied. Weak supervisors can elicit part of a stronger model's capability (Burns et al. 2023); in auditing games, teams with access to training data found a planted hidden objective that a team with black-box access alone did not, which supports mandated access (Marks et al. 2025). Oversight can be built with the right topology, redundantly or adversarially by debate (Proposition 6.9), and formal methods certify invariants without predicting trajectories. AI may cheapen independent correction as fast as control, so that AI-assisted correction keeps pace (Proposition 4.5), and correction people cannot follow can still lower residual risk (Proposition S6.4). Research can drive the alignment tax below zero, class by class, until the safe variant is also the winning one; once published it is common to all, so the safeguards it makes profitable spread without coordination, while those it brings to a tax of exactly zero are neutral under selection. At zero, withdrawing the common constraint additionally needs evidence that the safeguard persists without it; existing unsafe variants need not disappear (Section 6.4; Proposition 6.7). Its proponents' own plans rank a strong international agreement with a long slowdown first, and treat technical work as what one does when that is unavailable.

**What remains.** Principal alignment does not change the game among principals (Proposition 6.1). The window lasts only as long as the race allows, and the race shortens it. Systems are retained for performance (Lemma 6.5), and progress that lowers the hazard per unit of racing or delegation is partly absorbed by more of both (Proposition S6.2). Redundant oversight is capped by common causes, which selection produces, and certified row sums are a sufficient certificate, not a proof of independent errors (Proposition 6.9). AI-assisted correction is only as independent as its funding, access, appointment and lineage. One part of the tax no technique removes: where countering a divergence means waiting for evidence that arrives after a manifestation window, its cost is time, which relative selection with decisive advantage always prices (Proposition 5.12). For masked divergences, which change under evaluation or appear only in some contexts, admission deters evasion only while detection times the enforceable sanction covers the tax. With a fixed positive tax and a bounded sanction, detection tending to zero eventually defeats deterrence; if the tax falls faster, deterrence can persist (C9 and C12 together). A detector's failure to verify removal is a limit of assurance, not proof that removal or low realized hazard is impossible. Under the protection standard, the recommendation where independent control cannot establish safety is a common capability bound, supported by auditor access to weights, activations and training data; beyond it, the independent coverage and control condition of E3 applies even under broad E1, with suspension or rollback where needed, while E2 separately governs the decisive class (Remark 6.8; EV6). Which divergences are tolerated is set at the outermost loop (Proposition 6.7).

**What it changes.** Tempo, observation and residual harm per unit, and the alignment tax where the research that lowers it is published. It becomes part of a change of selection, G3 and G7, when held by correctors separated from competitive success, under constraints common to all. **What would show it suffices:** the safety of control protocols at a fixed audit budget not falling across generations; catch rates of planted defects at fixed human hours not falling as capability rises, controlled for evaluation awareness (C3a); auditors with fixed access still finding planted and emergent hidden objectives as capability rises (C12); independent evaluators keeping certificates while depending on the evaluated for access; the tax measured negative, or zero with evidence that the safeguard persists after the constraint is withdrawn, class by class (C9); residual risk per hazard class meeting its registered cumulative-risk tolerance, with a summable upper bound where unbounded survival is claimed (C1). A falling trend alone is insufficient.

### Regulated competition with strong correction

**Best case.** Competition in products and services continues; liability and licensing make consequences return; evaluation access is mandated and thresholds fixed in advance; and a capability-race model supplies a conditional pacing equilibrium without an agreement (Fudenberg and Koh 2026; comparison below). There are precedents of durable safety with unchanged commercial motives: an industry that knows one accident can end it for all, as civil nuclear power came to after a major accident; and aviation, with independent accident investigation, liability and insurance pricing. This is the bounded competition a cooperative order can contain (V5).

**Installation.** Each jurisdiction regulates its own developers; coverage must reach every capable actor, since the race is set by the most race-inclined actor (Proposition 5.1); the rule-makers are themselves in a contest of standing, so no one of them imposes the liability alone (Proposition 9.2); rule-making is subject to Section 4.

**What remains.** Liability cannot exceed the assets of the liable (Shavell 1986): for catastrophic and common harms that is the point at which a common cost cannot be turned into a private one, which is why liability alone does not settle the alignment tax. Regulation in one market leaves the load elsewhere. Two developers' frameworks condition commitments on rivals, one of them stating that it cannot commit to mitigations unilaterally because of a collective action problem while keeping a discretion to pause; one unreviewed study codes 77% of the changes it traced, in twelve pairs of consecutive framework versions, as weakening (EV2). Public evaluation bodies have shifted their mandates toward industry contact under voluntary access (EV3). The precedents had no decisive prize.

**The pacing equilibrium, compared with E3.** Fudenberg and Koh (2026, Theorem 1(c)) show that sufficiently high effective hazard sustains their pacing profile as a Markov perfect equilibrium and excludes their everywhere-racing profile. They do not establish uniqueness. Their pacing profile can itself race forever from sufficiently unsafe initial states; safe pacing is an equilibrium outcome from a tie on safety. Their private-capability extension permits pacing at every monitoring lag when effective hazard is high enough. This hazard is defined by racing payoffs, not by perceived or internalized harm (EV11; full paper checked). The result supports the possibility of E3 under competition; the correspondence with this paper's mechanisms remains to be established.
- *The effective hazard.* In this paper's translation, which is heuristic and not the source's definition, it falls with what the objective an actor acts on counts and with what it sees. Relative accounting weighs a shared hazard by $1+\theta$ and drops it at $\theta=-1$ (Proposition 3.5), and compounding narrows the working objective as the discounted loop gain rises (Proposition 3.9), so the weight on shared harm falls where the gain rises; whether the source model's effective hazard falls requires a mapping of payoffs. Where harm manifests late, or is masked, the hazard seen is below the real one by the gap on which the silent route runs (Proposition 4.13): a path can lie in the pacing region of the real parameters while its participants, seeing less, compute the racing region; the translation would have to be checked by mapping one model's payoffs into the other's.
- *Transparency* fails where the conditions of winning predict that it fails: the quiet deviation is the one that binds enforcement (Proposition 5.4), captured evaluation masks the harm (Proposition 4.8), masked divergences hide under evaluation (Remark 6.8), and internal deployment and the open–closed gap hide capability (EV5, EV6). Monitoring delay alone does not defeat pacing, since the source's lag extension covers it; what falls outside its scope is a threat that arises before deployment and a breakout that is absorbing.
- *What the comparison adds.* Where outrunning the frontier can yield a decisive position, the prize ends the game and the enforcement condition, not the hazard, decides (Proposition 5.4). Speed can also be bought outside the frontier by delegation (Section 6.3), and the frontier's position is measured by someone whom success can buy (Proposition 4.3).

G1, consequences returned, and G6, shared measurement, are candidate ways to support pacing in this paper's account. Their necessity does not follow from the source theorem, and transparency is not required in every extension. The empirical questions are whether the present parameters support a pacing equilibrium, whether it is selected, and whether the current state lies on a path that reaches safety, class by class. The measurements that answer it are those of Section 7.3 and the cruxes of Section 12.3: late-manifesting hazard classes and their evidence lags (C1), the open–closed gap and internal deployment (C5), and the conversion data of T4 (C2). Where a class reaches and sustains safe pacing under present incentives, E3 for that class needs no additional agreement; equilibrium existence by itself does not establish that outcome. Otherwise the arrangements of Section 11.3 must be assessed for whether they make the safe path reachable and durable. The correspondence remains this paper's reading, to be checked against the full paper's payoffs.

**What it changes.** This depends on content. Regulation that returns consequences and separates evaluators from success, across every capable actor, is G1 and G3 for all, changes the basin, and is the change this paper argues for, whatever it is called. Regulation that constrains conduct while success can still buy the rule-makers is predicted to erode as stakes and speed grow. **What would show it suffices:** competitor clauses not invoked; thresholds held when rivals advance; the mandates of evaluation bodies widening as the stakes rise; findings executed against leaders (C2); effective liability above the private advantage of load for every capable carrier.

### Leadership and a safety margin

**Best case.** A leader spends part of its lead on safety: it slows, evaluates and hardens before the follower catches up. A large lead can discourage the follower (Harris and Vickers 1987; Konrad and Kovenock 2009); a laggard far behind under moderate hostility does not gamble on risky technology (Stafford, Trager and Dafoe 2022); and a developer able to commit first to a slightly slower speed gains, because rivals slow too, a local result that does not depend on holding a lead (Bueno de Mesquita, Dziuda and Polborn 2026). One developer's framework includes such a "leading" scenario (EV2).

**What remains.** Remark S8.2: leads diffuse even when the follower does not accelerate; the margin must buy coverage, and buys nothing for hazard classes whose evidence lags exceed it; it is judged inside the contest; and a leader seeing its window close has a motive to strike first. Spending the lead returns the contest toward parity, where effort is highest in contests of step-by-step innovation (Aghion, Harris, Howitt and Vickers 2001) and, where a lead discourages the follower, the price of standing peaks in the contests computed (Section 7.2). **What it changes.** Tempo for a time; it keeps the objective of winning. Its best case, a first commitment to slow that rivals answer by slowing, is a step toward reciprocal restraint, the first stage of a transition. **What would show it suffices:** leads not spent on speed; rivals slowing in response; margins long relative to the evidence lags of the hazard classes.

### The benign winner: win, bind, share

**Best case.** The strongest form is that a winner wins, binds itself and shares. After great wars, victors have bound themselves through institutions to lower others' fear and their own costs of enforcement (Ikenberry 2001); a dominant power can supply public goods a field of equals would not (Kindleberger 1973; Gilpin 1981), and unipolarity can be stable (Wohlforth 1999); an encompassing ruler internalizes the shared conditions (Olson 1993). In the model of Section 3.5 a single developer restrains whenever restraint is worth more to the field than it costs, so the failure lies in the race, not in the existence of a winner. The holder of the first nuclear monopoly, through the period of superiority to 1954, did not extend its dominance to everything it could reach (X11). In AI the strategy has been proposed, in its authors' terms, as humanitarian and defensive: a coalition seeks a clear advantage, including military superiority and the denial of key inputs such as chips and semiconductor equipment to adversaries, and then offers its benefits to others in exchange for their support, so that adversaries give up competing (EV1). It persuades by promising both security and benefits; both rest on the winner's restraint after the fact, which is what the commitment tests examine. By this paper's own results the form is strongest where the winner still needs the governed: a winner whose self-model is accurate toward what can be divided, $\theta=\kappa_W$, gives them $\kappa_W/(1+\kappa_W)$ under logarithmic utility (Proposition 5.8; Proposition 3.6(2)). Most strong international institutions were built by victors; that is the record this path appeals to, and the record a transition among rivals near parity has to answer (X11).

**What remains.** The commitment tests. After a decisive win what the others receive is set by the winner's objective alone, and a promise to share is worth breaking (Proposition 5.8). What the race cannot verify beforehand is $\kappa_W$ after victory: a winner, like any institution, stays answerable because it depends on the labour, consent and judgment of the people it governs, and AI can substitute for each (Kulveit et al. 2025). The historical cases had advantages that were not decisive, and their restraint rested on institutions others could check and on that dependence (Acemoglu and Robinson 2012); whether AI removes it is crux C13. Where conduct before victory and after it go together, the race shifts winners toward those that extend dominance (Proposition 7.3). And the winner is itself a field of agencies, firms, laboratories and systems, whose restraint after victory depends on whether its own correctors can be bought by its successes; an AI aligned to the winner is not thereby aligned to the people it governs (Section 6.3).

**What it changes.** Nothing in selection before victory; after victory, everything depends on the winner's internal field. **What would show it suffices**, before victory: binding limits on what victory would confer; benefits shared as they arise rather than promised; the coalition's own correctors, constitutional correctors included, certified with row sums below one, with succession rules that survive changes of leadership (Test I); and a measured dependence of the coalition on the people it would govern that AI does not erode (C13). A winner that adopts these has changed what it selects for.

### A finite sprint, then restraint

**Best case.** An actor sprints to a decisive lead, then freezes or rolls back its own hazardous capability and governs at the tempo of understanding. Under the premises of Proposition 5.14, this gives positive survival on the growth channel. Decisive contests can be won with a modest edge when $r$ is large, so the sprint need not run far beyond understanding. The post-sprint choice must be specified: relinquishing superiority is different from preserving it by stopping others.

**Relinquishment after the sprint.** The winner freezes or rolls back itself, allows others to catch up and accepts that its advantage ceases to be decisive. Permanent superiority is not required by its survival criterion. This branch imposes no freeze on rivals. It must still answer three objections: exposure and preventive incentives during the sprint (Proposition 5.9; C1, C5); whether the announced self-restraint survives changes in the winner's leadership and internal correctors (C4); and whether the contest that returns as rivals catch up stays within the enforcement, coverage and human-authority requirements. Freezing one actor's hazardous variety does not bound the hazards all other actors produce under (shared failure). Those conditions, not unequal incidence of an imposed stop, decide this branch.

**Permanent monopoly after the sprint.** The winner instead keeps its decisive capability and stops rivals from acquiring it. The stop then has unequal incidence: the holder retains what the others may not build. It is evaded wherever a type's gain from evasion exceeds its expected sanction (Lemma 3.3(2), (3)); the required sanction is supplied by the holder. The holder is also a large conversion target, by the square-of-share return to influence and the high moment of cultivated conversion (Proposition 4.3; Proposition 4.5(4)). Where maintaining the monopoly removes others' consequential decision authority, V2 and V3 reject that loss. These objections apply to this continuation, not automatically to relinquishment.

**Against common custody.** E2 and common custody also constrain access to the decisive class, and a coalition that stops outsiders need not end their O2 (the non-proliferation row of X11). Each branch is therefore compared by incidence, current control and actual correction, with the same evidence standard:

| | Sprint, then relinquish | Sprint, then preserve monopoly | Common custody (Section 10.5; X10) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who bears the stop | The sprinter freezes or rolls back itself; rivals may catch up | Everyone except the holder is barred from acquiring what it keeps | Everyone's unilateral use, custodians included, is constrained by design; equal incidence in effect is unmeasured |
| D4 Who corrects the holder | Initially its own correctors; an independent transfer or constraint can be added but must be specified | The holder's own correctors, a large conversion target | Separately held functions and independently appointed correctors by design; certificates not yet measured (C4) |
| D5 When the strongest violates | Before relinquishment is effective, an unkept promise has no external sanction; afterward, the renewed contest's actual enforcement matters | No independent party can compel a decisive holder | Dependence caps, standby and installed custody controls where available; host-jurisdiction execution, then the compared-not-settled coercive fallback; the hard case remains |
| What ends it | Verified rollback or erosion of superiority; continued coverage and restraint still need support | The holder's choice unless an independent constraint is installed | Registered release and withdrawal procedures, distinct before and after construction; built capability and knowledge prevent assuming a return to the pre-build state |

The comparison does not establish that custody is easier to enforce than a credible relinquishment. Its proposed advantage over monopoly is incidence that includes its members and correctors independent of the holder; those are claims to test, not consequences of the word “common.”

**What it changes.** Relinquishment changes the winner's post-sprint trajectory and allows the contest to return; monopoly preserves the lead through a restriction on others. Neither erases the sprint's exposure. **What would show it suffices:** measured exposure within tolerance; independently verifiable self-restraint, transfer or restraint of the holder; and enforcement and coverage that remain effective after the chosen continuation.

### Deterrence and prevention

Prevention can be survival-preferred for the preventer (Proposition 8.5). The objections are three, and none is a change of criterion. *Normative:* it ends or endangers others' O1 and O2. *Epistemic:* aimed at a rival believed certain to race, it destroys, when the rival is in fact survival-first, a game in which restraint was possible, and can lead the rival to act as the type it was feared to be (the spiral model; Jervis 1976; Kydd 1997), while shifts that cannot be observed raise the chance of mistaken prevention (Debs and Monteiro 2014). *Technical:* detecting unobservable progress and striking at machine speed brings the delegation of Section 6.3.

Deterrence by threatened sabotage aims to make a decisive sprint not worth attempting. *Best case:* large datacentres can be observed and compute thresholds made salient (Hendrycks, Schmidt and Wang 2025). *What remains:* red lines that are unclear and hard to monitor, and doubtful credibility of the threat of sabotage, make deterrence of this kind unstable unless they are refined (Abecassis 2025); where unsanctioned gains grow without bound relative to fixed cooperative and punishment values, required timely detection approaches one (Theorem 8.7); among nuclear-armed rivals credibility is the first problem, and under survival-first comparisons it can reverse; pushing AI into military capability strengthens the pressure to delegate (Proposition 6.2); verification that supports deterrence can also supply targets (Coe and Vaynman 2020); and algorithmic progress erodes thresholds stated in compute (EV9). *What it changes:* it acts on $p_d$ by threat, and its fallback is war or race.

### The hard case

Let a survival-first actor hold the prior $\mu$ that a capable rival holds an unbounded relative objective. When $\mu$ is low, its best course is to propose verifiable limits, and to take narrow E1 unilaterally and verifiably in its own strategic loops, the costly signal of Proposition 11.1. When $\mu$ is high, it compares denial, a coalition that stops the decisive class, and prevention. Coverage of every capable actor then comes from consent or from coercion. Denying the inputs to the decisive class is the coercive form of a stop; it raises the weight on winning and the trust threshold, and if it escalates it falls under Proposition 8.5. The theory does not derive that its net effect is positive and does not endorse it. Section 12.1 admits the coercive restriction of frontier inputs to its comparison only under three conditions of its own, and even there compares it rather than recommends it. If neither consent nor a coercion of positive net effect is available, no actor has a secure position: that is the hard case, stated as such. Insuring against falling behind is not an exit (Remark S8.4): it accepts the frame of the contest, and against a winner of the logic Section 3.8 describes, falling behind cannot be made survivable.

### A verified agreement over a contest that remains

**Best case.** Rivals agree to slow and to delay decisive capability, with compute declarations, supply-chain accounting, recomputation of workloads, research transparency, and mutually assured destruction of compute if the agreement dissolves; it works without trust and bounds the largest covert project that could escape detection. One detailed plan of this kind, a deal among leading powers to delay superintelligence to about 2040 (Larsen et al. 2026; EV10), adds inference-only retrofits of datacentres and hardened reserves of about two per cent of pre-deal compute, and aims to end winner-take-all dynamics through many frontier developers, transparency, broad diffusion and a citizens' dividend. Its own scenario estimates do not show near-certain dissolution: one author's judgment puts decline in years five to ten at about 4% a year, half dissolution and half impairment, and cumulative decline over ten years at about 48% (EV10). Its handoff to AI systems is a conditional decision that weighs further alignment research against other risks, and it keeps an option to extend the pause. The plan is one instance of a position, keep the contest, slow it and stay safe; it is cited so that the tests can be applied to documents rather than to a type, and the answer below is to the position, not to the plan's figures.

**What remains.** Verification is required exactly to the degree that the value of winning exceeds the value of cooperating; its threshold is the deterrence condition of the inspection game (Avenhaus, von Stengel and Zamir 2002), the enforcement condition seen from verification (Remark 5.5); and the net gain from exit can grow with the capability held in check (Theorem 8.7; Proposition S8.5). The bound on covert projects holds only while the coalition's compute advantage keeps pace with the diffusion of algorithmic efficiency, which is not derived (EV9).

**What it changes.** It slows and caps, acting on tempo and decisiveness, and keeps relative standing in its enforcement, through proportional reserves and a coalition that must stay ahead of covert projects; the conversion of success into control over correction is addressed only through transparency. An agreement that preserves relative positions preserves the relative term of every payoff (Proposition S8.5(3)); that term alone does not establish a positive net gain from exit or defeat an effective sanction. The agreement is a bridge if its bound on covert capability or its effective sanction cannot keep pace with deviations; it can be a durable endpoint if every deviation remains within (14) and the other applicable requirements, including independent correction and coverage, remain met. G2 and G5 can reduce the burden on enforcement without being necessary for every endpoint. Common custody faces the same verification limits; any claimed advantage in its fallback or in the value of winning must be shown across its allowed lifecycle, including withdrawal after authorized construction (X10).

### Normal technology and resilience

**Best case.** AI is a normal technology: its impacts materialize not as capabilities improve but as they are diffused through the economy, at the pace of institutional adoption, so the danger to govern is that of a powerful general-purpose technology, and policy should build resilience and reject nonproliferation, which lowers it (Narayanan and Kapoor 2025). In the history of general-purpose technologies, great-power advantage has come from the capacity to diffuse them, not from a lead at the frontier (Ding 2024). On this reading no lead at the frontier is decisive in itself, and custody of a decisive class is a concentration of power answering a danger that does not arise.

**What remains.** The growth channel of Section 5.3 binds at the pace of deployment, not of the frontier: where adoption is slow enough for contestable coverage to keep up, (coverage lags) fails for that class, and where it is not, the rate of deployment is the rate to compare with coverage; both are measured class by class (C1). Whether any advantage is decisive is C5. The diagnosis registers both, and where both are measured favourable the activation of tier 2 lapses (THEORY §12.3) while the requirements on correction, coverage and measurement stand. The concentration that custody risks is answered by its form, separately held functions certified by row sums, and by the guardian test (Remark 10.3; Remark 10.5). **What it changes.** Its prescriptions, resilience, diffusion of what is not decisive and shared measurement, are G1, G3 and G6, and diffusion is a lever of Part I in every channel (Proposition S2.4); it does not change the selection of those who would lead where leads do compound. **What would show it suffices:** contestable coverage keeping pace with deployment class by class (C1), and positive evidence that balancing prevents decisive leads on the relevant capability and response measures, with uncertainty over a registered horizon (C5). An unmeasured decisive lead is not evidence that none exists.

Read across paths, the table has one failure in common: one party comes to hold decisive capability. On continuation it is a decisive win; for the benign winner, its restraint failing; for the sprint, the freeze broken; for the agreement, a covert project escaping; for the coercive path, the restrictor's correctors converted; for the design, the capture of custody. That failure is irreversible on every path. A race aimed at a decisive win selects toward it under Proposition 7.3's premises; verified containment can prevent it while a contest remains. The design contests it by separation, plural lineages and the tests D4 and D5. All paths retain the irreversible modes their actual mechanisms permit, with risks evaluated before and after any authorized construction. The design names independent remedies for other failures, but their late or failed execution has irreversible branches too. On every path, correction must precede the protected loss and retain an independent executor. None of the design's proposed reversals has yet been shown to work; its case rests on testing whether those routes improve timely recovery and bounded own risk (C4). Section 12.1 weighs that conditional case, and a reader who weighs the rows differently can reach another ranking.

### Where the criteria disagree

Under the protection standard the worst case includes a rising uncovered share on the continuation path, because its premises are named mechanisms the register does not contradict (EV5–EV7); it equally includes the capture of a common custodian on the cooperative path, because its correctors' certificates can fail (Proposition 4.10), and the other failure modes of the table above. Those possible failures do not by themselves show either probability bound above the tolerance. Neither path has yet been demonstrated to meet it, and applying the standard's third step needs estimates on both sides. An expected-value criterion that puts low probability on a rising gap and values speed highly can prefer continuation; a discounted criterion with utility linear in consumption prefers racing whenever the hazard it adds is below the growth it adds, and with curvature in utility it stops (S8). A well-corrected continuation can outperform a fragile cooperative arrangement; the analysis does not establish that every cooperative arrangement outperforms regulated competition.

## X10. The design in full

One configuration is developed far enough to expose its assumptions. It is one candidate among those Section 12.1 compares under one range of uncertainty: a polycentric complex of mutually recognized institutions, a verification agreement together with shared provision, regulated competition meeting the requirements of Section 9, an agreement of the type of Section 8.3(g) and the benign winner are others, and the coercive restriction of frontier inputs enters that comparison only under conditions of its own (Section 12.1). The text does not claim the configuration is superior; which design is best is part of crux C4. It is called here a *compact*: an arrangement for shared AI provision and correction, made of separately held functions rather than one institution. Each clause below aims to prevent a party under assessment from turning it against the assessment, and the hardest functions, execution and the fallback, carry the proposed financing, appointment and certificates that the others carry.

**What the functions carry.** The functions below give the carriers of the generative loop (Section 10.4) bodies, financing and ends. Each strand of Section 10.4 faces the questions the pathological loops answer: how it keeps obtaining resources, how it meets free riding and the pressure to expand, how it allows itself to be corrected, and how it avoids turning "protecting the whole" into a purpose no one can challenge. The columns of the table give proposed answers: a rule of financing for the first; formula contributions, the share cap and the levy's wide base for the second; a corrector for the third; and a condition that ends or reverts the body for the fourth.

**Correction through replacement.** The compact's continuity is not the protected outcome; the people's ability to observe, challenge and correct its consequences is. A replacement of an evaluator, provider, funding rule or decision system therefore needs a transition test: do independent evidence, access, practiced human capacity and effective intervention remain available before, during and after the change? Test N applies such perturbations to the whole correction chain. Appeals and proposals to amend a mistaken purpose are legitimate exercises of correction, not capture merely because they weaken an incumbent. A capture claim still needs the separately documented impairment of Definition 4.1. Formula funding and separated appointment are candidate mechanisms; Proposition S16.1 does not prove that either survives. The unresolved production and authority relations below determine whether the candidate can maintain that result's premises.

### Functions, held separately

Each function is held by a different body, separately financed and separately corrected, so that correction lies outside what it corrects. On a planet, "outside" means outside the function: a different source of funds, a different lineage, and certificates anyone can recompute from public data. Every body has a rule of financing, a rule of appointment, a corrector, and a condition that ends or reverts it, so that each can answer the withdrawal question of the guardian test (Remark 10.5), and every body whose findings or decisions have force carries a full inbound row-sum certificate (Proposition 4.10(2)), recomputed on a schedule from the data of the disclosure registry. A valid row bounds eventual capture under Proposition 4.10's premises; it does not certify current decisions. Before executive force is assigned or restored, independent assessment must also bound the body's captured share and inherited rule support against registered tolerances for the relevant residence time, using direct performance and intervention evidence as well as conversion data. Recertification in the table includes both tests. Three funds are fed by formula from the levy below: a *service fund* for provision, a *correction fund* for standard-setting, evaluation, disclosure, the chamber and the appeal, each by its own formula line, and an *execution fund* for execution and custody. The rules body is financed by treaty contributions outside the levy, so that the body that sets the formula receives nothing from it.

**Separation rule.** No body both revises the levy formula and appoints evaluators, and neither of those executes findings. Each has its own row-sum certificate. Supermajorities and the chamber's consent apply to the slow loops, amendment and the formula; the fast loop, restricting a specific capability, stays fast, with review after the fact, because every veto point adds delay, and the robustness margin of Proposition 5.12 depends on it.

**Monocentric by function, nested by option.** Each function is still held by one body. That is a separation of powers at one scale, monocentric function by function, and not polycentricity in the sense of overlapping jurisdictions at several scales that adjust to one another, which is a different candidate in Section 12.1; "many hands" applies inside evaluation only. The compact can instead be nested: each function held at national, regional and planetary levels, above a common floor of admission, custody and disclosure that no level may lower, with mutual recognition of certificates between levels, and with the fast loop placed at the level where the capability is held. Nesting adds redundancy of correction and local knowledge. It also multiplies the bodies whose certificates must hold and the seams through which a party under assessment can play one level against another, every level is a further conversion target, and the common floor must then be entrenched at every level. Nested governance of a planetary commons is itself an open problem (Stern 2011). The requirements of Section 9 constrain functions, not the number of centres; the choice is open.

**The chamber.** It is a corrector, and Section 4 applies to it. Its members are drawn by a rule no participant controls, they rotate, its inbound row sum is certified like any evaluator's, and its removal conditions are in the table. Node-by-node certificates replace an appeal to a trusted body with conditional requirements that many parties should be able to recompute from independent evidence. They do not end the implementation problem: standard-setting, disclosure, access and the authority to execute a correction can themselves be captured or withdrawn. Their continuation through amendment and replacement is part of C4 and Test N, not a consequence of the certificates. The chamber must also remain answerable to legitimate challenge under V3.

**Injection is the injector's violation.** Row sums count conversion into an evaluator from the party it assesses (Proposition 4.10(2); Proposition S10.2), so an assessed party could push an evaluator's row sum above one by sending it funds, staff or data, and so strip its findings of executive force; the same move repeated could manufacture the certificate failures that trigger dissolution. The compact proposes the following response, subject to independent attribution and appeal: Conversion into an evaluator from a party it assesses, outside the evaluator's disclosed channels, is a violation by that party; it does not suspend findings against that party; the assessment is reassigned to an evaluator of different lineage; and certificate failures attributable to a party under assessment count toward no end condition.

**Recusal, and the clock.** This candidate separates a conflict over an individual enforcement decision from participation in revising general purposes. A participant does not decide its own enforcement case. Opening an assessment does not by itself remove its standing to propose a general amendment, give evidence or seek review. Any additional recusal needs stated conflict grounds and a prompt ruling by an appeal panel independent of the initiating evaluator and assessed party, before the relevant amendment decision; if that review is unavailable in time, the disputed recusal cannot determine the general amendment. Assessments open on stated evidence and lapse at a registered deadline. Tolling for withheld access, data or personnel requires independent confirmation that the demand is necessary, proportionate and feasible, with a registered maximum interval and restoration of participation when the grounds lapse. Disputing a demand is not itself withholding. Repeated assessments cannot renew exclusion without new grounds and review. Urgent measures confined to the capability at issue may act on the evidence already available, with prompt review, without giving the assessed party a veto over its own case. These are proposed safeguards whose timely execution must be demonstrated; the appeals panel is itself subject to the correction and replacement requirements. Test N includes an evaluator using assessment and tolling to obstruct a legitimate reform.

**Held-out metrics.** A held-out metric published at registration is no longer held out: it becomes a target (Section 5.4). Metrics are committed in advance, as a salted, high-entropy test package under a stated commitment scheme, and revealed only at evaluation, so that anyone can check that the metric was fixed beforehand. The commitment does not keep the metric secret from those who hold it: that needs a separate secrecy protocol, tested on its own, with holders apart from the assessed, access recorded, leaks traced and tests rotated after each revelation.

**Disclosure.** Certificates are only as good as the data on conversion into correctors, and omission is the cheapest capture: a channel left unreported counts for nothing in a row sum. The disclosure registry keeps mandatory, audited records of funding, staff, data and access flowing into every corrector, from every party, the drafting parties included, with a sanction on the omitting party above its gain from omission (Lemma 3.3(3)). Without it the certificates of the design could not be recomputed by anyone, so it is the layer on which every other certificate rests, and it gets what every corrector gets: separate funding, an audit of a different lineage, and its own certificate.

**The levy formula is itself a prize.** The levy's *base* is wider than the activity the compact constrains: total compute, or treaty shares set by economic size, with a surcharge on frontier compute above a threshold. A base confined to frontier compute would shrink under exactly the stopping (E2) and slowing (E3) the compact exists to be able to impose, and would make its correctors stakeholders in the activity they constrain; the wider base does not shrink, while the surcharge still prices the frontier. The *correction fund* has two separately assessed obligations: to sustain institutional independence against conversion, and to provide hazard-specific coverage and timely response. The $d^\ast$ of Proposition 4.5 is a required rate of dissipation of institutional rule support; it is neither hazard coverage $U$ nor a budget. The production relations from money, human effort, AI assistance, access and authority to that rate, and separately to coverage and response time, remain open (S13). Provisional budgets must therefore be tested against both obligations, not calculated by identifying them. A body that receives none of the correction fund, such as the rules body, estimates conservative requirements from audited conversion data and direct intervention evidence; independent coverage tests also measure correlated misses and the catch rate of planted defects at fixed human hours. These tests constrain different interfaces and do not by themselves establish a dollar-to-$d$ conversion. Payers gain by hiding conversion and recipients by overstating requirements, so estimates and resource changes are independently audited. Formula funding does not rise mechanically with capability or perceived harm, and may fall by at most a registered rate per period, so that an apparent recovery does not immediately dismantle correction. The formula is fixed for a registered period; revision needs the supermajority and the chamber, and revisers are separated from beneficiaries and carry row-sum certificates, because by Proposition 4.3 the formula is a conversion target.

**Frontier contributions by formula.** In a common frontier, cooperation is an equilibrium only while the effort each cooperator contributes to the pool stays above a threshold that rises steeply with the value of winning (Proposition 10.1), and effort in the pool is a good shared by every member, which members choosing one by one under-supply (S10). Contributions to the common frontier are therefore paid by formula from the same base as the levy, not subscribed by each participant.

**Providers: voice without vote.** Providers earn revenue and contracts by usefulness and have no rights to appoint evaluators, set thresholds, exclude entrants or raise their cost of entry, or amend the rules. They have voice: notice and comment on every rule and standard, technical input to evaluation, and standing to contest a finding through the appeal, with their own conversion into every body disclosed. The experience of commons is that users excluded from revising their rules withhold local knowledge and compliance, and here the strongest of them also hold an exit option (Ostrom 1990); Section 4 instead identifies conditions under which appropriable influence over assessment pays (Proposition 4.3), not a universal implication from voting rights to capture. The compact makes a design judgment that this risk warrants voice without vote for providers in those governing functions. The preference needs comparative testing against arrangements with bounded voting rights and equally effective correction; its necessity is not established. The tension is real, and it does not end at the line: a voice that is heard more when its holder is larger is itself a channel of conversion, which disclosure and the row sums have to count. The chamber, the appeal and the open channels of comment are where the tension is managed, not dissolved.

**End conditions.** If coverage falls below the participation threshold, or a stated number of the functions' certificates fail for a stated period for reasons not attributable to any party under assessment, the compact dissolves into its separately held functions, each continuing under its own rule. Dissolution does not release a constructed decisive class or terminate its custody obligations. Protections against unilateral domination are entrenched, but legal continuity alone is not effective control. Every power that confers authority states what would end it (Remark 10.5), who then controls its assets and how that transfer is enforced.

**Before and after construction.** Before a decisive class is built, withdrawal may leave less physical overhang than a sprint, but diffused knowledge, installed compute and reconstruction capacity can still make a renewed race fast. After construction, that earlier fallback is unavailable. Before any construction is authorized, the candidate therefore requires a registered disposition plan for enrolled systems, copies, compute access and release keys; a separately funded successor custodian and independent executor, with access and authority installed in advance; and D4/D5 drills of the transfer against resistance by the strongest participant. On failure of readiness or participation, release and use authorizations revert to the registered limits, enrolled assets remain in custody or pass to the independently assessed successor, and the executor quarantines them or disables their enrolled frontier use under those limits. Rollback or destruction of inventoried copies is an option only where its safety and completion can be independently checked. Essential services move to nondecisive standby systems. If effective independent actuation cannot be demonstrated, this candidate has not established readiness to construct the class; it returns to the hard case of consent or the separately compared coercive fallback. No disposition erases scientific knowledge, proves that concealed copies do not exist, or prevents reconstruction from all unenrolled resources. Those residuals, the transfer's own risk and the successor's failure risk enter C4; the plan is a testable candidate, not a guarantee of returning to the state in which the class was never built.

**Against Ostrom's principles.** Boundaries (capability thresholds and hazard classes), monitoring and graduated sanctions are present. Proportionality of benefits and costs is partial: the largest payers have no vote, and strong types take part only for shares near their race payoffs (Proposition S10.3). Collective choice is answered by voice without vote. Conflict resolution is central, through one appeal, and neither cheap nor local, which nesting would answer. Recognition by an outside authority has no analogue at the scale of the planet; the host jurisdiction's first execution is the design's only link to the sovereignty of its participants. Nesting is the option above. The principles were drawn from commons that lasted at smaller scales (Ostrom 1990), and their transfer to a global commons is itself in question (Stern 2011).

**Other provisions.** A charter with three purposes: useful provision for all participants' populations; protection against O1 and O2; effective correction. Membership open to every state and provider above a capability threshold on non-discriminatory terms, participation made individually rational by access to shared services, a share of the dividend and capacity building (Proposition S10.3). Participation benefits, voting rights and protection against unilateral domination kept apart (Section 10.3). Baseline access to useful services with continuity guarantees; portable interfaces, escrow of systems providing essential services, and support for each participant to audit, operate and replace them. Narrow E1 first, for decisions whose delegation would lock with near certainty: human authorization and a minimum time for deliberation for decisions on the use of force and on nuclear command, drilled parallel capacity, an evidence channel independent of AI, doctrine open to mutual inspection, and drills measuring the full detection-and-response delay under human authorization against the breakout bound; broad E1 as the scope (Section 11.3). Admission constraints common to every system, before consequential deployment, internal deployment included, for divergences with common costs and a positive tax, with decision policies retained by performance only inside them; a zero tax removes the positive-tax rationale for a constraint only with evidence that deployed safeguards persist without it, since neutrality does not remove existing unsafe variants; for masked classes, admission by capability threshold with control protocols that do not assume the system's cooperation (Remark 6.8). Commonly funded, openly published alignment and control research. Pace: no building of the decisive class while its governance is unmet (E2); independently, hazardous growth and deployment stay within demonstrated coverage and control, with suspension or rollback where needed (E3), on every branch including preferred broad E1. Capability thresholds are set by evaluation, and input thresholds in effective compute tighten with algorithmic progress. Graduated remedies: notice; restriction of a specific capability; suspension of specific deployments; never suspension of essential services; emergency powers narrow, time-limited, reviewable and stating what would end them. Bounded competition continues inside: providers compete on usefulness, cost and reliability, and jurisdictions keep plural local purposes.

### Hard cases

**An adverse finding against a weaker participant.** Pending appeal, only the capability at issue may be restricted; essential services continue through alternative providers; the appellant sees the evidence under confidentiality, the panel decides within the deadline, and compensation follows if the finding is overturned. The power to suspend could become containment; the safeguards are the narrow scope of interim measures, the burden of evidence on the compact, and the appellant's standing.

**An adverse finding against the leading provider.** Suspending it could interrupt services for everyone, so formally equal procedures would be unequal in effect. The share cap, escrowed systems and funded standby capacity mean that restricting the leader's hazardous capability need not stop services. The author's book gives the rule for this case: the greater the power, the stricter the regulation it must receive, because it can shape everyone's conditions (力愈大者，受调当愈严，以其能塑众人之境也) [corpus: shengsheng, 2026-07-14 第十四章]. The compact's version is that the leader's row in every certificate is weighted by its share, since the return to influence rises with the square of share (Proposition 4.3). Execution falls first to the leader's host jurisdiction. If that fails, what remains is the coercive restriction of inputs to the decisive class, the same form whose endorsement Section 8.3 withholds. The design does not settle it: it enters only as the candidate of Section 12.1, under the conditions stated there, with its escalation risk counted in $\Theta$, and the theory does not derive that its net effect is positive. Where neither consent nor such a restriction is available, the case is the hard case of Section 8.3. This is the hardest case, and the design does not dissolve it. It reduces the asymmetry of dependence that would otherwise make enforcement against the strongest impossible, and it gives the body that would execute a restriction a financing, an appointment and a certificate that do not run through the party restricted.

**A coalition of insiders.** Rival elites agree on mutual secrecy and immunity while weakening public correction. Four channels answer to people outside the coalition: the chamber, protected reporting, evaluators funded by formula, and publication of findings by default after a fixed delay. The disclosure registry makes the coalition's own conversion visible, and omission is the violation.

**An unjustified restriction.** The compact is used to contain a developing participant. It has standing to challenge, the burden is on the compact to show evidence of hazard, restrictions carry sunsets, and the compact has obligations to build capacity.

**Succession and withdrawal.** A change of leadership triggers review, not suspension. A withdrawal by a large participant makes the constraints borne by the rest unilateral and can trigger further exits; the participation threshold and the conditions of market access are what keep coverage above it (Section 9, "coverage can unravel"). Before construction, shared benefits may lower the value of winning and the absent class may lower physical overhang, without proving that reconstruction is distant. After construction, withdrawal activates the disposition and independent execution plan above; separately held functions alone do not neutralize built capability. If custody cannot be maintained or disposition verified, the fallback includes the remaining capability and knowledge overhang, and no lower hazard than the compared race has been established.

**Loss of an essential service.** Redundancy, escrow and standby capacity carry the service.

### Federations, at both orders

Federations meet both orders of Section 10.2 at once. Competition among member units for capital and residents can become a race to the bottom, the pressure of winning among members, and the strongest members can capture the centre, the selection of the selectors. At the first order, common minimum standards bind every member, and fiscal equalization gives members capacity they do not have to win, both widening the effective self of members that value their absolute payoff and supplying the $k_0$ of the loop gain; for members that act strategically, pooling raises the working self-model rather than only slowing the race (Proposition 10.2(1)). At the second order, equal representation of small members and supermajorities for constitutional change keep the centre from being bought by the largest, a precedent for voting that does not follow compute. A global minimum tax on corporate profits is a recent instance of one rule for all, and of how coverage unravels: the largest participant's groups were exempted from two of its rules in recognition of that participant's own minimum-tax rules (EVIDENCE EV18), the coverage problem of Section 9's participation threshold arising at the participant whose coverage matters most. The same federal form can select for winning or for contribution; what decides is what it rewards.

### Narrow E1 and the value of commitment

Deterrence theory holds that removing human discretion from retaliation makes a threat credible: a commitment that cannot be revoked is worth more than one that can (Schelling 1960). Its stronger form is that semi-automated retaliation could relieve the pressure to launch on warning. Five answers follow from the core.
1. The commitment value comes from removing the possibility of human overrule, which is the lock of Lemma 6.3 and, in its domain, a loss of O2.
2. Survivable, human-controlled forces relieve the pressure to launch on warning without removing judgment.
3. An automated commitment cannot tell a false alarm from an attack. Several false alarms of early-warning systems are documented. On 26 September 1983 a satellite early-warning system reported launches that were sunlight reflected from high clouds, and the duty officer judged the alarm false and did not report an attack (Podvig 2002; Hoffman 2009); the register records the event and does not claim that a report would have launched retaliation (EV18).
4. Automation adds a common risk, escalation at machine speed on a false alarm, that relative selection does not see (Lemma 3.3), so the contest under-prices it.
5. Among actors who price in the lock, keeping AI out of these loops is a strict equilibrium, and doing so verifiably is a costly signal (Proposition 11.1).

The strict equilibrium of Proposition 6.4(1) needs a certain lock, $q_L=1$, and it survives while the lock fails with a probability below the bound of Remark S6.1. The narrow domain meets that bound by construction: a decision irreversible within the time for deliberation cannot be overruled in time once it is delegated. Keeping detection automated does not by itself preserve the speed of answering a breakout: any human authorization required for an irreversible response enters C7's total latency and the bound of Remark 5.5. In tactical domains, where the speed advantage is large and the lock uncertain, delegation can be an equilibrium (Proposition 6.4(2), (3)), and bounded, reversible defensive automation, such as response in cyber defence within stated limits, falls under the test of O2 of Section 6.6, not under E1.

### Feasibility

Each actor can take part of E1 alone, and taken verifiably it is the signal of Proposition 11.1. E2 and E3 are the most verifiable among rivals, because chips, fabs, datacentres and compute can be observed. Algorithmic progress erodes thresholds stated in compute: with pre-training efficiency doubling about every eight months (90% interval about two to twenty-two months, on data to 2023), a nominal cap must tighten roughly two and a half to three times a year at the central estimate, and from about 1.5 to about 64 times a year across the interval (EV9), a rate condition verification must meet. Hardware-enabled mechanisms for verifying compute use are proposed and prototyped, not deployed (EV17). E1 is the hardest to verify, since it concerns software and internal procedure, and the most self-enforcing among actors who price in the lock, which is why it can begin as a norm: declared doctrine with inspected exercises.

Staying out of the contest alone while others race can be worse than joining. In the survival accounting of Proposition S8.1, take two actors, no hazard but the loss of the ability to overrule and the rival's decisive advantage, with $q_{\rm I}=p_{\rm dec}=1-\varepsilon$, and a decisive winner that extends its dominance, $p_{\rm ext}=1$. Joining gives $\Phi^P=\varepsilon\big(1-(1-\varepsilon)/2\big)=\varepsilon(1+\varepsilon)/2$. Staying out gives $\Phi^S=1-p^S_{\rm dec}$: that is $\varepsilon$, better than joining, if staying out leaves the rival's decisive chance where it was, and $\varepsilon^2$, worse than joining for every $\varepsilon<1$, if it raises that chance to $1-\varepsilon^2$ [thm, by substitution; num: TR]. That is why E1 must be held in common and begins with the decision loops in which the lock makes it an equilibrium.

The Cold War ended largely by the internal collapse of one side, accompanied by negotiated reductions in intermediate forces, conventional forces and strategic arms, and its hazards fell with learning (Nye 1987), the case in which residual risk becomes summable. Rivals survived where nature or balancing supplied saturation, and gave up capabilities by agreement when reciprocal limits made the extra advantage worth less. Balancing by others is a saturation mechanism that does not depend on the technology, and whether it operates for AI is part of crux C5. Whether advantage in AI saturates is not known (Section 7.3), so Section 11.5 states the consequence conditionally: if it is unsaturated, as the racers' own premise holds, none of these precedents supplies the saturation, and safety must then come from preventing the decisive position through changed incentives, durable containment or another arrangement meeting the requirements before advantage becomes decisive. The text prefers changing what winning confers; the precedents do not establish that this is the only feasible route.

### Crisis windows and standing

Arrangements of this kind are often adopted when a crisis opens a window, not through negotiation among the strong at a time of their choosing (Gunderson and Holling 2002; Olsson et al. 2006). A design prepared in advance, with its functions and protections worked out and its activation committed, is what a crisis can adopt; unprepared responses tend to strengthen the contest, through emergency powers without sunsets and measures aimed at rivals. A measure adopted in a crisis without a stated end condition runs the ratchet of Section 11.4 backward: held past its need, it becomes the lock-in the next reform must repay. Smaller jurisdictions, users and people who depend on services controlled by others need standing on every track, because they cannot leave the shared exposure; the chamber of X10 and the service guarantees are how they get it.
