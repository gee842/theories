## X8. The alternatives in full

Each alternative is given its best case on its merits, how it would be installed, what remains, what it changes, and what evidence would show that it suffices. Section 8.3 of the core states each in one paragraph; the formal results are in the core and S8. The chapter ends with the tables Section 12.1 compares on: the policies now in force, profiled in advance; the main residual risk of each path in the comparison of Section 8.4; the durability of every arrangement by its own documents; and the failure modes of every path, with whether each can be reversed. One evidence standard holds in every row, the paper's own design included.

The constructive target of Section 10.4 applies equally to every alternative: can the arrangement retain effective correction through changes in capability, leadership, providers and rules? A favourable test of today's systems does not establish that persistence; neither does naming a common custodian or independent regulator. The bounded result {{jointcorrection}} holds only while its incoming-gain and effective-liability bounds remain in force. Which design can maintain such conditions is C4 and Test N, assessed through actual access, authority and interventions. A competing arrangement that meets the target counts in its favour, even if it preserves competitive motives; the target does not establish the author's preferred arrangement as uniquely sufficient.

### Technical alignment and AI-assisted correction

**Best case.** The strongest form is not "technical alignment alone". Control is scoped to early transformative systems and used, under control, to accelerate alignment and oversight research while the window lasts (Greenblatt, Shlegeris, Sachan and Roger 2024): control protocols tested against red teams raise safety at a given level of usefulness in the settings studied. Weak supervisors can elicit part of a stronger model's capability (Burns et al. 2023); in auditing games, teams with access to training data found a planted hidden objective that a team with black-box access alone did not, which supports mandated access (Marks et al. 2025). Oversight can be built with the right topology, redundantly or adversarially by debate ({{topology}}), and formal methods certify invariants without predicting trajectories. AI may cheapen independent correction as fast as control, so that AI-assisted correction keeps pace ({{corrrate}}), and correction people cannot follow can still lower residual risk ({{opaquecorr}}). Research can drive the alignment tax below zero, class by class, until the safe variant is also the winning one; once published it is common to all, so the safeguards it makes profitable spread without coordination, while those it brings to a tax of exactly zero are neutral under selection. At zero, withdrawing the common constraint additionally needs evidence that the safeguard persists without it; existing unsafe variants need not disappear (Section 6.4; {{nested}}). Its proponents' own plans rank a strong international agreement with a long slowdown first, and treat technical work as what one does when that is unavailable.

**What remains.** Principal alignment does not change the game among principals ({{faithful}}). The window lasts only as long as the race allows, and the race shortens it. Systems are retained for performance ({{retain}}), and progress that lowers the hazard per unit of racing or delegation is partly absorbed by more of both ({{homeostasis}}). Redundant oversight is capped by common causes, which selection produces, and certified row sums are a sufficient certificate, not a proof of independent errors ({{topology}}). AI-assisted correction is only as independent as its funding, access, appointment and lineage. One part of the tax no technique removes: where countering a divergence means waiting for evidence that arrives after a manifestation window, its cost is time, which relative selection with decisive advantage always prices ({{tempo}}). For masked divergences, which change under evaluation or appear only in some contexts, admission deters evasion only while detection times the enforceable sanction covers the tax. With a fixed positive tax and a bounded sanction, detection tending to zero eventually defeats deterrence; if the tax falls faster, deterrence can persist (C9 and C12 together). A detector's failure to verify removal is a limit of assurance, not proof that removal or low realized hazard is impossible. Under the protection standard, the recommendation where independent control cannot establish safety is a common capability bound, supported by auditor access to weights, activations and training data; beyond it, the independent coverage and control condition of E3 applies even under broad E1, with suspension or rollback where needed, while E2 separately governs the decisive class ({{masked}}; EV6). Which divergences are tolerated is set at the outermost loop ({{nested}}).

**What it changes.** Tempo, observation and residual harm per unit, and the alignment tax where the research that lowers it is published. It becomes part of a change of selection, G3 and G7, when held by correctors separated from competitive success, under constraints common to all. **What would show it suffices:** the safety of control protocols at a fixed audit budget not falling across generations; catch rates of planted defects at fixed human hours not falling as capability rises, controlled for evaluation awareness (C3a); auditors with fixed access still finding planted and emergent hidden objectives as capability rises (C12); independent evaluators keeping certificates while depending on the evaluated for access; the tax measured negative, or zero with evidence that the safeguard persists after the constraint is withdrawn, class by class (C9); residual risk per hazard class meeting its registered cumulative-risk tolerance, with a summable upper bound where unbounded survival is claimed (C1). A falling trend alone is insufficient.

### Regulated competition with strong correction

**Best case.** Competition in products and services continues; liability and licensing make consequences return; evaluation access is mandated and thresholds fixed in advance; and a capability-race model supplies a conditional pacing equilibrium without an agreement (Fudenberg and Koh 2026; comparison below). There are precedents of durable safety with unchanged commercial motives: an industry that knows one accident can end it for all, as civil nuclear power came to after a major accident; and aviation, with independent accident investigation, liability and insurance pricing. This is the bounded competition a cooperative order can contain (V5).

**Installation.** Each jurisdiction regulates its own developers; coverage must reach every capable actor, since the race is set by the most race-inclined actor ({{join}}); the rule-makers are themselves in a contest of standing, so no one of them imposes the liability alone ({{nounilateral}}); rule-making is subject to Section 4.

**What remains.** Liability cannot exceed the assets of the liable (Shavell 1986): for catastrophic and common harms that is the point at which a common cost cannot be turned into a private one, which is why liability alone does not settle the alignment tax. Regulation in one market leaves the load elsewhere. Two developers' frameworks condition commitments on rivals, one of them stating that it cannot commit to mitigations unilaterally because of a collective action problem while keeping a discretion to pause; one unreviewed study codes 77% of the changes it traced, in twelve pairs of consecutive framework versions, as weakening (EV2). Public evaluation bodies have shifted their mandates toward industry contact under voluntary access (EV3). The precedents had no decisive prize.

**The pacing equilibrium, compared with E3.** Fudenberg and Koh (2026, Theorem 1(c)) show that sufficiently high effective hazard sustains their pacing profile as a Markov perfect equilibrium and excludes their everywhere-racing profile. They do not establish uniqueness. Their pacing profile can itself race forever from sufficiently unsafe initial states; safe pacing is an equilibrium outcome from a tie on safety. Their private-capability extension permits pacing at every monitoring lag when effective hazard is high enough. This hazard is defined by racing payoffs, not by perceived or internalized harm (EV11; full paper checked). The result supports the possibility of E3 under competition; the correspondence with this paper's mechanisms remains to be established.
- *The effective hazard.* In this paper's translation, which is heuristic and not the source's definition, it falls with what the objective an actor acts on counts and with what it sees. Relative accounting weighs a shared hazard by $1+\theta$ and drops it at $\theta=-1$ ({{twochan}}), and compounding narrows the working objective as the discounted loop gain rises ({{standprice}}), so the weight on shared harm falls where the gain rises; whether the source model's effective hazard falls requires a mapping of payoffs. Where harm manifests late, or is masked, the hazard seen is below the real one by the gap on which the silent route runs ({{slowroute}}): a path can lie in the pacing region of the real parameters while its participants, seeing less, compute the racing region; the translation would have to be checked by mapping one model's payoffs into the other's.
- *Transparency* fails where the conditions of winning predict that it fails: the quiet deviation is the one that binds enforcement ({{enforce}}), captured evaluation masks the harm ({{masking}}), masked divergences hide under evaluation ({{masked}}), and internal deployment and the open–closed gap hide capability (EV5, EV6). Monitoring delay alone does not defeat pacing, since the source's lag extension covers it; what falls outside its scope is a threat that arises before deployment and a breakout that is absorbing.
- *What the comparison adds.* Where outrunning the frontier can yield a decisive position, the prize ends the game and the enforcement condition, not the hazard, decides ({{enforce}}). Speed can also be bought outside the frontier by delegation (Section 6.3), and the frontier's position is measured by someone whom success can buy ({{influence}}).

G1, consequences returned, and G6, shared measurement, are candidate ways to support pacing in this paper's account. Their necessity does not follow from the source theorem, and transparency is not required in every extension. The empirical questions are whether the present parameters support a pacing equilibrium, whether it is selected, and whether the current state lies on a path that reaches safety, class by class. The measurements that answer it are those of Section 7.3 and the cruxes of Section 12.3: late-manifesting hazard classes and their evidence lags (C1), the open–closed gap and internal deployment (C5), and the conversion data of T4 (C2). Where a class reaches and sustains safe pacing under present incentives, E3 for that class needs no additional agreement; equilibrium existence by itself does not establish that outcome. Otherwise the arrangements of Section 11.3 must be assessed for whether they make the safe path reachable and durable. The correspondence remains this paper's reading, to be checked against the full paper's payoffs.

**What it changes.** This depends on content. Regulation that returns consequences and separates evaluators from success, across every capable actor, is G1 and G3 for all, changes the basin, and is the change this paper argues for, whatever it is called. Regulation that constrains conduct while success can still buy the rule-makers is predicted to erode as stakes and speed grow. **What would show it suffices:** competitor clauses not invoked; thresholds held when rivals advance; the mandates of evaluation bodies widening as the stakes rise; findings executed against leaders (C2); effective liability above the private advantage of load for every capable carrier.

### Leadership and a safety margin

**Best case.** A leader spends part of its lead on safety: it slows, evaluates and hardens before the follower catches up. A large lead can discourage the follower (Harris and Vickers 1987; Konrad and Kovenock 2009); a laggard far behind under moderate hostility does not gamble on risky technology (Stafford, Trager and Dafoe 2022); and a developer able to commit first to a slightly slower speed gains, because rivals slow too, a local result that does not depend on holding a lead (Bueno de Mesquita, Dziuda and Polborn 2026). One developer's framework includes such a "leading" scenario (EV2).

**What remains.** {{lead}}: leads diffuse even when the follower does not accelerate; the margin must buy coverage, and buys nothing for hazard classes whose evidence lags exceed it; it is judged inside the contest; and a leader seeing its window close has a motive to strike first. Spending the lead returns the contest toward parity, where effort is highest in contests of step-by-step innovation (Aghion, Harris, Howitt and Vickers 2001) and, where a lead discourages the follower, the price of standing peaks in the contests computed (Section 7.2). **What it changes.** Tempo for a time; it keeps the objective of winning. Its best case, a first commitment to slow that rivals answer by slowing, is a step toward reciprocal restraint, the first stage of a transition. **What would show it suffices:** leads not spent on speed; rivals slowing in response; margins long relative to the evidence lags of the hazard classes.

### The benign winner: win, bind, share

**Best case.** The strongest form is that a winner wins, binds itself and shares. After great wars, victors have bound themselves through institutions to lower others' fear and their own costs of enforcement (Ikenberry 2001); a dominant power can supply public goods a field of equals would not (Kindleberger 1973; Gilpin 1981), and unipolarity can be stable (Wohlforth 1999); an encompassing ruler internalizes the shared conditions (Olson 1993). In the model of Section 3.5 a single developer restrains whenever restraint is worth more to the field than it costs, so the failure lies in the race, not in the existence of a winner. The holder of the first nuclear monopoly, through the period of superiority to 1954, did not extend its dominance to everything it could reach (X11). In AI the strategy has been proposed, in its authors' terms, as humanitarian and defensive: a coalition seeks a clear advantage, including military superiority and the denial of key inputs such as chips and semiconductor equipment to adversaries, and then offers its benefits to others in exchange for their support, so that adversaries give up competing (EV1). It persuades by promising both security and benefits; both rest on the winner's restraint after the fact, which is what the commitment tests examine. By this paper's own results the form is strongest where the winner still needs the governed: a winner whose self-model is accurate toward what can be divided, $\theta=\kappa_W$, gives them $\kappa_W/(1+\kappa_W)$ under logarithmic utility ({{wingives}}; {{boundary}}(2)). Most strong international institutions were built by victors; that is the record this path appeals to, and the record a transition among rivals near parity has to answer (X11).

**What remains.** The commitment tests. After a decisive win what the others receive is set by the winner's objective alone, and a promise to share is worth breaking ({{wingives}}). What the race cannot verify beforehand is $\kappa_W$ after victory: a winner, like any institution, stays answerable because it depends on the labour, consent and judgment of the people it governs, and AI can substitute for each (Kulveit et al. 2025). The historical cases had advantages that were not decisive, and their restraint rested on institutions others could check and on that dependence (Acemoglu and Robinson 2012); whether AI removes it is crux C13. Where conduct before victory and after it go together, the race shifts winners toward those that extend dominance ({{raceselects}}). And the winner is itself a field of agencies, firms, laboratories and systems, whose restraint after victory depends on whether its own correctors can be bought by its successes; an AI aligned to the winner is not thereby aligned to the people it governs (Section 6.3).

**What it changes.** Nothing in selection before victory; after victory, everything depends on the winner's internal field. **What would show it suffices**, before victory: binding limits on what victory would confer; benefits shared as they arise rather than promised; the coalition's own correctors, constitutional correctors included, certified with row sums below one, with succession rules that survive changes of leadership (Test I); and a measured dependence of the coalition on the people it would govern that AI does not erode (C13). A winner that adopts these has changed what it selects for.

### A finite sprint, then restraint

**Best case.** An actor sprints to a decisive lead, then freezes or rolls back its own hazardous capability and governs at the tempo of understanding. Under the premises of {{freeze}}, this gives positive survival on the growth channel. Decisive contests can be won with a modest edge when $r$ is large, so the sprint need not run far beyond understanding. The post-sprint choice must be specified: relinquishing superiority is different from preserving it by stopping others.

**Relinquishment after the sprint.** The winner freezes or rolls back itself, allows others to catch up and accepts that its advantage ceases to be decisive. Permanent superiority is not required by its survival criterion. This branch imposes no freeze on rivals. It must still answer three objections: exposure and preventive incentives during the sprint ({{preventattack}}; C1, C5); whether the announced self-restraint survives changes in the winner's leadership and internal correctors (C4); and whether the contest that returns as rivals catch up stays within the enforcement, coverage and human-authority requirements. Freezing one actor's hazardous variety does not bound the hazards all other actors produce under (shared failure). Those conditions, not unequal incidence of an imposed stop, decide this branch.

**Permanent monopoly after the sprint.** The winner instead keeps its decisive capability and stops rivals from acquiring it. The stop then has unequal incidence: the holder retains what the others may not build. It is evaded wherever a type's gain from evasion exceeds its expected sanction ({{blind}}(2), (3)); the required sanction is supplied by the holder. The holder is also a large conversion target, by the square-of-share return to influence and the high moment of cultivated conversion ({{influence}}; {{corrrate}}(4)). Where maintaining the monopoly removes others' consequential decision authority, V2 and V3 reject that loss. These objections apply to this continuation, not automatically to relinquishment.

**Against common custody.** E2 and common custody also constrain access to the decisive class, and a coalition that stops outsiders need not end their O2 (the non-proliferation row of X11). Each branch is therefore compared by incidence, current control and actual correction, with the same evidence standard:

| | Sprint, then relinquish | Sprint, then preserve monopoly | Common custody (Section 10.5; X10) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who bears the stop | The sprinter freezes or rolls back itself; rivals may catch up | Everyone except the holder is barred from acquiring what it keeps | Everyone's unilateral use, custodians included, is constrained by design; equal incidence in effect is unmeasured |
| D4 Who corrects the holder | Initially its own correctors; an independent transfer or constraint can be added but must be specified | The holder's own correctors, a large conversion target | Separately held functions and independently appointed correctors by design; certificates not yet measured (C4) |
| D5 When the strongest violates | Before relinquishment is effective, an unkept promise has no external sanction; afterward, the renewed contest's actual enforcement matters | No independent party can compel a decisive holder | Dependence caps, standby and installed custody controls where available; host-jurisdiction execution, then the compared-not-settled coercive fallback; the hard case remains |
| What ends it | Verified rollback or erosion of superiority; continued coverage and restraint still need support | The holder's choice unless an independent constraint is installed | Registered release and withdrawal procedures, distinct before and after construction; built capability and knowledge prevent assuming a return to the pre-build state |

The comparison does not establish that custody is easier to enforce than a credible relinquishment. Its proposed advantage over monopoly is incidence that includes its members and correctors independent of the holder; those are claims to test, not consequences of the word “common.”

**What it changes.** Relinquishment changes the winner's post-sprint trajectory and allows the contest to return; monopoly preserves the lead through a restriction on others. Neither erases the sprint's exposure. **What would show it suffices:** measured exposure within tolerance; independently verifiable self-restraint, transfer or restraint of the holder; and enforcement and coverage that remain effective after the chosen continuation.

### Deterrence and prevention

Prevention can be survival-preferred for the preventer ({{preventown}}). The objections are three, and none is a change of criterion. *Normative:* it ends or endangers others' O1 and O2. *Epistemic:* aimed at a rival believed certain to race, it destroys, when the rival is in fact survival-first, a game in which restraint was possible, and can lead the rival to act as the type it was feared to be (the spiral model; Jervis 1976; Kydd 1997), while shifts that cannot be observed raise the chance of mistaken prevention (Debs and Monteiro 2014). *Technical:* detecting unobservable progress and striking at machine speed brings the delegation of Section 6.3.

Deterrence by threatened sabotage aims to make a decisive sprint not worth attempting. *Best case:* large datacentres can be observed and compute thresholds made salient (Hendrycks, Schmidt and Wang 2025). *What remains:* red lines that are unclear and hard to monitor, and doubtful credibility of the threat of sabotage, make deterrence of this kind unstable unless they are refined (Abecassis 2025); where unsanctioned gains grow without bound relative to fixed cooperative and punishment values, required timely detection approaches one ({{verify}}); among nuclear-armed rivals credibility is the first problem, and under survival-first comparisons it can reverse; pushing AI into military capability strengthens the pressure to delegate ({{delegate}}); verification that supports deterrence can also supply targets (Coe and Vaynman 2020); and algorithmic progress erodes thresholds stated in compute (EV9). *What it changes:* it acts on $p_d$ by threat, and its fallback is war or race.

### The hard case

Let a survival-first actor hold the prior $\mu$ that a capable rival holds an unbounded relative objective. When $\mu$ is low, its best course is to propose verifiable limits, and to take narrow E1 unilaterally and verifiably in its own strategic loops, the costly signal of {{signal}}. When $\mu$ is high, it compares denial, a coalition that stops the decisive class, and prevention. Coverage of every capable actor then comes from consent or from coercion. Denying the inputs to the decisive class is the coercive form of a stop; it raises the weight on winning and the trust threshold, and if it escalates it falls under {{preventown}}. The theory does not derive that its net effect is positive and does not endorse it. Section 12.1 admits the coercive restriction of frontier inputs to its comparison only under three conditions of its own, and even there compares it rather than recommends it. If neither consent nor a coercion of positive net effect is available, no actor has a secure position: that is the hard case, stated as such. Insuring against falling behind is not an exit ({{insure}}): it accepts the frame of the contest, and against a winner of the logic Section 3.8 describes, falling behind cannot be made survivable.

### A verified agreement over a contest that remains

**Best case.** Rivals agree to slow and to delay decisive capability, with compute declarations, supply-chain accounting, recomputation of workloads, research transparency, and mutually assured destruction of compute if the agreement dissolves; it works without trust and bounds the largest covert project that could escape detection. One detailed plan of this kind, a deal among leading powers to delay superintelligence to about 2040 (Larsen et al. 2026; EV10), adds inference-only retrofits of datacentres and hardened reserves of about two per cent of pre-deal compute, and aims to end winner-take-all dynamics through many frontier developers, transparency, broad diffusion and a citizens' dividend. Its own scenario estimates do not show near-certain dissolution: one author's judgment puts decline in years five to ten at about 4% a year, half dissolution and half impairment, and cumulative decline over ten years at about 48% (EV10). Its handoff to AI systems is a conditional decision that weighs further alignment research against other risks, and it keeps an option to extend the pause. The plan is one instance of a position, keep the contest, slow it and stay safe; it is cited so that the tests can be applied to documents rather than to a type, and the answer below is to the position, not to the plan's figures.

**What remains.** Verification is required exactly to the degree that the value of winning exceeds the value of cooperating; its threshold is the deterrence condition of the inspection game (Avenhaus, von Stengel and Zamir 2002), the enforcement condition seen from verification ({{detectlink}}); and the net gain from exit can grow with the capability held in check ({{verify}}; {{overhang}}). The bound on covert projects holds only while the coalition's compute advantage keeps pace with the diffusion of algorithmic efficiency, which is not derived (EV9).

**What it changes.** It slows and caps, acting on tempo and decisiveness, and keeps relative standing in its enforcement, through proportional reserves and a coalition that must stay ahead of covert projects; the conversion of success into control over correction is addressed only through transparency. An agreement that preserves relative positions preserves the relative term of every payoff ({{overhang}}(3)); that term alone does not establish a positive net gain from exit or defeat an effective sanction. The agreement is a bridge if its bound on covert capability or its effective sanction cannot keep pace with deviations; it can be a durable endpoint if every deviation remains within {{eq:enforceeq}} and the other applicable requirements, including independent correction and coverage, remain met. G2 and G5 can reduce the burden on enforcement without being necessary for every endpoint. Common custody faces the same verification limits; any claimed advantage in its fallback or in the value of winning must be shown across its allowed lifecycle, including withdrawal after authorized construction (X10).

### Normal technology and resilience

**Best case.** AI is a normal technology: its impacts materialize not as capabilities improve but as they are diffused through the economy, at the pace of institutional adoption, so the danger to govern is that of a powerful general-purpose technology, and policy should build resilience and reject nonproliferation, which lowers it (Narayanan and Kapoor 2025). In the history of general-purpose technologies, great-power advantage has come from the capacity to diffuse them, not from a lead at the frontier (Ding 2024). On this reading no lead at the frontier is decisive in itself, and custody of a decisive class is a concentration of power answering a danger that does not arise.

**What remains.** The growth channel of Section 5.3 binds at the pace of deployment, not of the frontier: where adoption is slow enough for contestable coverage to keep up, (coverage lags) fails for that class, and where it is not, the rate of deployment is the rate to compare with coverage; both are measured class by class (C1). Whether any advantage is decisive is C5. The diagnosis registers both, and where both are measured favourable the activation of tier 2 lapses (THEORY §12.3) while the requirements on correction, coverage and measurement stand. The concentration that custody risks is answered by its form, separately held functions certified by row sums, and by the guardian test ({{twoorders}}; {{guardian}}). **What it changes.** Its prescriptions, resilience, diffusion of what is not decisive and shared measurement, are G1, G3 and G6, and diffusion is a lever of Part I in every channel ({{chansign}}); it does not change the selection of those who would lead where leads do compound. **What would show it suffices:** contestable coverage keeping pace with deployment class by class (C1), and positive evidence that balancing prevents decisive leads on the relevant capability and response measures, with uncertainty over a registered horizon (C5). An unmeasured decisive lead is not evidence that none exists.

Read across paths, the table has one failure in common: one party comes to hold decisive capability. On continuation it is a decisive win; for the benign winner, its restraint failing; for the sprint, the freeze broken; for the agreement, a covert project escaping; for the coercive path, the restrictor's correctors converted; for the design, the capture of custody. That failure is irreversible on every path. A race aimed at a decisive win selects toward it under {{raceselects}}'s premises; verified containment can prevent it while a contest remains. The design contests it by separation, plural lineages and the tests D4 and D5. All paths retain the irreversible modes their actual mechanisms permit, with risks evaluated before and after any authorized construction. The design names independent remedies for other failures, but their late or failed execution has irreversible branches too. On every path, correction must precede the protected loss and retain an independent executor. None of the design's proposed reversals has yet been shown to work; its case rests on testing whether those routes improve timely recovery and bounded own risk (C4). Section 12.1 weighs that conditional case, and a reader who weighs the rows differently can reach another ranking.

### Where the criteria disagree

Under the protection standard the worst case includes a rising uncovered share on the continuation path, because its premises are named mechanisms the register does not contradict (EV5–EV7); it equally includes the capture of a common custodian on the cooperative path, because its correctors' certificates can fail ({{certificate}}), and the other failure modes of the table above. Those possible failures do not by themselves show either probability bound above the tolerance. Neither path has yet been demonstrated to meet it, and applying the standard's third step needs estimates on both sides. An expected-value criterion that puts low probability on a rising gap and values speed highly can prefer continuation; a discounted criterion with utility linear in consumption prefers racing whenever the hazard it adds is below the growth it adds, and with curvature in utility it stops (S8). A well-corrected continuation can outperform a fragile cooperative arrangement; the analysis does not establish that every cooperative arrangement outperforms regulated competition.
