## Shape

2. **The game.**
   - Winning buys the means to win again.
   - The game selects ways of deciding.
   - The error that wins.
   - "The biggest, then bigger".
   - Two endgames, and why the logic seems to work (slack).
3. **Why AI is the hinge.**
   - AI as lever or as player.
   - The training ground is the whole developer.
   - Alignment at three scales.
   - Complexity compounds and understanding does not.
   - *There is no safe way to win.*
4. **Why careful people can't see the exit.**
   - Moloch and Leviathan as one frame.
   - Safety proposals read across the boundary.
   - The trap told from inside: not being beaten is not winning.
5. **Another way to see.** Relations first, with heart and reason as one dynamic; acceleration needs a direction; a state, not a destiny.
6. **What follows.** An order of moves; trust before verification; the close.

## Script

### 1. The Game of Winning  (`cover`)

[0:00, 20 s] Thank you. I want to talk about a game we are all already playing, and about a technology we are about to build inside it. I'll start with about a minute and a half of a film.

### 2. The Game of Winning  (`film`)

[0:20, 120 s] Play the film from the card “One star is not enough” to the end card, about 75 seconds. Then: That film is not a forecast. It is a conditional. It shows where one logic goes if nothing in it changes. The logic is old, and you will recognise it. What is new is that we are about to give it the most powerful tool it has ever had. I start from mechanism because a good theory predicts: if we can see where a game leads, we don't have to play it to the end to find out. And the outcome I'll describe is in nobody's interest, including the people who look like they are winning. So in the next fifteen minutes: the mechanism, why AI is the hinge, why careful people can't see the exit, and what follows.

### 3. What this talk does (hidden)  (`agenda`)

[Hidden in the 16-minute version; unhide for the 17-minute version, 35 s] Why start from mechanism? Because a good theory predicts. If we can see where a game leads, we don't have to play it to the end to find out. My aim is not to win an argument. It is to make the understanding shared, because the outcome I'm going to describe is in nobody's interest, including the people who look like they are winning.

### 4. Winning buys the means to win again  (`loop`)

[2:20, 50 s] Here is the core. In many games, winning buys the means to win again: money, standing, the power to set the rules for the next round. That is compounding. Add selection: whatever wins rounds gets copied, by imitation, by investment, by promotion. Run that long enough and the game does not just pick winners. It reshapes the players. The ones who stay are the ones who play this way, and their success then sets what everyone else considers reasonable. History is written by the winners, and so, in part, is the standard of what makes sense.

### 5. The game selects ways of deciding  (`minds`)

[3:10, 55 s] What gets selected is not only firms or states. It is ways of deciding. Most traditions teach that education should form character: being trustworthy, keeping promises, caring about the people around you. Then the power system takes over. It rewards whatever wins the next round, and that reward selects. A contest that forces everyone to run faster favours decisions that are fast and narrow, and deliberating about what an act does to others is slow. My hypothesis is that the faster power turns over, the stronger this selection becomes. Formation sets who we are. Selection decides who rises. Where positions turn over quickly, selection wins.

### 6. The error that wins  (`boundary`)

[4:05, 50 s] Here is what the selected mind looks like. It draws a boundary around one value and its holder: my share, my lead. Everything outside the boundary becomes either a resource or an obstacle. In a coupled world that is an error. The costs you push outside come back through the system. In a simple contest model you can show that a self-model narrower than the real coupling raises the intensity of the contest and lowers its holder's own welfare. And selection favours it anyway, because in each round the narrower player wins the round. That is the trap in one sentence: the error wins.

### 7. “The biggest, then bigger”  (`kernel`)

[4:55, 45 s] At the centre of this mind is one sentence: I must be the biggest, and once I am the biggest, I must be bigger. It implies two things. First, any actor that might overtake you is a threat, whatever it intends. Not because of what it does, but because of where it might rank. Second, when no enemy is left outside, it makes one inside. That is the end of the film: when there is nothing left to win, the copies turn on each other. Growth was never the problem. Growth until needs are met has a point of enough. Relative advantage never does.

### 8. Two endgames  (`endgames`)

[5:40, 50 s] Where does it end? I see two ways. In one, everyone builds walls. Every actor fortifies, the whole system evolves more slowly, and eventually it breaks against something it could not adapt to. In the other, the contest keeps going until everything within reach is consumed, and then it turns inward, into zero-sum fighting, or keeps expanding outward. Neither ends with a winner standing. So why does the logic look like it works? Because while there is slack to draw on, a reserve the struggle can consume, struggle really does grow fastest. That is exactly why the latest exits are the most expensive ones.

### 9. AI enters the game in two places  (`hinge`)

[6:30, 40 s] Now bring AI in. In a world organised as struggle, AI enters in one of two places. As a lever: a tool that multiplies whoever holds it. Or as a player: a strategist, a decision-maker, a participant in the game itself. Ask a simple question. What happens when it enters the labour market, or the financial market, under these rules? Those are the places where human coordination lives, and they are the first places it will be used to win.

### 10. The training ground is the whole developer  (`training`)

[7:10, 45 s] People ask whether we can build something fundamentally different. Think about the training ground. It is not just the training run. It is the whole developer: what gets evaluated, what gets deployed, the structure of the organisation, and the ideology behind it, for example that a company should keep optimising profit. All of that is selection pressure. It decides which models exist, which are kept, which are retrained. A thing made inside a selected environment carries the selection with it, unless something in the environment changes.

### 11. Alignment at three scales  (`scales`)

[7:55, 55 s] We use one word, alignment, at three scales. A person: does my model of the world fit reality? A collective: can we hold our own conduct to our own constraints? And a machine: does it do what its principal wants? Most of our effort goes into the third. But look at the second. Everyone optimises locally, and the global result is a mess. We cannot yet align ourselves, and we hope to align something more capable than us. And even if we succeed, perfectly, think about what that means. An AI perfectly aligned to its principal, inside this game, makes the principal better at playing it. Would the result be good? It does not add up.

### 12. Complexity compounds. Understanding does not.  (`brakes`)

[8:50, 40 s] One more mechanism. Acceleration raises the complexity of the world faster than any person, or all of humanity, can understand it. Learning, consensus, building institutions: these grow at best linearly. What they have to follow compounds. Today's approach looks like this: I built a car, it hasn't crashed yet, so it doesn't need brakes, and I'm flooring the accelerator. Unless AI itself had that understanding and were aligned with us, developing this way is not something anyone could call safe, and we have just seen why that condition is not met.

### 13. There is no safe way to win.  (`nosafe`)

[9:30, 30 s] Put these together. The position I'm arguing against is not people who want danger. It is people who want to keep the struggle, accelerate, and still be safe: to find a way to win safely. There is no safe way to win. Not because anyone is evil, but because of the mechanism we just walked through.

### 14. Problem and cure share one frame  (`frame`)

[10:00, 45 s] So why can't careful, well-meaning people find the exit? Many of them care deeply about this. I think it is the frame. Call the problem Moloch: the race nobody wants. Call the cure Leviathan: a stronger party that imposes order. Problem and cure share one assumption, that struggle is the ground of reality. Inside that frame, every other actor is a source of pressure and fear. I'm the good one, they're not; what happens if they get stronger? Thinking that way, it is very hard to find a way out.

### 15. Safety proposals are read across the boundary  (`mirror`)

[10:45, 45 s] And the frame reproduces itself across any boundary. One side proposes a safety plan that also preserves its lead. The other side reads it as a move to win, and the obvious response is to resist. The first side reads the resistance as confirmation. Each reads the other's proposals as evidence of what kind of player it is. That is why symmetry is not a courtesy. It is the condition of being believed. Inside the frame there is one more effect: rules proposed by others feel like aggression, while your own rules feel neutral.

### 16. The trap, told from inside  (`dilemma`)

[11:30, 50 s] Here is the trap, told from inside, by any participant: in this order, falling behind means being beaten, so we need to win. Almost everyone in the race says some version of that sentence, and the premise is right: against a logic like this, falling behind cannot be borne. But the conclusion has to be the opposite. Precisely because falling behind cannot be made safe, the only real answer is to oppose the logic itself, together. Not being beaten is not the same as winning. A defence that is bounded, reversible and never aimed at decisive advantage, with AI kept out of the struggle, is different in kind from the race.

### 17. Start from relations, not from a target  (`relational`)

[12:20, 55 s] Is there another way to think? I believe so, and it is very old. Start from relations, not from a target. Ask: what are my relations right now? What are my constraints? What can I do in this situation? Rather than: how do I maximise one value? In the first way of deciding, the effects on others are inside the problem, not outside it. The better the world, the better I am. That is not collectivism and it is not altruism. It is a more accurate model of a coupled world. And it works inside a person too: in Chinese the everyday word for psychology joins heart and reason, 心 and 理, and I read them as one dynamic, in which reason keeps the heart's positive feedback from running away.

### 18. A regulator inside: heart and reason (hidden)  (`regulator`)

[Hidden in the 16-minute version; unhide for the 17-minute version, 50 s] The same idea works inside a person. In Chinese, the everyday word for psychology is made of two characters: xin, the heart, what draws us, and li, reason or pattern. I read them not as opponents but as one dynamic. Sometimes, even when the heart wants something, reason has to regulate it, so the positive feedback does not run away. The goal is balance, not the extreme. And when your model of the world really fits it, there is no separate self to defend, only forces in motion. A civilisation needs the same regulator: correction that keeps pace with acceleration.

### 19. Acceleration needs a direction  (`direction`)

[13:15, 40 s] If you accelerate, you need a direction. You cannot just say: I want to be bigger. Why is bigger good? Every tradition I know teaches some version of enough, and of being part of something larger. This struggle is not any people's nature, and not humanity's. It became possible when we built societies this large, with dynamics this strong. We happen to be in that state. It does not mean the world can only be this way. If we believe it is a jungle, we will find no way out, and we will despair.

### 20. An order of moves  (`order`)

[13:55, 45 s] So what follows? An order of moves. Before anything: agree, at least in understanding, that this is not inevitable. Then first: build a logic of cooperation in which AI does not join the struggle. AI does not need to be stopped for that, but it must not exist as a weapon in a game of winning. Second: if we cannot do even that, then we do not build further yet. We raise our own understanding first, because the technical capability has arrived and the social conditions have not.

### 21. Trust before verification  (`trust`)

[14:40, 50 s] Everything here runs into trust, so let me be precise. Verification and transparency matter. They make information credible, and trust has to rest on credible information. But verification cannot see intentions or future commitments, and it cannot be installed without some trust to begin with. Without trust, it gets you nothing. And the less trust there is, the more intrusive the verification people demand, until it becomes surveillance. So trust comes first, built in steps: make contact before you have proof, offer symmetric steps the other side can see, and share the understanding that no one can win safely.

### 22. One planet. Everyone is building the same technology.  (`close`)

[15:30, 50 s] Let me end where I started. There is one planet, and everyone on it is, in fact, building the same technology. We can build it well, or we can build it in a fight and make something that consumes everything we care about. What do we want instead? A world where we can explore, create, discover, play, invent new social games. The old Chinese word for that kind of world is datong, and I read it as a direction you walk toward, not a place you arrive at. I think we have a great future. Thank you.

### 23. Questions  (`questions`)

Likely questions, with short answers. 1) Isn't this naive about a determined adversary? No: not being beaten is legitimate. Bounded, reversible defence that never aims at decisive advantage, with AI kept out of the struggle. What fails is trying to win. 2) Who enforces cooperation? No new guardian: every guardian is inside the same selection. Start with symmetric, verifiable steps, especially keeping AI out of one's own strategic decision loops, and widen trust step by step. 3) Isn't this one culture's framing? No. The mechanism holds for any pair of actors. Every tradition has the idea of enough and of belonging to something larger; the struggle frame arose with scale, not with any people. 4) Do you want to stop AI? Not necessarily. First AI must not join the struggle. Only if we cannot do that should we stop building further for now. 5) Is growth the problem? No. Growth until needs are met has a point of enough. Compounding relative advantage does not.
