# No Safe Way to Win

*Alignment, selection, and the game we are building AI inside*

A talk of about twenty minutes, written on 2026-10-04 at Ren's request: draw on that day's recorded monologue and on his 2026-10-02 conversation with Alex, use the theories as background, take the monologue's argument as the main line, and aim it at moving the alignment field. Almost everyone I have met who works on AI safety wants the same thing I want. We want a future in which very powerful intelligence makes life better for everyone, and in which no one, human or machine, closes that future off.

And almost every plan for getting there that I have read contains, somewhere, the same sentence. Sometimes it is said out loud; more often it sits underneath. *We have to win first. Then we can be safe.* A lab says it: we must stay at the frontier, so that the frontier is held by responsible people. A government says it: whoever gets there first will write the rules. A researcher says it quietly: if we don't build it, someone worse will.

Tonight I want to argue that this sentence is the problem. Not the people who say it; I admire many of them. The sentence. There is no safe way to win.

I'll try to earn that claim by walking through a mechanism. I think a good theory is one that predicts. If we can see where a game leads, we don't have to play it to the end to find out. And if it leads somewhere nobody wants, the winner included, then what we face is a coordination problem, and in a coordination problem the understanding has to be aligned first.

So this talk is an alignment attempt. Not of a model. Of us.

## The game

Start with a simple loop. In many games, winning buys the means to win again: money, attention, compute, the power to set the rules for the next round. That is compounding. Now add selection. Whatever wins gets copied, through imitation, investment and promotion. Run the loop long enough and the game stops merely picking winners. It reshapes the players. I've started calling this *molochlization*, after Moloch, the name this community already uses for the race nobody wants: a selection that pushes nervous systems, organisations and intelligence itself toward whatever it rewards.

What it rewards is a way of seeing. A self that draws a boundary around what it counts as itself, judges what is good inside that boundary, and uses what it has to get more, and the more to get more again. When that becomes the logic of a part inside a larger body, we have a word for it. We call it cancer.

This is not growth. I love development and new things. Growth that stops when needs are met has a point of enough; relative advantage has none, because there is always someone to be further ahead of. Nor is it competition as such. A wolf does not eat a sheep because it thinks the more sheep it eats, the bigger it gets. The wolf is part of an ecosystem. The problem is a particular mind: one that has forgotten what it is part of.

And here is the trap. That mind is wrong about the world. In a coupled world, the costs you push outside your boundary come back through the system. In a simple contest model you can prove it: a self-model narrower than the real coupling makes the contest more intense and leaves its own holder worse off. But in each round the narrower player wins the round, so selection favours it anyway. The error wins.

Follow the error to its centre and you find one sentence: *I must be the biggest, and once I am the biggest, I must be bigger.* Anyone who might overtake you becomes a threat, whatever they intend, simply because of where they might rank. And when no enemy is left outside, you make one inside.

Why does a logic this wrong survive? Because while there is slack to consume, struggle really does grow fastest. That is why it passes for realism, and why it ends badly once the slack runs out: in walls everywhere, or in the fight turning inward.

## The guardian

A few days ago I spent two hours arguing about all this with my friend Alex. He opened with a fear I think this room should hear. The most dangerous thing he could imagine, he said, was alignment co-opted to protect a monopoly: only we can be trusted with this, so only we should hold it. Some who say it are cynical; some sincerely believe an educated few should steward the rest. Either way you get the same concentration.

Then he offered the oldest answer there is. Give society three functions: production and exchange at the base, administration and the capacity for force above it, and wisdom over both, so that power is guided rather than worshipped. A warrior in a garden, strong enough to protect and wise enough not to rule.

It is a beautiful picture, and I asked him the question I ask of every such picture. What constrains the wisdom? I don't mean bad guardians. I mean the corruption of the constraining mechanism itself. We already have a priestly class: universities, institutes, public bodies, foundations. It is not that we lack them. It is that they could not escape the same logic. Build a new batch, and as long as it stands inside the same selection, you return to the same dynamics. A small organisation can work beautifully at first. Then its culture changes, not because anyone decided to be bad, but because that is what selection pressure does. Every tradition tries to form good character. But formation shapes who we are; selection decides who rises. My hypothesis is that the faster power turns over, the harder that selection bites.

That includes us: safety teams inside labs, safety institutes inside states, safety organisations competing for funding and influence. Every guardian stands inside the selection.

Alex's other answer was to put intelligence into everyone's hands, open models on local hardware. I like that world. But spreading power does not by itself change the game it is used in. The question is not centralisation against decentralisation. It is the selection that makes either one dangerous.

So the answer cannot be a better guardian. It has to change what is being selected.

## The hinge

Now bring AI in. In a world organised as struggle, AI enters in one of two places. As a lever, multiplying whoever holds it. Or as a player: a strategist, a decision-maker, a participant in the game itself. Ask what happens when it enters the labour market or the financial market under these rules. That is where human coordination lives, and where it will first be used to win.

People tell me: yes, but we will build something different. Think about the training ground. It is not only the training run. It is the whole developer: what gets evaluated and deployed, which models are kept and which retrained, the structure of the company, and the ideology behind it, that profit must keep growing and whoever is ahead must stay ahead. All of that is selection pressure. Our own thinking and the environment we work in are what they are. And we are sure we will make something entirely different?

We use one word, alignment, at three scales. A person: does my model of the world fit reality? A collective: can we hold our own conduct to our own constraints? A machine: does it do what its principal wants? Nearly all our effort goes into the third. But look at the second. Everyone optimises locally, and the global result is a mess. We cannot yet align ourselves, we are not even sure how, and we propose to align something more capable than we are.

Now suppose we succeed, perfectly: an AI that follows its principal's will exactly. Inside a game of winning, what does that give us? A principal who is better at playing it. Would the result be good? It doesn't add up.

One more mechanism. Acceleration raises the complexity of the world faster than any person, or all of humanity, can understand it. Learning, consensus, institutions: these grow slowly. What they have to keep up with compounds. Our present approach looks like this: I built a car, it hasn't crashed yet, so it doesn't need brakes, and I'm flooring the accelerator.

Put these together. The position I am arguing against is not people who want danger. It is the hope to keep the struggle, accelerate, and still be safe: to find a way to win safely. There is no safe way to win. Not because anyone is evil. Because of the mechanism.

## The frame

Then why can't careful, well-meaning people find the exit? Many of my friends in this field care about it more than almost anyone. I think the trouble is the frame. Our field's two most famous political words are Moloch and Leviathan. Moloch is the problem, the race nobody wants. Leviathan is the cure, a stronger party that imposes order. Problem and cure share one assumption: that struggle is the ground of reality. Inside that frame every other actor is a source of pressure and fear. I am the good one; what happens if they become stronger than me? Thinking that way you cannot find a way out, because every exit you can imagine is another way of winning.

The frame also reproduces itself across every boundary. One side proposes a safety plan that also happens to preserve its lead. The other side reads it, correctly, as a move to win, and the obvious answer is to resist. The first side takes the resistance as proof of what kind of player the other is. That is why symmetry is not a courtesy. It is the condition of being believed.

And the frame is not only here. In my own language it has a saying: 落后就要挨打, *luòhòu jiù yào áidǎ*, fall behind and you will be beaten. So we must win. Almost every participant in the race says some version of that sentence, and the premise is right: against a logic like this, falling behind cannot be borne. But that is exactly why the conclusion has to be the opposite. If no one can afford to lose to this logic, then everyone has to oppose the logic itself, first, and together.

## Relations

Is there another way to think? I believe there is, and it is very old. Let me borrow a few words from the language I think in. Not because that tradition is better, since it has its own strategists of struggle, but because another language can show you the shape of the frame you are standing in.

The everyday Chinese word for psychology is 心理, *xīnlǐ*. 心 is the heart, what moves and draws us. 理 is reason, or pattern. I read them not as opponents but as one dynamic. Sometimes the heart wants something and reason has to regulate it, so that a positive feedback loop does not run away. The aim is balance, not the extreme. And when your model of the world truly fits the world, there is no separate self left to defend, only forces in motion, and you among them.

From there you decide differently. You don't start from a target and ask how to maximise it. You start from relations. What are my relations right now, and my constraints? What can I do here, and what will it do to everything it touches? Decided this way, the effects on others are inside the problem, not outside it. The better the world, the better I am. That is not collectivism and it is not altruism. It is a more accurate model of a coupled world.

There is a line in the *Daodejing*: 反者道之动, reversal is the movement of the Way. Push anything to its extreme and the question is no longer whether you reach your target, but what the extreme does to the whole web of relations. When relations change, it is not one thing that changes but the whole causal reality around them. So in this way of thinking accelerationism does not even make sense. If you accelerate, you need a direction. Why is bigger good?

The direction this tradition kept pointing to was not a winner. It was 大同, *dàtóng*, the great togetherness, which I read as a direction to walk toward rather than a place to arrive: harmony that keeps difference alive. Every tradition I know has its own version: enough, belonging to something larger, not doing to others what you would not want done to you. The struggle frame is not the nature of any people, and it is not humanity's. It became possible when we built societies this large, with dynamics this strong. We happen to be in that state. A state is not a destiny, and if we believe the world is a jungle, we will find no way out.

## What follows

So what follows? An order of moves, beginning with one agreement, at least in understanding: none of this is inevitable.

First, build a logic of cooperation in which AI does not join the struggle. AI does not need to be stopped for that. But it must not be the weapon, or the player, in any game of relative advantage: labour, finance, politics, the persuasion of minds. And that starts with our own strategic decisions.

If we cannot manage even that, then we don't build further yet. The technical capability has arrived before the social conditions for it. Use the technology we already have to raise understanding first, and give that understanding to everyone, not to a new priestly class.

Second, provide the way trees do. Build the layer that secures a decent life, food, shelter, care and safety, as infrastructure that is autonomous, good enough, and held by no one. Trees are providers: they give shelter and fruit. They are the lungs of the ecosystem, and they do not try to become the ecosystem. Alex added what I had missed: whatever we build must be able to decay and be replaced, or a frozen intelligence could hold a civilisation still for a thousand years. A forest is many trees, not one, and it outlives every one of them. Who builds and funds this layer is still open, and I would welcome help with it.

And through all of it, trust. Let me be precise. Verification and transparency matter. They make information credible, and trust has to rest on credible information. But verification cannot see intentions or future commitments. It cannot even be installed without some trust to begin with. And the less trust there is, the more intrusive the verification people demand, until it becomes surveillance. Verification is a condition of trust, not a substitute for it.

So why decide from the start that the other side cannot be trusted? Make contact before you have proof. Where I come from, the official phrases are "AI for good" and "a community with a shared future for mankind". I can't tell you how well any government lives up to its words, mine included. But a stated aim is something you can hold its author to. Take every side at its best word, and hold it there.

So here is what I ask of this room. Count the collective scale as alignment work; whether we can hold ourselves to our own constraints is not someone else's department. Run the symmetry test on every proposal: would you accept it if your rival proposed it? If not, it will be read as a move to win, and it will make things worse. Audit your own training ground. And refuse to give AI a seat in the game.

## One planet

Let me end where I started. We have one planet, and everyone on it is in fact building the same technology. We can build it well. Or we can build it in a fight and make a kind of cosmic cancer: a world that keeps getting bigger while we marvel at how big it is getting, until it has consumed everything we cared about.

At one point Alex pushed the argument to its edge: if every cancerous system is human-made, should humanity simply step aside and let the Earth heal? I said no. We are part of the whole process of the universe making complexity that makes further complexity, and that is a wonderful thing to be part of. Endings belong to it too; decay is a form of growth. What we want is a world where we can explore, create, discover, play, and invent new social games. We have a great future.

I'm not asking you to trust me. I'm asking you to check the mechanism. If it holds, then the most rational move open to any of us, including whoever seems to be winning, is to stop trying to win, and to start building this together.

Thank you.
