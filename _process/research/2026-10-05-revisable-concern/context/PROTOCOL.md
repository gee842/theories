# Construction and strategic correction protocol

5 October 2026. AI-assisted model study under Yongxu Ren's research direction. Baseline theory commit: `dd8ad7c`. This protocol is written before the numerical enumeration. All parameters are authored examples; no human behavior or real institution is measured.

## Research question

When will a participant construct and operate an alternative that makes an otherwise self-interested operator protect their interests? When do construction expense, dated liquidity, cheap interference, succession, or strategic depletion prevent this? The study tests a narrow formation mechanism. It does not derive property rights, compassion, sensing, or the origin of its action repertoire.

## Physical and strategic assumptions

In each period the participant has one rival input, such as a unit of labor or a component. It can be allocated to a shared activity or a constructed alternative, never both. The shared activity pays the participant `b` if safeguarded and `x=b-h` otherwise. A maintained alternative pays `o`; the inactive alternative pays `o0`. The core region is `o0 < x < o < b`. A higher outside alternative is also examined as a no-trade control. These are receipts in a common numeraire, not a universal moral measure.

The operator's net rent from receiving the input is `r`, relative to an available replacement or other opportunity. Before allocation, the operator chooses an observable implemented safeguard costing `c`, ordinary harmful operation costing zero, or temporary interference costing `chi` that disables the alternative for this period. An operator that incurs a cost pays it whether or not participation follows. Operator liquidity is sufficient for these moves in the main study. The physical ability to withhold the rival input and the cost of interference are explicit primitives. No command forces the operator to protect anyone.

The participant can build the alternative at real cost `K` and activate it at cost `m` per period. The durable installed asset can remain dormant without operating receipts; shelf decay is excluded. Required subsistence `ell`, construction, activation, and any handoff bill must be paid before current receipts. The cash state then receives the actual selected payoff `b`, `x`, `o`, or `o0`. Unaffordable moves are absent. Starting cash is at least `ell`; `x >= ell` ensures the inactive baseline can continue.

At declared successor dates, an installed alternative requires an actual handoff payment `J`. The participant can transfer and activate it, transfer it dormant, or lose the installed asset and rebuild later or immediately. Transfer failure is a modeled loss of effective access, not destruction of all physical material. The default study has no handoff bill; separate declared cases test it.

The sequence is: participant construction or upkeep choice and prefunded bills; operator implemented action; participant input allocation; receipts; next state. All choices and states are public. There is no statistical sensing problem or hidden operator type. Uncertainty and discovery remain later research tasks.

## Solution and comparisons

Solve the finite game by exact rational backward induction. Participant preferences are discounted receipts minus real expenditure and subsistence. Initial wealth is common to alternatives; terminal wealth is not added again. Operator preferences are net rents. A fresh operator values only the current period; a persistent operator values the full remaining horizon. The participant always values its remaining horizon. These are different strategic environments.

For exact utility ties, the main implementation uses a declared deterministic action ordering, with operator harm before interference before safeguard and participant lower expenditure before higher expenditure. This selects an equilibrium; it does not establish uniqueness. Selected sensitivity cases reverse operator tie preference. The constant one-period regime theorem is checked independently of this selection away from equalities.

Primary comparisons use the same available moves and endowment: optimal construction and operation versus no construction, a participant using only current-period consequences, and activation whenever affordable. The latter two are specified heuristics, not equilibrium-optimal participants. Operator responses are recomputed for each declared participant policy. A ready-made asset comparison is labeled a different initial endowment. A cheap-to-disable promise comparison changes actual control, not just a label.

## Predetermined checks and enumeration

1. Exhaustively solve the one-period public game and check the strict regime conditions `c < min(r,chi)`, `chi < min(r,c)`, and `r < min(c,chi)` under credible refusal. Inspect ties separately.
2. Compare backward induction with a separately implemented full-tree solution on small horizons. Check every realized payment prefix, input allocation, and cash identity. Check the fixed-policy values and operator responses against the same game timing.
3. Enumerate the Cartesian product: horizons `{2,4,6}`, construction `{1,3}`, upkeep `{1,2}`, initial cash `{3,4,6,9}`, safeguard cost `{1,2}`, rent `{0,2,4}`, and interference cost `{1/2,3/2,7/2,6}`. Fix `b=5,x=2,o=4,o0=0,ell=2`, discount one, and no handoff. There are 1,152 parameter cases, each compared under fresh and persistent operators. These are deterministic sensitivity cases, not independent samples from the world.
4. Examine the announced two-period strategic-depletion witness: `b=5,x=ell=2,o=4,o0=0,m=K=c=1,r=4,chi=3/2,w=4`. A static credible-threat test predicts safeguarding while active. The dynamic game may instead deter early construction because interference can remove the participant's future ability to prefund operation.
5. Examine succession with horizon six, handoff at period four, bills `{0,1,3,6}`, and successor interference costs `{1/2,3/2,6}`; compare inherited cash and capacity with actual delivery. Also inspect discount `3/4`, no rent, overly attractive alternatives, and an additive nonrival fallback as scope controls.

## Predeclared expectations and limits

Construction can fail despite a privately worthwhile installed capability when its initial bill is unaffordable. A cheap interference move can prevent construction even if no interference is observed on the equilibrium path. Credible exit can produce departure rather than safeguarding when the operator has little rent at stake. A persistent operator can rationally choose costly present interference to reduce future resistance; therefore a one-period deterrence inequality alone may not certify the dynamic game.

The last expectation is a proposed counterexample, not a claimed general comparative-static law. Increased patience need not always reduce protection. No prediction requires more inquiry, more funding, stronger outside options, or longer horizons to be universally beneficial. Failed expectations, tie dependence, and negative cases will be reported. The expected contribution is the coupled formation and dated strategic-capacity analysis, not priority for backward induction, credible exit, or outside options.
