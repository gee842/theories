# Recipient consequence becomes evaluatively effective: primary-data audit

6 October 2026. AI-authored supporting study of the connection **acquired recipient consequence → changed action with an actual recipient effect** in the [generative-grounding programme](../../../../theories/becoming/chapters/GENERATIVE-GROUNDING.md). This note freezes source access and identification limits before the separately owned retrospective analysis. It does not claim a newly formed moral motive, ethical truth or completion of the unified foundation.

## Why this evidence and what it can discriminate

The [existing cross-domain audit](CROSS-DOMAIN-IDENTIFICATION.md) distinguishes represented-beneficiary learning from actual delivery, and the [harm-identification audit](HUIGEN-HARM-IDENTIFICATION.md) separates stimulus, aversive experience, response and reason. The new source is **Hernandez-Lallement et al. (2020), “Harm to Others Acts as a Negative Reinforcer in Rats,” Current Biology**, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2020.01.017), [institutional original](https://pure.knaw.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/13278204/hernandez2020.pdf), [authors' deposited data](https://osf.io/65j3g/).

Its main contrast changes the recipient consequence of an already learned action while each completed action continues delivering one pellet to the actor. Choices physically control shocks delivered to a distinct recipient. This is closer to the missing action-to-recipient interface than a task whose displayed other-person reward is never delivered. It does **not** hold the actor's entire private outcome fixed: effort differs across levers, nonresponse loses the food opportunity, and observing distress may itself be aversive. Nor does it separately measure the actor's belief about the causal connection. The [mechanism identification note](RECIPIENT-VALUATION-IDENTIFICATION.md) therefore distinguishes efficacy/contingency acquisition from formation of valuation.

The strongest feasible primary discriminator is a full-trajectory comparison of **all 12 available male ContingentHarm actors against all 14 available male NoHarm actors**, baseline to the three shock sessions. Selecting only significant switchers would select on the outcome. Female ContingentHarm actors form a secondary descriptive group because there is no female NoHarm comparison. A reduction in preferred-lever choice within ContingentHarm alone is inadequate: selecting that lever for its high baseline frequency creates regression-to-the-mean concerns even without harm. NoHarm supplies an appropriate comparison for that selection rule, subject to the cohort limitations below.

## Direct primary-method reconstruction

Page references use the 22-page preserved PDF, because its internal article has in-press pagination.

**Training and supplied history (PDF 17–18, e2–e3).** Rats learn lever pressing for sucrose, then deliberate nose-poke initiation and choice. Advancement uses completion thresholds; this is substantial supplied reward training, not emergence from an unstructured agent. In the equal-food groups, one lever requires 30 cN and the other 60 cN. Hard-lever side is randomized across rats and stable within a rat. Before testing, the actor is placed alone in the victim compartment for a 10-minute baseline followed by four 0.8 mA, one-second shocks, with 240–360-second intervals. Actors are isolated for ten minutes afterward to reduce communication of stress to unexposed cage mates. The NonExposed group undergoes the same exposure session with shockers off.

**Baseline and recipient intervention (PDF 18–20, e3–e5).** Baseline occurs the day after exposure, followed by three consecutive daily shock sessions. Each begins with four forced trials, two per lever, then twenty free-choice trials. Forced trials repeatedly expose actors to the contingencies and are not present in the released trial-choice table. Failed forced trials repeat; failed free-choice trials advance. The 400-second deadline can fail at initiation or lever pressing. In ContingentHarm, the lever preferred at baseline immediately shocks the recipient at 0.8 mA for one second; the pellet follows one second after the press. The alternative gives the same one pellet without a recipient shock. The baseline-preferred action need not be the physically easier action; the paper says approximately a quarter preferred the harder lever. A switch cannot automatically be counted as increased effort.

**NoHarm and RandomHarm (PDF 19, e4).** NoHarm recipients stand on an insulating plastic floor. The grid is still activated, controlling sounds associated with shock delivery while preventing current from reaching the recipient. RandomHarm is a qualified comparison: its eight recipients receive the trial-indexed shock sequence of the eight strongest ContingentHarm switchers, with shocks independent of the current actor's chosen lever and delayed 3–8 seconds after departure from the food receptacle. Donor selection, shock timing and contingency all differ. RandomHarm is not a randomly assigned equal-dose comparison against the complete ContingentHarm population.

**Individuals, roles and assignment (PDF 16–19).** Each actor has one unique recipient, with sex-matched pairs. Four animals share a cage. Random tail numbering identifies animals 1–4; actor 1 pairs with victim 2, actor 3 with victim 4. Thus two actors may share a housing environment. Randomized housing, numbering and hard-lever side are explicit; random assignment to the experimental conditions is not stated. The summary table contains SBL# and Rat Number, but supplies no dictionary establishing a cage ID or the precise meaning of SBL#. Male ContingentHarm has SBL042e/SBL048, NoHarm SBL049/SBL053b, and NonPreExposed SBL053a/SBL060. These recorded cohort labels do not overlap between the comparisons. Do not present an ordinary label permutation as design-based randomized inference or silently equate a label suffix with a verified cage.

**Coverage and exclusions.** The paper reports 314 rats ordered for the complete programme. The released summary has 142 actor records across many conditions; the trial-choice file has 78 actors from six conditions. The main reported groups are all present in that file. This is coverage of the published released groups, not a complete enrollment/dropout register. The paper specifies training thresholds and pharmacology exclusions, but no main-group exclusion ledger was located. Analysis can retain all available actors without claiming intention-to-treat over every purchased animal. Published nonperformance is substantial for some actors, so missing action cannot be recoded as selection of the harmless lever.

## Prospective retrospective contrast and alternatives

Root owns the [analysis protocol](../../../../experiments/recipient-harm-valuation/PROTOCOL.md). The source supports three distinct quantities per actor and phase: harmful-lever responses per scheduled slot, harmless-lever responses per scheduled slot, and nonresponses per scheduled slot. Their joint changes distinguish successful replacement of action from cessation. Conditional choice among completed responses is informative but can select on post-intervention engagement. Keep actor, not trial, as the repeated unit; preserve the uncertain cage/cohort dependence in uncertainty claims.

A secondary male exposed versus NonPreExposed comparison can ask whether prior firsthand shock experience is associated with a different subsequent trajectory. Its baseline is already after the exposure manipulation, groups have disjoint cohort labels, and the study has no independent belief measure. It cannot isolate a learned evaluation term from generalized fear, context conditioning, salience or more accurate contingency learning. The two-versus-one and three-versus-one pellet arms introduce genuine reward tradeoffs but change training/reward structure and do not replace the equal-food primary comparison.

The strongest competing account is conventional learned avoidance: recipient reactions acquire aversive significance for the actor, and an action predicting those reactions becomes less attractive. This can explain an effective reduction in recipient shocks without an intention to protect the recipient. The authors explicitly acknowledge this distinction in PDF 13/internal p11, including personal distress, fear for one's own safety and nonsocial aversive cues. No independently manipulated recipient identity, private escape option, pre/post valuation measure or separate comprehension assay resolves those alternatives here. Food and shock reinforcement are supplied biological/task conditions; the analysis must not redescribe the analyst's desired recipient outcome as a newly derived reward.

## Search boundary and what would change the account

This selection follows existing repository coverage of represented-beneficiary learning and cue-triggered cooperation. Searches also located Crockett's 2014 self/other harm study and Volz's 2017 extension, but no accessible complete trial dataset was established during this bounded discovery. A sharper belief-measure comparator, [Fornari et al. 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-36807-3), has probability reports and money/shock outcome dropout, with [OSF data](https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/RK8W4). Its confederate videos are prerecorded and presented as live in the fMRI task, so it cannot supply actual choice-contingent recipient harm. It was not substituted for the actual-effects requirement. No models were run and no new data were collected.

If harmful-action reductions occur mainly through nonresponse, the account must distinguish recipient protection from lost actor engagement. If the full-group change resembles NoHarm, baseline selection or nonspecific drift remains sufficient. A robust descriptive difference with increased harmless responding would support functional sensitivity to recipient-linked consequences under the supplied apparatus and history. It would still leave the central stronger transition—accurate understanding becoming a newly acquired, recipient-directed evaluative ground—unidentified. This evidence can constrain that derivation without claiming to complete it.

For the last category, the completed-response Shock 3 percentages are 1700/19, 800/9 and 260/3; the differences exceed ordinary display rounding. For actor 37, retaining the undefined session is distinct from averaging available sessions. Ordinary finite rounding differences elsewhere were already within the producer's declared 0.011-percentage-point tolerance and do not account for any of the 39 flags.

One additional source convention matters for interpretation: the first 46 populated total cells have formulas summing only the three shock-session nonperformance counts, excluding baseline. The original trial records contain no baseline zero codes across the 78 actors, so including versus excluding baseline makes no numerical difference here. This observation should not become a general definition of the total field in other data.

**Primary implication:** none of the 39 flags concerns actors 1–26, the entire male NoHarm/ContingentHarm primary comparison. Its per-session percentages agree with the completed-response interpretation, and the frozen joint action/nonresponse outcomes come directly from the trial codes. The inspected discrepancies provide no evidence of a cohort join error, global code inversion or accidental inclusion of forced trials. The explicit CSV header, four twenty-slot phase blocks and methods' exclusion of forced trials support the chosen free-choice map. This source audit verifies that interpretation against the released records; it does not independently reconstruct omitted acquisition logs or verify that each physical shock was delivered.

The primary report can therefore retain all 39 flags while describing their causes. It should not call them 39 corrupted observations, silently fill blank summaries, or replace the frozen estimates with whichever source convention gives a preferred result. The cost-group denominator differences and the female no-response session remain visible limitations on comparisons with the published summary statistics.
