# Rescue requirements: justification, refusal and cumulative demands

6 October 2026. AI-authored primary-source assessment for the [universal ethical minimum](../../../../theories/becoming/history/UNIVERSAL-ETHICAL-MINIMUM.md). Named connection: **objective moral favor → required relief in a bounded serious case, with justified exceptions**. The earlier [source comparison](ETHICAL-MINIMUM-SOURCES.md) and [practical-necessity audit, §13](HUIGEN-PRACTICAL-NECESSITY-AUDIT.md#13-nonparticipating-patients-rescue-and-sustainable-concern) establish the research context. This note supplies a stronger demandingness objection and a test for the next argument; it does not establish a universal ethical foundation.

**Finding:** burden comparison can help justify a rescue requirement only through substantive judgments about which objections warrant permission to refuse. Accounting for cumulative costs is necessary but does not itself settle that comparison. A demanding principle can acknowledge every cost and still require further help. The disputed bridge is therefore not completed either by displaying an inequality or by reserving a quantity of resources for plural lives.

## 1. Scanlon: reasons for permission must withstand the opposing reasons

**T. M. Scanlon, “Contractualism and Intuitionism.”** [NYU-hosted manuscript](https://www.law.nyu.edu/sites/default/files/2025-09/T.%20M.%20Scanlon,%20Contractualism%20and%20Intuitionism.pdf). Directly inspected manuscript pp. 1–8 and 23–40 through web extraction; additionally encountered pp. 41–43, which are not used here. The cover and hosting path identify a colloquium version; the text records its origin in a 2022 lecture. No journal-publication date is inferred.

**Bounded synopsis:** The manuscript treats the sufficiency of reasons as a substantive normative matter, not a measurement of pain or life. It distinguishes an account of moral content from reasons to accept its authority. Its aid discussion compares protection under general principles, including security against intrusive demands. It defends minor-cost rescue but does not infer an unrestricted duty whenever another benefits more. The proposed limits remain contestable; giving someone sufficient reason to choose an option need not justify depriving others of their alternatives. Relevant locators: 26–30, 31–36 and 37–40.

## 2. Ashford: recognizing the helper's whole life does not settle priority

**Elizabeth Ashford, “The Demandingness of Scanlon’s Contractualism,” *Ethics* 113(2) (2003), 273–302.** [University repository record](https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/handle/10023/1568); [primary article PDF](https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/bitstream/handle/10023/1568/Ashford2003-Ethics113-Demandingness.pdf?sequence=1&isAllowed=y). Directly inspected the complete article through web extraction and a temporary local PDF extraction, including notes. Her historical empirical claims were not independently verified or adopted as current facts.

**Bounded synopsis:** Ashford includes cumulative losses to projects and relationships, yet argues that remaining severe needs can warrant extremely demanding aid. A fair-share rule assessed under hypothetical compliance need not justify actual omissions when others fail to help; unfairness by other helpers does not settle what is owed to patients. Equal-chance advance agreement cannot simply represent positions with predictably unequal exposure. She distinguishes this demandingness from a further objection concerning risk and aggregation. Relevant locators: 282–284, 284–293 and 294–302. This is her challenge to the theory she examines, not a demonstrated refutation of every account of latitude or of Scanlon’s later manuscript.

## 3. The next argument must expose its normative work

The following is this study's reconstruction and proposed assessment, not an additional summary of either author.

Consider one eligible actor, one independently identified patient, and one permissible intervention reliably ending severe, still-unwanted distress. No other patient loses protection. The action imposes a minor inconvenience; the full relevant history contains no comparable cumulative loss, neglected responsibility or restriction on the actor's bodily authority. Absence of these conditions is a substantive stipulation to check in an application. It cannot be inferred from calling the action easy.

The existing minimum supports a moral consideration favoring intervention. To conclude that the actor must intervene requires another claim: the reasons protecting permission to decline are insufficient in this case. That claim cannot be replaced with “there is a reason to help,” since moral favor and permissible alternatives can coexist. Nor does making the patient's benefit larger establish that every lesser reason loses its power to permit a choice.

One affirmative route compares two permissions. Allowing refusal merely to preserve this inconvenience leaves a grave patient-ground without effective protection. Requiring the bounded action protects that ground while preserving the helper's independently justified authority, competing responsibilities and meaningful pursuits as stipulated. The proposed priority is grounded in the character of the interests, their effects and the absence of an adequate competing basis for refusal. It does not depend on the actor's enthusiasm or expected reward.

This offers a reasoned case for requiring rescue, but it openly adds a substantive comparative premise: under these conditions, preserving the minor convenience is insufficient justification for withholding the relief. The supporting explanation is that the two permissions protect substantially unequal interests without a relevant difference of standing or another warranted ground reversing the comparison. A critic may dispute that premise, defend a residual prerogative or challenge the stipulated boundaries. Describing the premise as rational, contractual or generative cannot decide that dispute.

The derivation should therefore distinguish three successes: identifying a real patient-ground, defending its practical priority here, and explaining why that priority defeats permission to decline. Agreement on the first two does not make the third unnecessary. Conversely, the third need not be an arbitrary command if its account of permission is independently defensible. This is the work a rescue argument must make reviewable.

## 4. An exception must identify a changed ground

The decisive exception test is comparative. Introduce a genuine loss to the helper's health, an existing dependent's protection, a valid refusal of the proposed means, or an independently warranted control interest. Then ask which permission or requirement that ground supports and why. The existence of a competing good shows what must be considered; its priority still needs defense. A formal budget cutoff cannot supply it.

Past assistance matters through present exhaustion, foreclosed opportunities, actual undertakings, foreseeable policy burdens and any separately defended entitlement. A mere count of good deeds is not automatically a credit permitting a future grave omission. Equally, the present action's small incremental cost cannot erase its contribution to a damaging continuing pattern. The account must retain both the actual history and the present patient's reasons.

Other providers change the case only through warranted expectations of timely provision. A named backup who is unavailable leaves a different situation from a confirmed handoff. Likewise, a proposed institution does not yet deliver relief. Constructing a durable arrangement can be a justified response, but the transition must account for patients exposed before it works and for the actual costs of construction. This supplies an explicit interface to the generative programme's formation and renewal mechanisms without making their efficiency a moral ground.

## 5. Proposed discriminating comparisons

These are philosophical tests, not experimental results or settled judgments outside the initial stipulated case.

| Controlled change | What the proposed rule must explain |
|---|---|
| Only the actor's desire to help disappears | Whether the defended requirement survives without importing reward or identification with the patient |
| The same present intervention follows a history of severe sleep loss rather than adequate recovery | Which actual competing ground changes, and whether it now justifies refusal |
| A credible alternative provider withdraws at the last moment | Whether a previous allocation still justifies leaving the patient without relief |
| A patient cannot ever become a helper, or a helper cannot realistically become a recipient | Whether standing and protection survive without a fictitious symmetry of future positions |
| The claimed private project loses no opportunity through this intervention | Whether merely naming the project improperly generates an exemption |
| A valid restriction bars the proposed means, while another effective permissible means is available | Whether the rule distinguishes the barred action from the remaining relief claim |

The hardest unresolved case retains an actual serious patient need while further rescue would genuinely erode a helper's worthwhile life. It is insufficient to answer either that the patient remains worse off or that the helper has already sacrificed. A completed plural minimum needs a substantive account of why the permitted allocation is justifiable to each affected person, including those left with unmet needs. It may conclude that some circumstances impose grave demands or unavoidable losses; plurality cannot be guaranteed by definition.
