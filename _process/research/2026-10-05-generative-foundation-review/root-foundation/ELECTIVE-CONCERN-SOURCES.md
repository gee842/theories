# Elective concern: what a genuine power to create reasons would establish

6 October 2026. AI-authored primary-source comparison for **patient-ground → an unrelated actor's practical reason**, continuing the [constitutive argument](CONSTITUTIVE-SIGNIFICANCE-ARGUMENT.md) and its [elective-concern challenge](CONSTITUTIVE-SIGNIFICANCE-COUNTERREVIEW.md#2-the-strongest-coherent-elective-concern-rival). The target is a serious account of reason creation by commitment, not a caricature in which any desire automatically makes its object morally good. Two works by the same advocate supply a focused comparison; they are not independent empirical evidence or a literature survey.

## 1. Chang 2013: special reasons, genuine creation and retained constraints

**Ruth Chang, “Commitments, Reasons, and the Will,” in Russ Shafer-Landau, ed., *Oxford Studies in Metaethics*, volume 8 (2013), 74–113, DOI 10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199678044.003.0004.** [Author-hosted typeset chapter](https://www.ruthchang.net/s/Chang_CommitmentsReasonstheWill.pdf); [author's publication record](https://www.ruthchang.net/selectedpublications/).

**Bounded synopsis:** Chang proposes internal commitment as the ground of special practical reasons, distinct from promises requiring uptake. Her hybrid account retains given reasons: willing cannot reverse their all-things-considered direction, although it can add even a harmful contributory reason. Commitment is neither arbitrary intention nor a guarantee of moral permissibility. Withdrawal can remove its created reason while antecedent and downstream reasons persist. The account therefore defends real reason creation without making every reason depend on adoption. It does not establish that every patient's benefit already supplies every actor a reason either.

Exact relevant locations: 76–77, internal versus moral commitments; 94 n. 38, reason creation presupposing normativity; 94–96, exit and persistence; 97–103, triggering versus grounding; 104–110, hybrid constraints, objection and reply. ## 2. Chang 2020: a power need not merely activate an antecedent norm

**Ruth Chang, “Do We Have Normative Powers?”, *Aristotelian Society Supplementary Volume* 94 (2020), 275–300, DOI 10.1093/arisup/akaa012.** [Author-hosted offprint](https://www.ruthchang.net/s/Do-We-Have-Normative-Powers-offprint.pdf). This is Chang's contribution to the “Normative Powers” exchange, not a reading of Victor Tadros's companion contribution.

**Bounded synopsis:** Chang proposes reflexive willing as a metaphysical ground of reasons, rather than an ordinary event activating independently applicable norms. Its attraction is an active conception of rational agency. Appropriate exercise requires a well-formed choice involving incommensurable reasons; this is broader than a tie or parity, and can coexist with unequal strengths. The same consideration can have given and will-based support. The proposal leaves questions and supporting arguments to other work. It does not demonstrate that an actor's will is necessary for all moral significance of another person's condition.

## 3. Our comparison: distinguish creation, content and exclusivity

The following analysis is our reconstruction of the research problem, not an additional doctrine attributed to Chang.

A serious elective account may claim that commitment creates a reason in a constitutive sense: the agent's act is part of what makes the consideration reason-giving. The unified account must consider that possibility without requiring every legitimate power to work by triggering a prior conditional rule. Asking for a defensible account of a power is appropriate. Defining all acceptable powers as derivatives of antecedent normative rules would preclude the proposed alternative before assessing it.

Two source-specific cautions matter here. An exercise can be assessed by reasons without the specific willing being guided by them (2013, 110; 2020, 298). And “given” includes desire-based as well as value-based reasons in the 2020 terminology (294); it does not mean exclusively motivation-independent moral reasons.

Nevertheless, three different claims need separate justification:

1. An agent can have a genuine power to create some reasons.
2. Exercising that power creates a particular reason concerning this patient's benefit.
3. No reason grounded in that benefit can apply to this actor before the power is exercised.

The first does not entail the second in every case, and the second does not entail the third. A new reason can share its patient-directed content with a reason that has another ground. Conversely, shared content does not show that the newly created reason was already present: the act of commitment could make a real difference. The dispute about baseline ethical concern is the third claim, not whether agents can ever create reasons.

This also prevents a change of normative domain. A theory of special practical reasons need not identify every such reason as a moral claim of its beneficiary. Rational support, prospective moral favor, permissible conduct, decisive direction and an obligation owed to another remain separately assessable. A source's account of one cannot decide another through unmarked substitution.

## 4. How to strengthen the private-adoption comparison

The [existing temporal test](CONSTITUTIVE-SIGNIFICANCE-COUNTERREVIEW.md#private-adoption-and-renunciation-provide-a-sharper-contrast) keeps patient burden and eligible relief fixed while a private commitment begins and ends. Its next step should record the grounds of each alleged reason, not only whether “a reason” is present.

| Candidate ground | Before adoption | During commitment | After private withdrawal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Patient-grounded baseline, if independently justified | Present | Present | Present |
| Reason constituted by this private commitment, if the power is legitimate | Absent | Present | Depends on the account of ending that commitment |
| Obligation generated through communication, reliance or authority | Determined by its own history | May arise through further events | Requires a separate account of release or discharge |

This is an analytical comparison, not a completed experiment or an endorsement of every table entry's existence. The first row states the direct candidate; the second grants a genuine power for comparison. Their coexistence is logically possible. Whether admitting a baseline changes the conditions under which a particular power can succeed must still be checked. Compatibility alone does not preserve every prior comparison among reasons. The exclusion rival must justify removing the first row, not simply establish the second.

The same discipline applies to withdrawal. Ending a ground that arose through adoption does not automatically end reasons with other grounds. Nor can the direct account simply insist that every private commitment persists after withdrawal. It must distinguish patient significance, voluntary attachment and any independent responsibilities that the history produced.

The clean relief case remains a better target than treating every optional project as identical. A stranger's unmet entertainment preference, a demanding personal relationship and an immediately available relief of severe unwanted suffering may have different grounds and demands. Moving between them requires an argument about relevance. That neither assumes that all suffering generates an overriding duty nor licenses dismissal of optional projects as valueless.

## 5. Consequence for unified grounding

The strong comparator leaves room for an account in which generated agents both recognize some reasons and create others. Unification need not mean that commitment has no constitutive role. It must instead explain which powers formed agents have, their conditions and limits, and how those powers relate to independently realized patient stakes.

This round therefore strengthens the opponent that the direct account must address, while narrowing what would count as its defeat. A legitimate commitment-created reason would defeat a theory that allowed agents only to discover fixed reasons. It would not alone defeat a baseline patient-grounded reason before commitment. Establishing an exclusive adoption condition would require a further argument for that specific scope. Conversely, merely proposing a baseline does not explain why a genuine self-directing power cannot alter its application.

Neither inspected work derives the relevant powers or given reasons from the minimal generative root. That leaves a grounding obligation, not a demonstrated impossibility of a common foundation. The next decisive comparison should justify the scope of the alleged power in the retained patient case: addition, modification, priority, or exclusion. These are different claims with different burdens of argument.
