**Finding:** two primary arguments offer substantive grounds for permission rather than mere descriptions of personal goods. Their grounds remain contestable, and neither establishes the whole proposed bridge. The readings sharpen where an affirmative argument must operate: explain why a relevant feature of the helper changes what may be demanded despite the patient's continuing claim. The task is not solved by declaring those features valuable or by observing that other people have behaved badly.

## 1. Scheffler: a moral role for the personal standpoint

**Samuel Scheffler, “Agent-Centered Prerogatives,”** excerpt from *The Rejection of Consequentialism*, chapters 1 and 3, in Jonathan Glover, ed., *Utilitarianism and Its Critics* (Macmillan, 1990), 223–229. [Primary anthology scan](https://www.utilitarianism.com/utilitarianism-critics.pdf). Directly read the complete excerpt, PDF pages 236–242, including its source note; the original book's complete chapter 3 was not obtained.

**Bounded source account:** At 224, the maximizing alternative already counts loss of projects and personal fulfillment. The competing argument at 225 grants the personal standpoint an independent moral role because persons act from their own commitments, rather than treating that standpoint solely as something to improve in aggregate. Permission gives practical expression to that feature of agency. Pages 226–229 explicitly limit the result: this supplies a principled motivation under one plausible moral attitude toward persons; it does not conclusively establish superiority over consequentialism. The relevant normative attitude therefore remains part of the argument, not a consequence of descriptive psychology alone.

**Samuel Scheffler, “Prerogatives without Restrictions,”** *Philosophical Perspectives* 6 (1992), 377–397. ## 2. Murphy: distinguish actual effects from the benchmark of demand

**Liam B. Murphy, “The Demands of Beneficence,”** *Philosophy & Public Affairs* 22(4) (1993), 267–292. [Primary article scan](https://pdf.stafforini.com/murphy-1993-demands-beneficence.pdf); [stable publication record](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2265468). Directly read all 26 pages and notes. This is the 1993 argument, not a report on subsequent book revisions.

**Bounded source account:** Pages 279–281 assess actions through actual outcomes but cap demanded sacrifice by what full compliance from now would require; they do not assume actual provision. Pages 283–284 deny that fairness alone supports the compliance condition. Pages 285–287 instead defend beneficence as a shared cooperative aim, distinct from reciprocal advantage; this supports several rules, leaving the exact cap independently intuitive. Page 289 permits extreme demands in adverse cases. Page 290, note 41, retains the objection that a small additional distant rescue remains compelling after modest contributions, appealing to comparative plausibility. Page 291 judges the cap's permission to leave a second child to drown, when another rescuer refuses, unacceptable. Page 292 proposes a special rescue obligation but leaves its relationship-ground unresolved. This supplies a conditional argument for some latitude under noncompliance, not a general guarantee of moderate demands or preserved authorship.

## 3. What would count as independent justification here?

The following is this study's analysis, not a further synopsis of these authors.

A permission argument needs more than a desired conclusion inserted into a budget. It should identify a ground that can be recognized independently of the particular refusal; explain why that ground bears on legitimate demand; and defend its application when an actual patient remains exposed. Independence cannot mean deriving an ethical priority without any normative premise. It means making that premise accountable rather than hiding it in a measurement, a designation of ownership, or a story of eventual collective success.

Consider an actor whose continuing relief work leaves almost no genuine choice over the shape of a life. The question is not simply whether this actor feels frustrated. Imagine the work is gratifying and does not cause major distress, yet any substantial departure from its assigned pattern is prohibited whenever it would reduce aid. If this arrangement is objectionable, its defect needs an explanation beyond its discomfort. Conversely, describing it as objectionable must not preclude someone freely making service their principal undertaking. The target is a claim about the scope of what others may morally demand, not a judgment that intensive altruistic lives are defective.

A candidate independent ground is each person's authority to participate substantially in determining their own ends. Its possible force comes from the relation between a person and their activity: replacing one person's own practical direction with an externally determined agenda changes that relation even where a welfare total rises. This is a positive normative proposal. It needs a defense of why that relation sometimes protects permission, who possesses the relevant authority, and when other claims override it. Agency as a causal capacity cannot supply those answers by itself.

The patient-facing objection remains decisive for testing this proposal. A patient can agree that another person's authorship matters while maintaining that saving it at the cost of their severe suffering is unjustified. The reply cannot be simply that the helper has an independent standpoint; so does the patient, whose continued suffering may obstruct its exercise more drastically. A successful defense must explain the asymmetry between permitting personal direction and requiring resource transfer without counting one person's life as inherently more significant.

## 4. A permission must survive two changes of case

**Change the source of unmet need while fixing its severity and the helper's burdens.** Suppose refusal by other capable helpers produces one shortfall, while unavoidable incapacity produces another. An authorship-based permission would need to explain why this causal difference matters, if it does. A responsibility-allocation argument may treat it as central, but then must explain why the difference changes what the innocent patient may demand. These are separate questions. Combining two grounds does not excuse leaving either connection undefended.

**Change the available response while preserving that shortfall.** Suppose an actor can retain substantial direction of life through a narrower commitment, an actual handoff or a more effective means. A permission against total displacement does not automatically license complete withdrawal. Conversely, a nominal handoff to people who will not act does not protect anyone. The relevant alternatives must remain the real alternatives faced by these people, including the costs of arranging and verifying a transfer.

These changes distinguish a reason for latitude from a convenient stopping rule. They also identify evidence an application would need: the actual cumulative pattern of commitments; which opportunities further help removes; whether the loss concerns mere preference or substantial practical direction; other responsibilities; and warranted expectations of provision. None of these observations measures moral authority directly. They constrain whether the proposed ground exists in the case being judged.

## 5. Integration result and next discriminating case

The next central test should hold actual need and honest evidence fixed while comparing three policies: continued exclusive availability, limited predictable assistance with substantial personal authorship, and complete withdrawal. The second policy must be defended both against the patient's objection that more help remains possible and against the third policy's claim to greater freedom. Success means justifying a particular intermediate permission without pretending that unmet needs disappear. The result may instead be that this case warrants unusually severe demands; plural worthwhile lives cannot be guaranteed in every possible resource distribution.

The additional supplied premises are universal patient standing, a normative account of authorship, an account of permissible means, and a principle connecting comparative claims to requirements or permissions. Existing generative mechanisms can explain how limited resources, continuity, recovery and shared provision work. They cannot silently choose the priority among these premises. A counterexample showing that the proposed permission protects only minor preference would narrow it. A defense showing that the patient's claim always defeats authorship under the stipulated severe conditions would reject this candidate route at that scope.

**Status:** two authors' arguments directly inspected; conditional permission grounds and unresolved normative steps identified. No universal threshold, empirical validation, formal proof, or completion of the ethical minimum is claimed.
