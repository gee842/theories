## Bounded primary findings

**2012.** GoBifo randomized 236 villages equally between treatment and control. The 2006–2009 package combined facilitation, committees, plans and grants averaging $4,667; inclusion requirements aimed to change ordinary decisions through practice. Reducing chiefs’ power was a researcher hypothesis, not an explicit program objective (pp. 1758–1763, 1790 n.14, 1799).

Assessors outside program implementation introduced behavioral tasks in May 2009: matching-grant vouchers, a collective choice between actual gifts, and allocation of a tarpaulin. Observation included speaking and apparent influence; unannounced follow-up occurred five months later. These were additional opportunities, not the program’s own participation records. They did not measure every form of authority (pp. 1769–1776).

The public-goods/services index increased **0.204 SD (SE 0.039)**, while the inclusion index estimate was **0.002 SD (SE 0.032)** (Table II, pp. 1786–1787). Formal committees and bank accounts increased without comparable measured changes in the behavioral tasks (Tables IV–V, pp. 1793–1797). The bundled design does not isolate facilitation’s contribution to infrastructure. Public gift delivery itself could restrict opportunities for capture, limiting that test (pp. 1775, 1799 n.16).

**2023 follow-up.** All 236 villages were revisited in 2016, eleven years after baseline. Exposure continued: 60 treatment villages were nonrandomly selected for additional grants around 2010, and lighter facilitation persisted. Estimates therefore do not isolate persistence after complete withdrawal (pp. 1353–1355).

Table 1 reports public-goods gains of **0.228 SD (SE 0.046)** and an institutional-family gain of **0.066 SD (SE 0.025; FDR q = 0.005; 61 indicators)**. Inclusion and local-authority estimates remained imprecise and statistically nonsignificant. A new task observed deliberation over nominees for an actual project competition; its aggregate estimate was **0.009 SD (SE 0.057)** (pp. 1354–1356, 1362–1363).

Indicator composition changed. The exact-panel institutional comparison was already positive in 2009, and its difference from 2016 was not significant. Thus these results do not establish an initial-null-to-delayed-success transition. Committee presence appears in both outcome families (pp. 1362–1363). The introduction instead reports 0.059 SD, SE 0.024, and 63 institutional indicators (p. 1350); this note follows the mutually consistent Table 1 and results section, without resolving that discrepancy.

## Our identification test and its ethical limit

Preserve the material improvements while testing the distinct claim that participants acquired greater influence over new decisions. Buildings, formal membership, deliberation and effective authority are different relations within the same intervention history. Neither infrastructure nor an aggregate institutional score automatically identifies all of them. Conversely, an inconclusive authority measure does not erase material gains or establish domination.

For the integral, the continuation law μ must describe the actual exposure lineage, including later interventions; the interpretation of G or g must retain which outcome relations its evidence supports. A change in indicators or revised appraisal is not itself a newly realized good. The cross-listed committee measure also cannot silently be converted into two independent ethical benefits, although its statistical inclusion in two research indices is not thereby an error.

This supplies a causal and measurement constraint. It neither establishes that participation is intrinsically good nor adjudicates the priority of relief after different past adversities. The normative comparison still needs its own grounds, and no benefit to the aid provider is required for the recipients’ gains to enter it. No universal direction, scalar welfare scale or completed ethical foundation follows.
