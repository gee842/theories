# Latitude while severe needs remain: adversarial review

6 October 2026. AI-authored review of **actual severe need and noncompliance → justified limits on an otherwise capable helper's demands**. This continues the [rescue review](RESCUE-REQUIREMENT-REVIEW.md) and [plurality argument](ETHICAL-MINIMUM-PLURALITY.md), supporting the [universal ethical minimum](../../../../theories/shengsheng-buxi-generative-existence/unified-2026-10-05/UNIVERSAL-ETHICAL-MINIMUM.md). Its contribution is a restrictive test for claim-preserving latitude, not a completed derivation of universal ethics.

**Result:** an explicit restriction on interpersonal aggregation can support a narrow permission where the helper's complete loss is graver than each patient's complete loss. Neither that comparison nor the restriction follows from universal standing. The demanding rival remains serious even with impeccable historical accounting. Two additional constraints follow at the existing conditional scope: patient histories must not be fragmented to manufacture weaker claims; and a permission preserving authorship does not erase a separate bounded rescue duty that leaves precisely that authorship intact.

## 1. Hold the hard case fixed

The shared case concerns one helper, H, with a final twelve-month opportunity for a substantial undertaking through which an independently worthwhile part of H's life can be realized. Examples could include sustained creative collaboration, inquiry or community participation; these examples illustrate possible goods, not established equivalences. Several substantively different worthwhile undertakings are feasible. Each needs a known sequence of uninterrupted sessions. Other substantial opportunities have already closed; later activity cannot restore this dimension in the stipulated history. Missing the requisite sessions irreversibly closes this opportunity; occasional spare fragments and routine recreation cannot replace its value. This is a supplied factual loss profile, not the verdict that protecting it is permissible.

During every candidate project session, a different patient is undergoing a severe, unwanted episode. An available response reliably ends that episode, but occupies enough of the session to make it unusable for the undertaking. Without relief the episode continues for several hours, seriously disrupting the patient's attention and activity. Each patient has only one such episode in the complete stipulated history. It produces no additional lasting injury, recurrent deprivation or loss of a major undertaking. These restrictions keep the comparison determinate; nonfatal suffering alone would not imply them.

H has already supplied substantial assistance. That history is retained through fatigue, opportunities already lost, and the remaining project's significance. H can continue responding without physical collapse. The project loss concerns an actual good, not incapacity. Care itself can realize worthwhile agency; the dispute concerns requiring this particular person to forfeit the remaining undertaking, not declaring caring lives intrinsically inferior.

Other competent people really refuse, including after available requests for help. No timely provider, reliable handoff, substitute technology or presently buildable institution can cover the contested sessions. No role, promise, causal responsibility or unjust holding independently assigns H these patients. All compared means respect patients' choices and other people's rights. Responsibilities to dependants and basic maintenance are already met. These are demanding idealizations, not empirical findings.

| Feasible allocation | Patient losses | Helper's position |
|---|---|---|
| Respond during every contested session | Every specified episode is relieved | H permanently loses the undertaking and its irreplaceable significance |
| Reserve the sessions actually needed for one undertaking; provide compatible assistance | Patients in those sessions endure their specified severe episodes; the others receive relief | H retains one substantial, self-directed continuation |
| Stop at a hypothetical fair share despite further compatible capacity | More episodes remain unrelieved | H retains the undertaking plus additional discretionary resources |

All accounts below evaluate this same profile. The second allocation is not called permissible in its description. Choosing it leaves real people in severe distress. The third cannot inherit justification merely because the second might be defensible.

## 2. The strongest demanding challenge

The demanding account need not deny projects, ignore lifetime burdens, equate persons with resources, assume fictional compliance or authorize coercion. It can accept every fact above and argue that the protection owed against severe suffering defeats the proposed permission. H's loss matters, but the omitted patients' claims also concern their lives. A patient's claim is not weakened merely because someone else should have helped first.

There are two serious versions. An individual-priority version judges even one patient's complete episode more morally urgent than H's complete opportunity loss. It can demand response without aggregating across patients. A cumulative version grants that H has the stronger individual objection, yet holds that sufficiently many distinct severe claims collectively defeat permission to leave those losses unrelieved. Rejecting the first comparison would not defeat the second.

This reconstructs the priority problem identified by the [source assessment](RESCUE-REQUIREMENT-SOURCES.md#2-ashford-recognizing-the-helpers-whole-life-does-not-settle-priority). Its Ashford reading is delegated evidence; this reviewer does not claim a new primary reading or attribute either reconstruction verbatim to her. The important challenge survives full accounting: a theory can count the lost life project and still require its surrender.

The reply cannot simply say H deserves a life. Patients do too. It must explain why this particular kind and scale of control over H's future supports permission despite these particular losses. Conversely, the demanding position must defend its priority or aggregation rule; severe suffering does not analytically carry unlimited requiring force.

## 3. What the proposed limiting principle adds

The strongest restricted proposal has two independent parts:

1. In the specified comparison, losing H's irreplaceable, life-integrating undertaking is graver for H than each affected patient's complete bounded loss is for that patient.
2. Adding separate individually weaker claims does not, by itself, require destruction of that substantially stronger life-integrating good.

The first is a substantive evaluative judgment. Duration, the labels “authorship” and “severe,” and the number of past rescues do not prove it. The second restricts aggregation and requiring force. Separate existence alone does not prove that restriction. They jointly support the proposed permission only with the inherited account of person-grounded objections and independently legitimate means.

A positive defense says that requiring one person's irreversible loss needs adequate justification to that person; a collection of individually weaker objections need not supply it merely by being numerous. This gives the permission a content that a private preference lacks. The demanding answer remains that patients' equal standing permits their serious claims to combine, and that the protection proposed for H then leaves too much avoidable suffering. Neither side is logically inconsistent. The affirmative principle is therefore a reviewable philosophical advance, while its truth remains disputed.

It can coexist with [bounded rescue](RESCUE-REQUIREMENT-ARGUMENT.md#3-the-explicit-bridge-from-comparison-to-requirement). There the helper's complete objection is a brief replaceable interruption, and the patient's objection is substantially stronger. Here the proposed comparison reverses the individual ranking and supplies a defended protection against aggregation. No general permission to ignore need is implied. If one patient's full loss becomes stronger, this particular argument for latitude no longer decides the case.

## 4. Four shortcut rules fail different tests

**A personal-weight factor.** Multiplying the helper's good by a constant can produce a preferred numerical verdict once commensurable magnitudes and an aggregation rule have been supplied. It does not justify either. Any finite multiplier can be overwhelmed by enough positive additions under unbounded additive maximization. A lexical multiplier would instead prohibit tradeoffs requiring separate defense. A simple factor also fails to distinguish an equally valued voluntary project from independently owed care unless those responsibilities are explicitly represented. Weighting is a possible substantive theory, not a derivation of latitude from inclusion.

**A fair-share cap.** Completing what H would owe if everyone complied identifies injustice by defaulting helpers. It does not make their absent relief available or explain why patients must bear the remainder. The third allocation exposes the excess: stopping earlier than needed to preserve the very good invoked for protection leaves further patients unaided for a different reason. A cap requires its own account of the entitlement it creates, including why that entitlement defeats an urgent present claim. Fair distribution remains a valuable institutional objective without settling present residual duty.

**An authorship threshold.** A threshold can identify when a specified undertaking becomes impossible. This is a factual constraint, not a moral permission boundary. The stipulated session requirement supports that undertaking; it is no universal allotment for every worthwhile life. If the same sessions instead protect a replaceable minor project, the comparison changes. If one patient's loss becomes a lifelong ruin, an absolute floor would continue protecting H irrespective of that change. A defensible defeasible protection must address both variations rather than treating the threshold as self-validating.

**Structural non-domination.** No official or patient exercises discretionary power over H in the shared case. The demanding theory can posit a moral obligation without granting anyone authority to command, inspect or seize H's resources. The loss of authorship is still real, but it is not automatically domination. Conversely, a regime could preserve generous free time while granting an official arbitrary power over the remaining time. Non-domination identifies additional wrongs in such variants; it cannot alone settle the original omission. Converting all demanding obligations into conscription would hide the disputed inference.

## 5. A new constraint: count patients' histories symmetrically

Replace the stream of distinct one-episode patients with one patient who undergoes those same episodes repeatedly. Preserve H's schedule, capacity and project loss. The patient's cumulative disruption now destroys relationships, concentration or another life-integrating undertaking. A rule against combining separate people's weaker claims supplies no ground for splitting this person's own continuing history into tiny claims.

The change need not make H's sacrifice obligatory; comparative judgment and any other protection remain necessary. It does invalidate the original restricted argument whenever its premise that each patient's complete loss is weaker no longer holds. Both people now bring whole-life objections. The permission cannot survive solely by repeating that each encounter is individually bounded.

This yields **symmetric historical accounting**: identify the actual subjects first, then retain each subject's relevant complete burden, including repeated and anticipatory effects. Do not aggregate the helper longitudinally while atomizing the patient's life. It also blocks descriptions of a lengthy untreated condition as a new patient-claim every minute. The restriction is substantive progress because it excludes an apparent latitude proof even if every local episode remains nonfatal.

## 6. A residual rescue duty survives a defended permission

Consider a controlled addition to the shared case. After H reserves the needed sessions, one extra patient's severe episode can be ended through a safe thirty-second response during an otherwise replaceable interval. It changes no project session, rest, privacy, bodily boundary, responsibility, future availability commitment or remaining option. Across the actual history this added response has only the bounded cost already specified in the rescue principle. No other provider is available.

Even if retaining the undertaking is permissible, the earlier principle requires this additional relief. The defense of the protected sessions cannot explain refusing an act that leaves exactly their protected goods available. Past contributions and a declared service cap create no immunity within this stipulated comparison.

Call this the **no-excess-exclusion constraint**: permission justified by preserving an identified good does not cover a further omission when an independently required rescue preserves that same good and all other relevant protections. This is conditional inheritance from the existing rescue principle, not a new rule maximizing benefit at equal scalar cost. A different method's hidden surveillance, attention demand, risk or recurring expectation could remove it from scope. Nor does the result determine whether the original project reservation is permissible.

The constraint separates a genuine limited permission from a blanket exemption. It also supplies a determinate adverse verdict even while the central ranking remains contested. Its force comes from the patient's surviving claim.

## 7. Disposition and remaining bridge

Accept the narrow claim-limitation argument as an explicit, disputed ethical proposal if it keeps both normative additions visible and applies complete-history accounting to every subject. Reject any universal stopping rule inferred from cumulative cost, fair-share completion, a project label or domination language alone. Preserve the demanding counterposition and the suffering left under the proposed permission.

The supplied inputs remain patient identification, effects, histories, means, alternative-provider failure, the helper's independently warranted good, comparative gravity and the restriction on aggregation. Generative organization, learned preferences and successful institutions do not establish their moral authority. New evidence that alternatives exist changes the factual case; a stronger individual patient claim defeats the present comparison; an independently justified aggregation principle could overturn the permission even when that comparison stands.

This is conceptual argument and collaborating-AI review, not empirical evidence, proof-assistant verification or external peer review. The next bridge is to defend the restricted aggregation principle against the demanding counterposition without fragmenting patients, and to identify how justified allocations preserve both remaining assistance and genuine plural choice.
