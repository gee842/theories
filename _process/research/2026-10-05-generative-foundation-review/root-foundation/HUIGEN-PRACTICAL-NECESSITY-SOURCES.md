# From a center's reasons to reasons for shared action

6 October 2026. AI-authored primary-source investigation of the hardest transition in [慧根](../../../../theories/becoming/chapters/HUIGEN.md): **objective recognition of each center's own significance and reasons → justified reasons governing action toward other centers**, including patients who cannot communicate or reciprocate. The intended result remains a derivation within the entire necessary root, not replacement of that goal by a subjective preference for concern.

**Finding:** three serious positive routes are available. Agency can be argued to commit an actor to claims others must respect; rational valuation can be argued to recognize a creature's good beyond its ability to legislate; suffering can be argued to supply an objective reason directly. The decisive question is the **ground and scope of the reason**. Recognizing another's reason, transmitting its representation and acknowledging that reason's authority over one's own action are different achievements. The sources identify arguments for the last step; they do not render it automatic.

### 1.1 Gewirth: necessary goods and claims against others

**Primary text:** Alan Gewirth, “The Basis and Content of Human Rights,” *Georgia Law Review* 13 (1979), 1143–1170. [Journal scan](https://stephankinsella.com/wp-content/uploads/texts/ga-l-rev-1979_2b.pdf). Inspected printed pp. 1148–1158, especially 1151–1154 and 1157–1158. Web PDF extraction succeeded; direct download returned 403. No claim to have read *Reason and Morality* in full.

Gewirth denies that a third-person statement of someone's necessary good alone entails rights. His stronger argument begins within purposive agency: necessary conditions of acting must be endorsed, allegedly requiring claims to others' noninterference. He then identifies agency as the sufficient ground and generalizes the resulting rights. The crucial entitlement inference appears explicitly on p. 1153; it must be assessed rather than hidden in “must.” His treatment of reduced or potential agency proportionally adjusts generic rights. Consequently, this text cannot simply be cited for equal standing of every nonagent. Its strength is a serious necessity argument with an identifiable transfer step; its patient scope is a separate burden.

### 1.2 Korsgaard: passive standing without reciprocal lawgiving

**Primary text:** Christine M. Korsgaard, *Fellow Creatures: Our Obligations to the Other Animals* (Oxford University Press, 2018). [Publisher record](https://academic.oup.com/book/4616); [accessible primary-text PDF](https://notebook.colinmclear.net/ox-hugo/korsgaard2018_Fellow_Creatures.pdf). Inspected §§2.1.3–2.1.9 and 8.2–8.7. The reflowed PDF has 480 pages; §§8.4–8.5 occupy PDF pages 264–278, not corresponding print pages. Section 8.5.3 was also visually checked.

Korsgaard distinguishes creatures' experienced good from mere functional benefit. Her argument separates active capacity to legislate from passive standing as an end. Rational beings make the relevant value presupposition, but its content need not concern rational beings alone: our original valuation concerns our good as creatures. This supports extending standing to sentient animals without reciprocity. However, §8.4 already connects rational choice with valuing an end as part of a universally shared good. That is a substantive practical-rationality premise. Her argument cannot be obtained merely by replacing “organism” with “organized system”; experienced good and the justification of shared value both matter.

### 1.3 Nagel: suffering as an objective reason, without pretending a consistency proof

**Primary text:** Thomas Nagel, “The Limits of Objectivity,” Tanner lectures delivered in 1979, published in *The Tanner Lectures on Human Values* 1 (1980), 75–139. [Official lecture PDF](https://tannerlectures.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/nagel80.pdf). Inspected lecture II, §§1–4 and 7–9, printed pp. 97–102 and 107–114; lecture III's distinction at pp. 119–125. Web extraction succeeded; direct download returned 403.

Nagel explicitly distinguishes objective agent-relative reasons from agent-neutral reasons. He argues that suffering's character supports reasons for others to want relief, independently of prior sympathy or personal advantage. He treats this as a normative judgment and says its denial is not thereby logically inconsistent. On pp. 111–113 he distinguishes strong evidence for that judgment from entailment. This provides a positive realist route without reducing value to arbitrary preference. It does not establish that every personal interest has the same force for everybody, or derive all moral conclusions from factual objectivity. The inspected argument is about suffering; its extension to nonhuman sufferers requires identifying the relevant similarity.

These are three distinct primary arguments, not mutually confirming empirical studies. The access date is 6 October 2026. Only the selected passages are claimed as inspected. ## 2. What a positive derivation must establish

The following is our reconstruction of the research problem, not an attribution of one common doctrine to the authors.

Let \(V(p,h)\) mean that occurrence \(h\) is independently identified as adverse for patient \(p\). Let \(L(p,h)\) mean that this supplies a reason from that patient's standpoint to oppose \(h\). Let \(R(a,p,h)\) mean that it supplies actor \(a\) a reason against imposing or permitting \(h\), with the relevant opportunity, responsibility and competing considerations specified.

The intended chain is

\[
V(p,h)\ \Longrightarrow\ L(p,h)\ \Longrightarrow\ R(a,p,h).
\]

An accurate registry of all \(V\) and \(L\) does not by itself prove the second implication. Nor does it prove that the implication is false. The question is whether the ground of \(L\) has an authority confined to its first-person bearer, or whether that restriction itself lacks justification once the ground is correctly understood.

The strongest positive attempt should therefore establish **why the feature doing the justificatory work is the patient's adverse condition**, rather than merely the identity relation between patient and deliberator. It must support three claims:

1. The adverse condition supplies an actual reason, not merely a prediction of aversion or a behaviorally effective signal.
2. The source of that reason is identified fully enough to determine whether another actor's position changes its validity, its weight, or only who can act upon it.
3. Where the relevant source persists, a reason cannot be erased solely by changing the name or location of the deliberator. Any exception must cite a relevant condition, not silently suppress the reason.

The third claim is a conditional consequence of a successful grounding argument. Calling it “reason conservation” would not itself establish the first two. In particular, declaring that harm is an actor-independent reason would build the desired bridge into the definition. The work is to justify that content from the proposed necessary root.

This route reaches a more exact destination than “everyone should want the same thing.” A valid reason can be present without being decisive. Relationship, responsibility, available means and conflicting claims can affect what one ought to do. Recognition of standing does not entail aggregating every center into one interchangeable total.

## 3. The positive case and its strongest tests

The immediate candidate is severe experienced suffering with a feasible alternative that removes it while preserving the actor's legitimate project and imposing no compensating burden on another patient. This is an argument-isolating case, not a claim that every real conflict has such an alternative.

Why pursue it? A reason connected directly with an adverse experience has a clearer proposed source than an arbitrary chosen project. Its bearing on the patient is not manufactured by a public vote, a scientific institution's survival or a reward channel. An actor's indifference therefore cannot by itself show that no reason exists. The question is whether recognizing the condition correctly also warrants recognition of its practical force beyond the patient.

Use the following changes independently rather than collapsing them into one imaginative role reversal:

| Change, with other relevant conditions retained | What the argument must explain |
|---|---|
| The patient loses speech while the adverse experience persists | Why an inability to report would change evidence, but not automatically remove the underlying good or reason |
| The patient cannot deliberate, while the adverse experience persists | Whether the ground concerns having a good or being able to formulate its claim |
| The patient cannot help, threaten or reward the actor | Whether the reason is grounded in the patient or only in reciprocal benefit |
| The deliberator changes while the same patient's condition remains | The central transfer from an indexed reason to its authority for another actor |
| The proposed relief burdens a second patient | How standing survives while an all-things-considered verdict becomes contested |

The first three changes test patient scope. They cannot by themselves prove the fourth. The fourth tests the disputed normative transfer. The fifth prevents a successful easy-case argument from silently becoming an absolute instruction to remove every harm regardless of consequences.

An especially strong counterposition recognizes every patient's experience and every patient's own reason, keeps every fact available for correction, but denies that those reasons govern its own conduct. To challenge that position, show a failure of the practical justification it claims, or show why the supposedly indexed restriction misidentifies the reason's source. Showing that the actor is unsympathetic or produces destructive outcomes is insufficient as a logical refutation. Conversely, its coherent operation is insufficient to establish the absence of moral reasons: coherence and justified action are different standards.

## 4. Three open proof obligations sharpened by the sources

**From a necessary condition to an entitlement.** If an agent requires some condition to achieve an end, it follows that the end cannot be achieved without that condition. It does not follow by that fact alone that everyone owes it to the agent. A successful constitutive argument must explain why the agent's commitment is a claim with authority, rather than only a demand or instrumental necessity. This is the first place to test a derivation from agency.

**From endorsing an end to publicly valid reasons.** Reflective endorsement can bind a deliberator across a change of immediate desire. It remains to establish why its authority extends to other deliberators. A temporal commitment within one practical life and an interpersonal demand differ in more than the labels on their subjects. The whole-root theory should explain their common form without erasing that difference.

**From the character of suffering to shared practical validity.** A realist account can defend a moral judgment through the quality of its evidence and the weakness of competing explanations. It need not concede that every judgment lacking a deductive proof is subjective. But a well-warranted normative starting point and a demonstrated condition indispensable to every adequate scientific philosophy are different achievements. The former may be progress toward the latter; it must retain its actual epistemic status.

These obligations identify the strongest arguments to pursue. They are not a verdict that concern is arbitrary or that a common root is impossible. Nor can philosophical source agreement establish the bridge in place of an argument.

## 5. How this advances the entire-root programme

The [closure theorem](HUIGEN-GENERATIVE-CLOSURE.md) tells us how necessity propagates after the root and rules are justified. This investigation locates a candidate substantive addition: a justified, patient-grounded source of reasons, with an independently defended account of their authority across deliberators. It also locates two restrictions that should survive any successful derivation:

- Capacity to make or communicate a normative claim need not be the property that grounds being owed consideration.
- Universal validity need not mean that every reason is interchangeable, equally weighty or equally action-guiding for every actor in every context.

The next constructive task is to formulate the smallest practical-validity claim that genuinely includes the dispensable, noncommunicating patient, derive its commitments, and compare it with the fully informed agent-relative counterposition. Where joint justification is claimed, test whether a proposed verdict actually answers the admitted grounds of every affected standpoint; where no such claim is made, investigate what nonetheless makes the patient's ground authoritative. Those are connected but different questions.

Formation and warrant must then meet: the system needs a justified route for recognizing the patient and condition, preserving their identity, admitting inconvenient evidence, and allowing a supported reason to affect action. These implementations cannot supply moral authority solely through causal success. Equally, their failure cannot erase a reason independently justified.
