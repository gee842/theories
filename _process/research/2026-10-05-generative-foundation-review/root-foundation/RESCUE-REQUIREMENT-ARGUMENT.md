# From moral favor to a bounded rescue requirement

6 October 2026. AI-authored argument for [the universal ethical minimum](../../../../theories/becoming/chapters/UNIVERSAL-ETHICAL-MINIMUM.md). Named connection: **objective patient-grounded moral favor → requiring force in a bounded serious rescue, with principled exceptions across an actual life**. This develops the [plurality study](ETHICAL-MINIMUM-PLURALITY.md). It supplies a positive philosophical argument and an explicit additional normative bridge; it claims neither universal necessity nor derivation from causal organization. Source comparison and counterreview are separate companion work.

**Proposed advance:** severe unwanted suffering can defeat a permission to decline a brief effective response when the actor's strongest contrary grounds are minor inconvenience and unrestricted discretion. The argument concerns how separate persons' claims justify permissions. It does not equate the largest benefit with a duty, or a duty with permission to compel. Its vulnerable step is an openly defended restriction on unconditional personal discretion.

## 1. Specify the case before evaluating it

Consider a competent adult who encounters an unrelated person undergoing severe, unwanted suffering. A known, safe mechanism can end that episode if the adult performs a two-minute action. The patient requests it. The adult has access, understands the situation and can perform the action without trespass, deception, bodily intrusion, significant risk, forgone care of dependants or breach of another commitment. No other response will arrive in time. The adult would otherwise continue an enjoyable, interruptible activity. Across their actual history, the interruption neither adds to an exhausting pattern nor closes a scarce opportunity to sustain a relationship, project or self-directed life.

These are stipulated features of a conceptual case, not empirical findings. The action's effectiveness, the patient's condition and consent would need evidence in an application. “Two minutes” is illustrative, not an ethical threshold. “Severe” identifies an experience substantially disrupting the patient's ability to attend, act and live through the episode; “minor” describes the helper's limited delay and retained opportunities. Neither term means “already sufficient to make rescue obligatory.”

The live alternatives are timely response or knowingly leaving the episode unrelieved. If several responses work, the argument can require timely relief without requiring one uniquely specified motion. There is no prior contract, responsibility for causing the episode, threatened penalty or expected return to the helper.

## 2. Why the comparison favors relief

The inherited patient-ground is that this person's actual burden morally favors an eligible response independently of the adult's adopted ends. Its ultimate grounding remains under investigation. Add the separately defended recognition that the helper's enjoyment and direction of activity are also goods.

Both persons therefore have something to say about the choice. The patient stands to escape an overwhelming experience; the helper stands to retain an uninterrupted interval. Relief protects the patient's basic capacity to undergo this part of life without severe distress while leaving the helper's activity, relationships and continuing authority largely available. Refusal preserves the exact timing of one chosen activity while the grave episode continues.

The comparison does not require adding welfare across a population. It compares the character of two losses borne by actual persons. The proposed priority follows from what the losses do to each person's life: severe interference with ordinary living is a stronger objection than a short postponement that leaves the relevant activity and self-direction available. This is a substantive evaluative judgment, not a theorem from the losses' descriptions. A theory could deny that priority, but would then need to explain why preserving this uninterrupted interval answers this patient's grave complaint.

Ownership of the preference does not establish that answer. The patient's experience belongs to them just as the helper's activity belongs to the helper. Differentiation grounds both claims. What could favor the helper categorically is a further prerogative attached to controlling their own conduct. That is the serious rival, examined below.

## 3. The explicit bridge from comparison to requirement

Comparative superiority alone leaves open supererogation: an act can be better without being required. This argument therefore adds the following **defeasible comparative requirement principle**:

> In a choice about relieving present severe unwanted suffering, where an available response faces a substantially weaker person-grounded objection than every available nonresponse, and no independently supported right, responsibility or personal prerogative protects nonresponse, the stronger objection defeats permission to decline. Timely response is then morally required.

“Objection” here means the goods, burdens or claims that count against an option for an affected person. It does not mean whatever someone declares unacceptable. “Independently supported” requires a defense of the alleged protection beyond repeating that refusal is permitted. The principle concerns moral permission; it does not define competent agency, rational consistency or social membership by agreement with the conclusion.

This is a genuine normative addition. It rejects a theory on which another person's interests can always justify helping yet never defeat refusal. Its defense has three parts. First, moral permission purports to justify a choice in relation to affected persons, not merely report which movement remains under an actor's control. Second, those persons have standing as the bearers of the goods that make the choice morally significant. Third, admitting their claims while giving an actor's discretion an unconditional veto makes the severity and avoidability of their losses incapable of constraining that actor at all. The account proposed here instead requires a ground for such immunity, just as it requires a ground for intervention.

This does not prove the principle to someone who rejects interpersonal justificatory accountability. It explains what work the principle does and why it is preferable within an ethic that treats each actual life as capable of constraining others' choices. Equal standing alone does not entail it. Nor does the unexplained word “reasonable” perform the inference.

Applied to the specified case: relief has the weaker personal objection; independent constraints on that means are absent; and the strongest remaining alleged protection is unlimited discretion. If that protection fails, as argued next, the principle defeats permission to decline. Refusal is wrongful under this account even if the helper feels no concern. Motivation, culpability and others' authority remain distinct.

## 4. Why ordinary self-direction does not supply an unlimited veto

A strong rival grants the patient's reason while protecting a person's authority to decide whether their activity will serve others. It need not claim that suffering is worthless or that selfish pleasure outweighs everything. It can say that reasons belonging to another person do not acquire requiring force over one's independently controlled conduct without responsibility or consent.

The positive reply begins by accepting the ground of that protection: a life can lose something important when its course is continuously assigned by others. But a protection should track what warrants it. The bounded interruption here leaves the helper choosing projects, relationships and future activity; it assigns no occupation, continuing dependence or comprehensive life plan. The helper retains the ability and standing to direct a life while facing a particular constraint within it.

That distinction is not the claim that small infringements of rights never matter. Bodily integrity, promises and protected expression may constrain means despite small measured costs. Such grounds need their own assessment and are excluded from this case only by explicit stipulation. Rather, the proposed general self-direction ground does not by itself explain why every short interval must remain morally optional irrespective of another person's grave condition. A categorical veto protects more than the continuing self-direction invoked to justify it.

The rescue argument consequently imposes a burden of justification on that excess. If a rival can establish an independent right never to perform unchosen aid, the present bridge will not defeat it merely by pointing to benefits. Without that added right, bounded requirement better preserves both persons as centers of life: one sustains a limited interruption; the other escapes a grave episode. This is an argumentative preference, not a demonstration that the rival is logically incoherent.

## 5. Principled exceptions and repeated demands

An exception changes a premise that supports requiring force. A patient's valid refusal, an unsafe means or a violation of another person's protected claim defeats the proposed route. A substantial risk to the helper, collision with a dependant's care, or loss of an irreplaceable personal opportunity changes the comparison. A reliable alternative rescuer can remove the need for this actor's intervention. These facts need assessment; naming “consent,” “risk,” “project” or “alternative” does not automatically establish the exception.

The requirement is to provide an adequate permissible response; it need not identify a unique least burdensome means or forbid voluntarily choosing a costlier one. If a brief call secures equally reliable timely relief, this argument does not require prolonged personal care. If the call merely passes the burden into a known gap, it is not an adequate substitute. Means must be assessed without inventing provision.

Repeated requests require a different case description. A new patient does not erase the helper's accumulated sleep loss, fractured relationships, foregone commitments or anticipation of permanent availability. The objection to another interruption can include its marginal contribution to that continuing pattern. A whole history of nominally brief services may threaten precisely the self-direction that one isolated service preserves.

Yet cumulative accounting alone establishes no stopping rule. A past contribution is not a banked exemption from every subsequent emergency; a hypothetical endless sequence does not make today's actual small burden large. When ongoing demands really foreclose a worthwhile life, the self-direction ground becomes stronger, and the original bounded-case argument no longer settles the verdict. Other claim priorities, burden sharing and institutional provision must then be justified. This study offers no universal percentage, quota or guaranteed painless exit from catastrophe.

A demanding rival can acknowledge lifetime losses of projects and relationships and still judge the patients' remaining grave losses stronger. Cumulative accounting defeats only the argument that repeatedly resets costs; it does not defeat this rival. Similarly, fair sharing cannot assume that absent helpers actually comply. Unfairness to the conscientious actor does not automatically license leaving the residual burden with endangered patients. Robust latitude under chronic severe need therefore remains open, even if bounded rescue is required.

There is also no inference from “perform this response now” to “remain permanently available in this location.” Requiring permanent availability has further costs and needs an additional argument. Deliberate avoidance of foreseeable claims raises its own question; it cannot be licensed merely by changing locations or automatically prohibited by iterating the isolated rescue. A mature account must evaluate the proposed continuing arrangement, including preventable burden concentration, while retaining each abandoned patient's loss.

## 6. Requirement is not coercive authorization

The verdict here is that the helper ought to respond and lacks moral permission to decline within the stipulated comparison. It does not establish a patient's standing to command every detail, an official's power to punish, or a bystander's permission to seize the helper's body or property.

Enforcement adds agents, means and consequences to the comparison. Its legitimacy needs grounds concerning authority, evidence, proportionality, procedure and the rights of those coerced. Some duties may eventually prove enforceable; nothing here establishes that no rescue duty can be enforced. The narrower point is that neither enforceability nor permission for a particular coercive act follows from duty alone. Blame also requires attention to knowledge, control and excuse, beyond the idealized case.

## 7. Derivation ledger and next test

| Element | Status |
|---|---|
| Actual severe burden, efficacy, consent, bounded costs and absent alternatives | Supplied conceptual-case premises; applications require identification |
| Stranger-directed objective moral favor | Inherited defended candidate; universal grounding remains open |
| Helper's enjoyment, relationships and self-direction as goods | Separately defended evaluative premises |
| Grave loss has stronger person-grounded objection than this limited delay | Defended comparative judgment; no common reward scale assumed |
| Stronger objection can defeat permission absent independently supported protection | Explicit additional normative principle defended in §3 |
| Ordinary self-direction supplies no categorical exemption in the bounded case | Defended scope argument; unconditional-right rival remains live |
| Timely rescue is required | Conditional philosophical conclusion from the above |
| Compulsion, universal thresholds or complete ethical necessity | Not established |

The generative account contributes sensitivity to actual differentiated centers, costs and histories. Those descriptions do not establish the normative bridge. An analyst's reward, a controller's learned preference or an intervention's survival payoff does not certify the duty.

What would change this account is a defensible categorical refusal right, a showing that apparently bounded demands necessarily undermine the self-direction they claim to preserve, or a better account separating permissible optionality from severe unrelieved loss. The next substantive comparison should test a case with a genuine nontrivial self-direction claim against urgent relief, while defending the requirement principle against the strongest optionality rival. This bounded result advances determinate guidance; the full universal-minimum objective remains unfinished.
