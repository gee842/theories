## Primary reading

Jessica Isserow, “Mercy: A Right and a Privilege,” *Noûs*, first published 12 July 2026, [publisher HTML and DOI](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/nous.70056). Accessed 6 October 2026. Read the complete main argument, §§1–6; endnotes and cited works are not claimed as a complete independent review. No source bytes were downloaded.

**Bounded source synopsis:** Isserow defends a moralized account of mercy involving voluntarily forgoing harm one has a moral privilege to impose, out of other-regarding concern (§4.1). A beneficiary can lack a claim to leniency while leniency is admirable (§§3.3, 4.3). This differs from simply having the power to harm or acting less badly than prevailing practice (§3.2). Third parties can usurp a victim's moral power even when they are not judges; she leaves a broadened treatment of public mercy open (§5). Her account develops substantive moral and conceptual judgments; it does not derive universal patient-directed favor from descriptive facts.

## Application to the minimum

The following distinctions are this investigation's application. They preserve the [minimum's existing separation](../../../../theories/shengsheng-buxi-generative-existence/unified-2026-10-05/UNIVERSAL-ETHICAL-MINIMUM.md) of favor, obligation and authority; they are not new proofs of those distinctions or conclusions attributed wholesale to the source.

**A reason to act and the motive of an act differ.** A classification that reserves “merciful” for actions performed from concern cannot settle whether relief had moral favor before that concern was adopted. An initially indifferent actor may have a reason without acting on it. Conversely, an actor can adopt a compassionate motive while lacking the permission to carry out a particular intervention. The intended C2 inquiry concerns prospective favor for a response, not whether a completed action earns a virtue label.

**No claim to relief does not settle absence of favor.** If a patient has no enforceable claim that an actor remit a burden, this establishes neither the moral pointlessness of remission nor its moral merit. The merit needs a ground beyond the permission. Describing the act as supererogatory would already affirm a favorable evaluation; using that description as evidence without defending it would beg the question.

**The authority of one route does not transfer automatically to another.** A claimant's justified power to waive a claim is different from a bystander's ability to prevent its enforcement. If the proposed relief consists in that waiver, the bystander cannot acquire the needed authority merely by intending a benefit. The hard test must therefore identify the actual act: supplying an available remedy, remitting an obligation one may remit, or interfering with someone else's claim. These can have similar experiential consequences while differing in permission. A real moral reason to relieve would still not establish a right to choose any of these means.

This creates a useful check on the proposed desert experiment. “Independently permitted relief” cannot simply mean that some sympathetic actor has the physical power to reduce suffering. Permission must address whatever authority or claim that very response affects. Equally, a theory's prohibition on a specific unauthorized waiver cannot be relabeled a finding that the patient's suffering supplies no moral consideration anywhere. The response must stay fixed when testing whether its favorable aspect survives.

## Consequence for a nondual foundation

Removing a primitive I does not make authorization, wrongdoing or differentiated consequences disappear. In a generative explanation they would be relations whose formation and justification must be accounted for within the history. A proposed responsibility account might use capacities, choices and consequences without positing an unconditioned soul. Nonduality therefore cannot dismiss the desert rival merely by denying such a soul; nor can the rival establish deserved suffering just by pointing to a generated controller.

The exact ethical dispute remains whether the allegedly deserved adverse undergoing has a surviving patient-directed moral contribution to the specified relieving act. Neither a virtue definition, a right to punish, the absence of a claim to mercy nor the disappearance of a metaphysically independent owner answers it. This source comparison improves the test's discrimination while leaving its normative ground open.
