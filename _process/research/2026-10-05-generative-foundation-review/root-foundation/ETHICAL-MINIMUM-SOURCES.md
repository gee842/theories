# Ethical minimum and plural worthwhile lives: primary-source comparison

6 October 2026. AI-authored selective research note for Ren's goal of **establishing a universal ethical minimum compatible with plural worthwhile lives**. Named connection: **patient-grounded reasons → a legitimate practical minimum without total optimization**. This note extends [practical uptake](PRACTICAL-UPTAKE-SOURCES.md), which already distinguishes positive justification from requirement. It does not repeat that dossier's Darwall/Gert summaries or the [earlier necessity routes](HUIGEN-PRACTICAL-NECESSITY-SOURCES.md).

**Finding:** the argument for plural lives can be affirmative. A helper's continuing life contains things that can deserve protection for their own sake. Recognizing them need not delete the patient's reason for relief. The new work is to justify which competing claims limit a requirement, under the actual distribution of need, capacity and responsibility. The sources support this research direction; they do not prove universal stranger-directed reasons or furnish a finished threshold.

## 1. Wolf: moral improvement and comprehensive life guidance differ

**Susan Wolf, “Moral Saints,” *The Journal of Philosophy* 79(8) (1982), 419–439.** [Accessible journal scan](https://pdf.stafforini.com/wolf-1982-moral-saints.pdf); [original stable record](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2026228). Inspected printed 419–426 and 431–439 through web text extraction. Printed 427–430 were not systematically reviewed. The scan's last page begins a different article after Wolf's conclusion; that subsequent article is excluded.

**Bounded synopsis:** Wolf argues that nonmoral excellence and concrete attachments can supply positive reasons for a life, rather than excuses for failing to maximize moral worth. Her response does not cap moral goodness: further altruism can remain admirable without furnishing the best comprehensive personal ideal. She retains the practical significance of others' equal reality while resisting morality's operation as a universal filter. Her footnote 6 cautions that the existence of required and optional acts does not establish two fixed sets of principles that invariably classify them. Her conclusion is a philosophical defense of plural evaluation, not a measured aid threshold or a proof that all refusal is permissible. Its challenge is to justify forms of life substantively, rather than protecting whatever an actor happens to enjoy.

## 2. Cullity: examine the grounds of beneficence and total burden

**Garrett Cullity, “Asking Too Much,” *The Monist* 86(3) (2003), 402–418.** [Author-hosted journal scan](https://garrettcullity.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/Asking-Too-Much.pdf); [original stable record](https://www.jstor.org/stable/27903832). Inspected the complete main text, 402–416, and notes 417–418, through web and local PDF extraction. This is the 2003 article, not the 2004 book or a later revision.

**Bounded synopsis:** Cullity argues that some interests grounding duties of assistance concern relationships, achievement and other personally fulfilling pursuits. A demand making all such pursuits wrongful would undermine that justification. He rejects assessing every successive rescue as if it were the only one: aggregate sacrifice matters. This does not permit unlimited luxury, and friendships resist simple substitution. The conclusion concerns chronic-need beneficence; other duties may be more demanding. Crucially, page 414 expressly leaves the permissible-burden threshold at a common-sense starting point, acknowledging that agreement does not defend it. The paper argues from accepted assistance and impartiality premises; it does not derive them from neutral facts. It supplies a principled objection to endless iteration, not an established universal donation percentage.

## 3. Positive reconstruction and necessary corrections

The following is this study's proposed argument and criticism, not a further summary of either author.

**Candidate continuity of standing.** If a helper is also an affected being, giving the patient's burden standing cannot require treating the helper's actual losses as normatively nonexistent. A response policy must keep both centers identifiable. This is a consistency constraint on a supplied inclusive standing premise; it does not show equal weights, equal entitlements to scarce resources, or permission to refuse any particular rescue.

**Candidate continuity of worthwhile pursuit.** Suppose a particular pursuit is independently justified as worthwhile, and another person's access to that pursuit can ground a reason for assistance. A theory that prohibits every instance of that pursuit needs to explain how those judgments fit together. This tests a total prohibition. It does **not** refute selectively onerous duties: differences in need, wealth, responsibility and alternatives can justify different permissions. Being a benefactor does not confer immunity from such differences.

The two-stage route is therefore: identify goods that justify aid to a patient, then retain the significance of comparable goods in a helper. This rebuts blanket dismissal of the helper's projects as worthless. A maximizing theory can accept both stages while holding that greater aggregate gains justify sacrificing those projects. Defeating that conclusion requires an additional account of legitimate demand, not just a reminder that sacrifice has a cost.

**Keep the minimal patient ground independent.** Relief of an actual unwanted burden need not restore achievement, productivity, communication or a long future to matter. A terminal patient's relief remains part of the target. The argument for plural lives therefore supplements the suffering-based minimum; it cannot replace it with a test of whose life contains recognizable projects.

**Do not infer disappearance from defeat.** Where a warranted competing claim permits refusal, the patient's burden can remain a reason to seek a different helper, less costly means, or later relief. A ground can survive without requiring this act from this actor now. The further claim that one of those alternatives is itself obligatory needs its own feasibility, permission and comparison assessment.

## 4. Discriminating burden and latitude tests

These are proposed conceptual comparisons, not experimental findings or settled verdicts.

| Change while preserving actual patient need | What the account must explain |
|---|---|
| A single modest aid request becomes a continuing series that cumulatively destroys the helper's sleep, relationships or subsistence | Why previous and foreseeable costs affect the present requirement without making later patients less real |
| A stranger becomes a legitimately appointed caregiver, or becomes responsible for causing the danger | How added obligations change the comparison; baseline beneficence limits cannot automatically settle them |
| The helper merely dislikes assisting; no actual competing good or authorized boundary has been identified | Why preference alone would defeat a reason claimed to be independent of that preference |
| The patient validly refuses the offered intervention | Why evidence of benefit cannot itself establish authority to impose that intervention; standing can remain while this means is barred |
| Equivalent feasible relief becomes available with less interference in any affected life | Whether the burden justification updates; an asserted exemption cannot ignore a genuinely available improvement |
| An agent identifies an expensive project as identity-defining after learning that aid is requested | Whether the claimed good and its costs survive independent scrutiny, rather than self-serving declaration |
| A machine has operating costs and an assigned service role but no established welfare or personal projects | Why human personal latitude cannot simply be copied onto it; resource scarcity, stakeholder claims and the legitimacy of its role still require assessment |

Permission and authority in the refusal comparison are supplied ethical premises, not conclusions of Wolf or Cullity. Likewise, forecasting cumulative losses does not yield a decision by itself: justified comparison remains necessary. A practical spending policy can implement a defended judgment, but its chosen numerical limit cannot supply the ethical ground of that judgment.

## 5. Result that would advance or revise the account

The next positive obligation is to defend a burden-sensitive requirement in identifiable cases while retaining nonzero patient reasons beyond those cases. This needs an argument about actual permissible relief, legitimate claims, complete costs and available alternatives. It must leave more than one worthwhile life available without assuming that every preferred life is permissible.

A counterexample would matter if it showed either that the proposed limit licenses gratuitous neglect with no legitimate competing ground, or that the proposed minimum cumulatively eliminates every permissible independent pursuit. Neither can be answered by silently changing a patient's standing or by treating a programmer's budget as moral truth. The scope of every cost judgment must remain visible: a local rescue, a repeated policy and an institutional allocation are different objects of assessment.
