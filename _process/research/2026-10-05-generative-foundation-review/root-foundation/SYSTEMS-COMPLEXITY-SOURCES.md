## Four directly consulted primary comparators

### 1. Bertalanffy: laws of classes of organized systems

Ludwig von Bertalanffy, “An Outline of General System Theory,” *British Journal for the Philosophy of Science* 1(2), 134–165 (1950). [DOI identity](https://doi.org/10.1093/bjps/I.2.134); [selected reading edition](https://www.isnature.org/Events/2009/Summer/r/Bertalanffy1950-GST_Outline_SELECT.pdf).

**Read:** the selected transcription, especially §§2–3 and 8, corresponding to pp.136–143 and 155–157. This is an edited excerpt: §§4–7 are explicitly omitted. It was not treated as the complete paper or an original facsimile.

**Claim supported:** parts and their dynamic relations must be considered together. General laws apply to systems or system classes satisfying their conditions; a cross-domain resemblance needs a specified structural correspondence. Section 8 treats an organism as an open system that exchanges material and can preserve organization despite turnover. A steady state is conditional, not a consequence of openness alone.

**Use here:** this supports asking which relational properties a class shares before claiming universality. It does not identify one actual biological, social and ethical law, or establish that persistence is good. The selected edition is sufficient for these passages; it cannot support claims about omitted arguments.

### 2. Bertalanffy: general systems theory and cybernetics overlap without identity

Ludwig von Bertalanffy, “The History and Status of General Systems Theory,” *Academy of Management Journal* 15(4), 407–426 (1972). [Original article scan](https://newsyllabus.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/bertalanffy.pdf); [stable publication record](https://www.jstor.org/stable/255139).

**Read:** pp.410–414, within the complete article scan.

**Claim supported:** Bertalanffy describes general systems theory and cybernetics as parallel developments with different starting points and characteristic models: organismic/dynamic interaction and technological feedback circuits. He also identifies their shared concern with organization and goal-directed behavior. His open-system account connects exchange with chemical kinetics and irreversible thermodynamics.

**Use here:** cybernetics is an important intersection with the wider systems program, not its synonym. This is a participant's historical account, not proof of sole intellectual priority or a complete contemporary taxonomy. It also gives no license to call an abstract finite stock ledger a thermodynamic model without the relevant physical interpretation.

### 3. Simon: organization through stable intermediates and conditional separation

Herbert A. Simon, “The Architecture of Complexity,” *Proceedings of the American Philosophical Society* 106(6), 467–482 (1962). [Original article scan in an Iowa State faculty archive](https://faculty.sites.iastate.edu/tesfatsi/archive/tesfatsi/ArchitectureOfComplexity.HSimon1962.pdf).

**Read:** pp.467–471, 473–475 and 477–478.

**Claim supported:** Simon treats complex wholes as interacting parts whose collective properties can be difficult to infer without positing metaphysical irreducibility. His stable-intermediate assembly example explains a conditional advantage of retaining subassemblies under interruption. Near-decomposability requires stronger interactions within than between subsystems; short-term approximate independence can coexist with longer-term aggregate coupling. His nested hierarchy need not mean authority or subordination.

**Use here:** formed intermediate structures can enable further formation, and a justified subsystem description can preserve important couplings. Neither hierarchy nor near-decomposability is proved for every complex system. The assembly parable is a model with declared assumptions, not a measured universal evolutionary law. Strong coupling, changing boundaries or incompatible time scales can defeat the approximation.

### 4. Holland: adaptation requires mechanisms of credit and rule formation

John H. Holland, “Studying Complex Adaptive Systems,” *Journal of Systems Science and Complexity* 19, 1–8 (2006). [Publisher identity and abstract](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11424-006-0001-z); [consulted article-text reproduction](https://www.scribd.com/document/66443058/Studying-Complex-Adaptive-Systems-John-h-Hollan).

**Read:** original article body from its journal/title header through the introduction and §§1–3, including the reservoir and classifier descriptions. The publisher page supplied identity/abstract only; an official full PDF was not accessible. The reproduction has transcription errors and is not authenticated against original PDF bytes. Scribd's separately labeled AI description and generated Q&A were excluded from evidence.

**Claim supported:** Holland describes interacting agents whose conditional rules can change, emphasizing parallelism, modularity, credit assignment and rule discovery. His concrete classifier architecture supplies signals, detectors, effectors and depleted reservoirs; replenishment participates in its performance and reinforcement scheme. He explicitly characterizes exploratory computer models as demonstrations of possibilities, not actualities.

**Use here:** this is a substantive conventional comparator for consequence-dependent learning and rule revision. It does not derive its reservoir architecture, credit rule or ethical grounds from interaction alone. Because this reading relies on an unauthenticated reproduction, claims about fine notation or exact wording require a checked original; the broad architectural comparison is the present scope.

## Relationship to the generative account

The following is our comparative reconstruction, not terminology asserted uniformly by all four authors.

| Perspective | Concrete question it contributes | What still needs identification |
| --- | --- | --- |
| General/open systems | How do relations and exchanges maintain or change an organization? | Boundary, exchanges, internal transformations and applicable law class. |
| Cybernetics | How do consequences become available to regulation and change later action or the regulator? | Actual sensing, retained state, feedback, targets and update mechanisms. |
| Complex systems | How do interacting processes produce collective organization across scales? | The interaction law, collective observable and why the chosen scale preserves the relevant dependencies. |
| Complex adaptive systems | How do interacting agents or rules alter their later conduct through consequences? | The variation, credit, selection and retention mechanisms, including their costs and support. |

These questions can concern the same history. For example, recurring exchange can support a formed boundary; interactions within it can retain a consequence; that retention can alter subsequent response and the conditions supporting further retention. Differentiating these roles does not require initially independent actor, observer and world substances. Conversely, a shared generative description must preserve where consequences differ and where a proposed identification fails.

This comparison sharpens the existing formation → significance/regulation → learning/revision connection. A local controller with a supplied target is a valid mechanism model. Explaining how its target, sensor, boundary or updater forms is a further, specific task when that is the claimed scope. Recasting an existing classifier/controller in generative vocabulary alone establishes neither novelty nor a stronger explanation. A useful advance must identify an additional formation dependency, more general conditional result, preserved physical constraint or discriminating observation.

Chaos is a dynamical question distinct from these architectural descriptions. Neither many interacting parts, emergence, adaptation nor a failure of stable regulation establishes chaos. The companion [mathematical comparison](SYSTEMS-COMPLEXITY-MECHANISM.md) supplies the dynamical criteria and feedback examples; it preserves stable, oscillatory and unstable cases separately. None of these source comparisons establishes universal constructive direction, phenomenal experience or ethical concern. Those questions remain at their existing independently argued and tested scopes.
