# Assistance, jurisdiction and the scope of exclusion

This continues the [elective-concern comparison](ELECTIVE-CONCERN-SOURCES.md) within the [unified-grounding investigation](../../../../theories/becoming/chapters/UNIFIED-ETHICAL-GROUNDING.md). The issue is whether protected agency can exclude an otherwise relevant benefit from counting in a particular choice. Neither an ethical baseline nor unrestricted personal jurisdiction is assumed. These are two philosophical comparators, not new empirical evidence.

## 1. Adams: an exclusion can affect justification itself

**N. P. Adams, “In defense of exclusionary reasons,” *Philosophical Studies* 178 (2021), 235–253; published online 11 February 2020, DOI 10.1007/s11098-020-01429-8.** [Author manuscript](https://www.npadams.prof/s/Adams-In-Defense-of-Exclusionary-Reasons-Penultimate.pdf); [publisher metadata and abstract](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11098-020-01429-8).

**Bounded synopsis:** Adams proposes justificatory exclusion: a consideration can stop counting for a particular kind of choice without being outweighed or losing its value elsewhere. Practices, roles and personal autonomy can determine relevant reasons. Exclusion remains sensitive to the choice, narrowly scoped and limited. An exceptional reason can support reconsidering whether to choose under that practice. This is a proposed account of exclusion's coherence and justification, not a general exemption from assisting strangers.

No facsimile reading or identity with the final version is claimed.

## 2. White: responsibility has a burden beyond compliance sacrifice

**Stephen J. White, “Responsibility and the Demands of Morality,” *Journal of Moral Philosophy* 14 (2017), 315–338, DOI 10.1163/17455243-46810062.** [Author manuscript hosted by Northwestern](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.northwestern.edu/dist/5/764/files/2016/10/Responsibility-and-the-Demands-of-Morality-JMP-final-2016-2kmy4j2.pdf); [author publication record](https://sites.northwestern.edu/stephenwhite/research/).

**Bounded synopsis:** White interprets demandingness through answerability for others' situations, not merely sacrifices required for compliance. Permission to favor oneself may leave that responsibility extensive. His argument articulates why responsibility could require limits even when ordinary costs are held fixed; it does not vindicate those limits. The lifeguard comparison assumes differing obligations for illustration. Nothing here establishes that every optional benefit lacks positive justificatory relevance, or that an unrelated actor can extinguish patient-based reasons at will.

## 3. Our analysis: the sharper disagreement

The following is our assessment of the candidate and its rival, not further exposition of either source.

The unified candidate concerns an independently identified severe, still-unwanted experiential burden in its restricted domain, and an actor with an informed, effective, otherwise permissible means of relief. It proposes that the patient's condition can positively justify relief before the actor adopts helping as an end. That claim must survive legitimate restrictions on practical relevance; it cannot merely preserve a label saying that the patient's condition matters somewhere.

Several positions therefore need different tests:

| Position | What the rival would need to establish |
|---|---|
| No reason ever arises before adoption | An exclusive adoption condition for this kind of patient-based justification |
| An initially relevant reason is excluded here | A justified restriction applying to this actual choice and this consideration |
| The actor need not attend to the reason now | An account of warranted deliberative nonuse; this alone does not settle justificatory status |
| The actor may decline relief | Permission consistent with the full relevant normative situation; this alone establishes neither positive favor nor its absence |
| The actor need not answer for the patient's continuing burden | Limits on responsibility, distinct from the patient's condition and the reasons available for acting |
| A previously created reason ends | An account of the relevant ground and its termination, such as a valid release from a particular promise |

No row automatically supplies another. Conversely, the direct account cannot dismiss a genuine current exclusion by appealing to a reason that survives only for another agent, another act or a counterfactual unrestricted decision. If that is all that remains, the intended practical justification in the disputed choice has not been preserved.

This matters for unification. Generated beings can be both patients and authors of projects. A common foundation could recognize real powers to constitute practices and control their deliberation. The question is which powers such agents possess and why. A theory cannot obtain unlimited jurisdiction merely by calling a choice personal; nor can it rule out every restriction merely by calling an outcome beneficial. Both moves would supply the disputed conclusion through their vocabulary.

## 4. A comparison that retains protected agency

Use the [argument's fixed-project comparison](ASSISTANCE-JURISDICTION-ARGUMENT.md). The actor has already freely selected a permissible project and is choosing between two ways of carrying it out. Both serve the project equally well across relevant costs, commitments, expressive significance and history. The actor already knows the consequences. One way also relieves an independently caused stranger's actual unwanted burden; the other does not. There is no prior helping commitment, entrusted role or manufactured special duty.

The comparison asks whether the relief can positively justify the first means. It does not stipulate that declining relief is permissible, that both means are all-things-considered equally good, or that the actor owes an explanation for every unchosen benefit. Those are possible conclusions to investigate. It also does not infer a reason from permission or from the absence of conflict.

The strongest exclusionary response should identify the kind of choice being made, the value or authority supporting the restriction, and why including this consideration would impair that protected choice. These criteria need an independent defense; they are not automatic conditions imposed by the direct account. A restriction can be legitimate without money, effort or welfare loss. But equally, preserving the chosen project does not obviously require suppressing every incidental improvement for another person. The scope question remains substantive.

To prevent either side from winning through description alone, compare three nearby cases: a genuine practice restriction; ordinary discretion among project-serving means; and a self-declared policy excluding all strangers' benefits. Keep the patient episode fixed where possible. If the cases differ normatively, explain which feature warrants the difference. A label such as “my project” cannot do all the work, and neither can the label “help.”

The positive proposal has a defensible answer to test: relief may count in ordinary implementation without replacing the actor's project or making the actor responsible for the whole distribution of need. This is a normative proposal, not a theorem from equal costs. It remains vulnerable if the opponent establishes a legitimate restriction covering this very choice. If the alleged restriction instead concerns permanent vigilance or compulsory replacement of one's ends, the comparison has exposed a scope mismatch rather than refuted that protection.

## 5. What changes the grounding account

A demonstrated exclusion would require revising any candidate that claims patient relief always retains active justificatory force for every capable actor in every otherwise permissible choice. Preserving the patient's value elsewhere would not repair that claim. The account would need to explain both patient significance and legitimate limits on its practical application.

A blanket pre-adoption exclusion would need substantially more: a reason why all relevant stranger relief falls outside every unrelated actor's practical jurisdiction. The present sources do not establish that result. Nor does their failure to establish it prove our positive baseline. The next work is to justify the boundary in the retained case, connecting protection of differentiated agency with the same proposed ground of patient significance.

This advances the governing derivation by locating a constraint on **stakes → practical reasons**, without pretending that the minimal causal root already entails either side's normative powers. The existing source record on commitment, and the empirical distinction between regulation and negative experience, remain inherited evidence with their original limits. No result here establishes irreducibility or completes unified grounding.
