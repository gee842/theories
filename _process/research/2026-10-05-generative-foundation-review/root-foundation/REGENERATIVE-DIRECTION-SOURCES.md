## 2. Rolston: nonliving trajectories and practical respect

**Holmes Rolston III, “Living on Earth: Dialogue and Dialectic with my Critics,” in Christopher J. Preston and Wayne Ouderkirk, eds., *Nature, Value, Duty* (2007), pp. 237–268.** [Publisher record](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/1-4020-4878-5_14); [public text containing the chapter](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/301701984_Nature_Diminished_or_Nature_Managed_Applying_Rolston%27s_Environmental_Ethics_in_National_Parks_Pages_203-220_in_Nature_Value_Duty_Life_on_Earth_With_Holmes_Rolston_III_W_Ouderkirk_and_C_Preston_eds_Spr).

**Reading scope.** This reviewer read the continuous primary text of printed pp. 246–248, including the geophysical section, biological transition and aesthetic discussion, through the public text extraction. The hosting record is titled after John Lemons's earlier chapter, but the visible book text includes Rolston's separately headed response with its printed pagination. This is not a full-chapter reading, facsimile inspection or edition collation. Rolston's quotations from his earlier book were read as part of this response, not as an independent reading of that book or of his critics.

**Primary comparison.** Rolston attributes significance to the distinctive histories and trajectories of nonliving formations without making interests necessary. His crystal example links that significance to restraint on destructive visitation. He also gives biological development special prominence and distinguishes an object's organization from the experience of appreciating it. These passages supply a concrete comparator for beneficiary-independent significance and differentiated practical response. They do not show that every regenerative capacity is favorable, establish a ranking of unused capacity against completed formation, or prove universal authority across intelligence.

**Our use.** The useful question is how a response answers an actual formation without treating its appreciation as the entire ground. The present argument cannot import the source's assessment of naturalness, creativity or biological priority. Ren's one unfolding includes intelligently formed organization; spontaneous origin is not substituted for the stated generative criterion.

## 3. Sapontzis: an explicit experiential restriction

**S. F. Sapontzis, “The Nature of the Value of Nature,” *Electronic Journal of Analytic Philosophy* 3 (Spring 1995).** [Primary journal text](https://ejap.louisiana.edu/ejap/1995.spring/sapontzis.1995.spring.html).

**Reading scope.** This reviewer read the complete visible essay, numbered §§1–39, references and notes, through the journal's text extraction. No linked book or reference was independently assessed. Its opening environmental-policy claims and psychological generalizations were read as the author's assertions, not independently validated evidence.

**Primary comparison.** Sapontzis distinguishes bases of value from comparative priority, permits complex derivative relations, and includes nonhuman experience. His restriction locates nonderivative significance in affective experience; functional contribution alone does not suffice. Sections 25–26 connect imperative significance to affect's motivational role, while §§32–36 challenge significance attributed to insentient organization. This gives a substantive restrictive comparison rather than an arbitrary verdict applied to an unchanged description.

**Our assessment.** An explanation of how affect moves response does not by itself establish which responses are warranted or that their entire ground must be affective. That distinction challenges the offered inference, without disproving the restriction or deriving the generative alternative. The stronger realization rival in our review is our construction: it grants noninstrumental abiotic achievement and questions unused regenerative capacity. It is not attributed to Sapontzis.
