**Result.** The previously specified lineage model retains better aggregate probability scores than two changing-block models in all four treatments, although a block model wins a phase-specific comparison. ## One history, two possible sources of predictive structure

The [preceding comparison](LINEAGE-FORECAST-INTEGRATION.md) used a conventional Bayesian lineage model to forecast 3,424 later observed culture outcomes without using their own outcomes in fitting. Related cultures also share blocks and experimental conditions. The remaining question was therefore whether a structured block predictor could account for the apparent advantage over a treatment-wide stage average.

The [follow-up specification](../../../../experiments/integrated-correction/general-framework/generative-basis/ecological-selection/lineages/TRANSFER-PROTOCOL.md) retains the earlier outcomes, training cutoff, losses, phase splits and time windows. Two new conventional models assign a changing latent viability propensity to each of the six experimental blocks. Both use the same beta transition family, probability grid, finite sigma prior and earlier observations as the prior analysis. One joins initial block propensities to a shared unobserved root; the other uses independent uniform roots. Neither follows biological parent assignments. Current destination blocks identify the new cultures' forecasts.

A missing block observation contributes no outcome likelihood, while its latent location state continues through the scheduled transition. This does not fill unknown genealogy: the model has a different, explicit persistence assumption for a known location. Model structure, pooling and priors still differ across the alternatives, so this is a comparison of specified predictors, not a causal intervention that varies only inheritance. All models and both root choices are reported; no result is selected from the scores to redefine the test.

## The block alternative was tested

Mean Brier scores on the same cycles 2–10 observations are below; lower is better. Original lineage predictions are reused byte-for-byte.

| Treatment | Observations | Lineage | Changing blocks, common root | Changing blocks, independent roots |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Standard, initially switch-negative | 848 | **0.240287** | 0.252755 | 0.251239 |
| Large, initially switch-negative | 848 | **0.206472** | 0.217209 | 0.221457 |
| Standard, initially switch-positive | 864 | **0.057148** | 0.057621 | 0.060359 |
| Large, initially switch-positive | 864 | **0.125486** | 0.132344 | 0.133401 |

Aggregate negative log-probability also favors the lineage model over both block alternatives in all four treatments. The original changing-stage and constant-stage comparisons remain in the same output. Numerical resolutions of 50, 100 and 200 states are retained rather than selecting the most favorable one.

The counterexamples matter. In the standard switch-negative treatment, **phase I** is predicted better by independent block roots than by the lineage model on both losses. In standard switch-positive **phase II**, the constant model has the best Brier score and the common-root block model has the best log score. In cycles 6–10 of the standard switch-positive treatment, the constant model beats all the more structured alternatives in aggregate. A general claim that ancestry always dominates location or simpler frequency prediction would be false for these results.

The affirmative inference is narrower: these two specified models of persistent block conditions do not account for the lineage model's aggregate advantage. This strengthens the predictive relevance of the recorded history. It does not exclude unmeasured shared exposures, a more adequate joint block-and-lineage model, or alternative priors. It also does not establish superiority of our generative framework over the conventional lineage model that produces the successful forecasts.

## Separate assays reveal a transfer failure

The original fitness workbook has **3,802 raw rows in 139 date/strain/size/phase cohorts**. The [new inventory and results](../../../../experiments/integrated-correction/general-framework/generative-basis/ecological-selection/lineages/assay-transfer-results.json) preserve every cohort's counts, flags and replacement labels. A source-labelled reading contains **485 focal rows** from five dated genotype batches. Strain strings remain unchanged; normalization of `LARGE` only groups the size label.

For the same focal genotype names, the first-phase contrast changes across dates:

| Assay date | Evolved `PE-2-WT-A5` | Revertant `PE-2-WT-A5 mutL` | Evolved minus reverted observed viability |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 17 February 2020 | 41/80 = 51.25% | 51/80 = 63.75% | −12.50 percentage points |
| 2 June 2020 | 47/72 = 65.28% | 31/72 = 43.06% | +22.22 percentage points |

A Beta(1,1) predictor fitted separately to the February genotypes forecasts **36.88** and **45.66** viable cultures out of June's 72 each. The observed counts are **47** and **31**. Its Brier scores are **0.246422** and **0.286627**. Pooling the two February genotypes predicts 41.33 for each and has lower Brier scores of **0.232853** and **0.265775**. This is a failure of the specified transferable genotype-specific rate model on those later observations, not a claim that genotype has no causal role.

All first-phase cultures in these focal batches have positive smooth-cell counts. Their recorded viability agrees with the joint mat-and-smooth-cell criterion; within this subset, the observed failure distinction lies at the mat criterion. That identifies a measured stage component, not the physical cause of its variation. Date differences could reflect conditions, handling, sample history or other unmeasured factors; the workbook alone does not distinguish them. Culture counts and rack labels do not justify a culture-independent significance test or unchanged-genotype certainty across stored samples.

February cannot calibrate the later whole-cycle genotype contrast. Only **7 of 41** evolved first-phase survivors have second-phase rows; all seven survive, but the remaining 34 have no recorded follow-up. The revertant has **no second-phase records for any of its 51 survivors**. Those absences are neither failures nor successes. Treating the selected 7/7 as the full conditional population, or assigning an unsupported revertant transition, would manufacture the missing mechanism. The output explicitly leaves this full-cycle prediction uncalibrated.

## A linked earlier assay locates the component that transfers

On **22 May 2018**, the evolved strain has 48 initial cultures, 40 phase-I survivors and 48 phase-II records. Eight second-phase slots contain replacements for first-phase failures. The actual 40 original survivors all have corresponding phase-II records, and **36** remain viable. The comparison follows those original survivors, excluding the eight replacement destinations from that conditional denominator. This gives a linked history without treating replacement as resurrection of the original failed culture.

With explicit Beta(1,1) priors, that assay predicts first-phase viability **41/50 = 0.82** and conditional second-phase viability **37/42 = 0.880952**. Testing them on the June 2020 evolved-strain assay gives:

| Target in June | Prediction from 2018 | Recorded June outcome |
|---|---:|---:|
| Phase I, among 72 initial cultures | 59.04 | 47 |
| Phase II, conditional on the 47 actual phase-I survivors | 41.40 | 42 |
| Complete cycle, among 72 initial cultures | 52.01 | 42 |

The second row evaluates the conditional stage predictor once the June first-phase survivors exist; it is not an advance prediction that there would be exactly 47 survivors. The third row is the advance full-cycle mean from the product of the two fitted stage means. Its independent stage-prior and cross-date correspondence assumptions are explicit. It overpredicts the cycle despite the close second-stage result. A direct complete-cycle Beta count model from the same 2018 linked outcomes predicts 53.28; the modest difference between the scalar and component versions also reflects their different priors and is not evidence of uniquely generative superiority.

The 2018 training records include one phase-II `ERR` row with missing phenotype counts and recorded extinction. It remains a failure for the primary **recorded-viability** target, not a confirmed biological inability to form the next phenotype. Among the 39 original-survivor records without that error flag, 36 survive. All June target records have condition zero, so the declared condition-zero scoring sensitivity makes no change. No missing phenotype is silently converted into a measured absent state.

The primary paper's §3d separately reports that reverting mutL chiefly affects the phase-II smooth-to-wrinkly transition and that its effects on phase-I components were not significant in the authors' analysis. Our dated observations are compatible with the need to distinguish those components; they do not reproduce the author's statistical test, microscopic mutation rate or genome analysis. The earlier directly matched **42/72 versus 7/72** complete-cycle result remains intact. A rank reversal of a first-stage assay does not erase that source-supported whole-cycle intervention evidence.

## What changes in the derivation

The connection is now more precise: inherited reconstruction and its actual stage conditions jointly determine observed continuation. The chronology test supports the information carried by recorded ancestry beyond these block alternatives; the assay test shows that one dated genotype-level viability estimate is insufficient for reliable transfer. The empirical mechanism cannot be completed by assigning the focal mutation one favorable scalar fitness and applying it unchanged everywhere.

The next central test concerns **the formation and endurance of the first-phase mat under changed physical and developmental conditions, joined to the independently assayed second-phase transition and actual sampling**. Identify which measured conditions explain the first-stage variation, then test that account on a separate condition or batch. A joint block-and-lineage model can remain a predictive rival; it cannot itself supply the physical explanation. The second-stage genetic intervention provides a scoped mechanistic constraint, not a complete kinetic law. Genealogy, phenotype formation, replacement and resource provision must remain part of the same history.

This supports the normative programme by improving calibration of whether a proposed capacity actually permits further life under the relevant conditions. It does not infer ethical authority from increased viability or remove G0 and the additional ethical commitments. The completed practical-judgment candidate keeps its original scope. This is author self-review with independently coded numerical checks, not independent agent review. The full evolutionary theory and its broad comparative strength remain unfinished.
