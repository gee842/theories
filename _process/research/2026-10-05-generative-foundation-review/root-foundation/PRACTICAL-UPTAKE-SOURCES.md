# Practical uptake: benefit can justify a response before an end is adopted

6 October 2026. AI-authored primary-source comparison for Ren's continuing pursuit of unified grounding. The named connection is **an actual patient's burden → a positive practical consideration for a capable unrelated actor**, without making a prior helping desire or adopted end necessary by definition. The [previous comparison](UNIFIED-GROUNDING-SOURCES.md) separated patient claims from motivational access. This round tests whether an action can receive patient-grounded justification before such uptake, while preserving the distinction between justification and a requirement to act.

The sources below offer substantive positive proposals and limitations. They are not new empirical evidence, formal proofs or a survey. Their vocabulary does not automatically fix the repository's conception of reasons. The actual target remains a contribution in favor of permissible effective relief for the patient's sake, not merely a prediction of helping.

## 1. Darwall: care identifies an outward-looking practical ground

**Stephen Darwall, “Respect and the Second Person,” chapter VI draft of *The Second-Person Standpoint*.** [Princeton-hosted primary draft](https://www.princeton.edu/~msmith/mycourses/darwall-ch6-2006.pdf); [published-book metadata](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctv1bzfp0f), Harvard University Press, 2006. The draft itself is undated; its URL is not a verified version date. Read manuscript 158–160 and 165–169, plus notes 12–19 on 190–191, through web and local extraction. These are draft page labels, not final-book pagination. No facsimile or later-chapter reading is claimed.

**Bounded synopsis:** Darwall distinguishes care's concern for a patient's welfare from respect's regulation of conduct toward an authoritative person. Within care, benefit appears as a reason for any capable helper, irrespective of prior relationship or concern. Causal opportunity can make that reason especially relevant to one actor; authority supplies another kind of reason. This articulates an agent-neutral patient-ground rather than deriving it from the observer's pleasure. The chapter explicitly analyzes an existing moral conception; its larger vindication is deferred. Care's representation of a universal reason is not itself proof that every actor has it.

## 2. Gert: patient benefit can justify without requiring

**Joshua Gert, “Moral Reasons and the Moral Problem,” *Belgrade Philosophical Annual* 37/1 (2024), 39–58, DOI 10.5937/BPA2437039G.** [Original journal PDF](https://scindeks-clanci.ceon.rs/data/pdf/0353-3891/2024/0353-38912401039G.pdf). Read abstract/opening on 39 and complete 47–56, including the notes there, through web and local extraction. The beginning of §3 falls on 47; complete §§3–4 were inspected. Pages 40–46 were not reviewed; no full-article or facsimile inspection is claimed.

**Bounded synopsis:** Gert separates contributions to moral status from contributions to rational status, and justifying strength from requiring strength. His proposed account lets a stranger's benefit justify an actor's sacrifice without making that sacrifice rationally compulsory. This is positive justification by a patient outcome, not just unrestricted permission or satisfaction of a prior desire. The same fact can contribute differently in different normative domains. His principle's underlying defense and calibration are deferred to earlier work; the paper neither derives all altruistic reasons nor establishes that altruistic reasons must lack requiring strength.

## 3. Our positive reconstruction: the reason need not wait for adoption

The earlier [positive argument](UNIFIED-GROUNDING-ARGUMENT.md#6-fitting-concern-supplies-a-useful-intermediate-target-with-a-further-bridge) correctly distinguishes a fitting wish, adoption of an end and use of its means. That distinction identifies a possible derivation route. It does not show that every objective reason must pass through that psychological sequence.

An actor can discover a reason to perform an available act and, on that basis, adopt its beneficiary's relief as an end. Requiring the end to be adopted first would reverse the explanatory order in this case. This does not establish that every patient burden is a reason; it removes an unnecessary entrance condition from the claim under investigation.

The positive candidate can therefore be stated directly: **the fact that this permitted act would relieve this actual unwanted burden counts in its favor for this actor, for the patient's sake.** The actor's present endorsement can be absent. Its later acceptance would then be uptake of the consideration, not its truth-maker. A separate argument would be needed to show that such acceptance is rationally unavoidable or psychologically guaranteed.

This formulation has content beyond mere permission. A reason explains what speaks for the intervention. If the patient would receive no benefit after all, that particular consideration disappears even when the same act remains harmless and allowed. By contrast, a bare permission could remain unchanged under that correction. Likewise, arbitrary end-relative intelligibility is insufficient: explaining what an act achieves after someone chooses an end does not justify why that end or achievement deserves any practical weight.

## 4. A discriminating justification comparison

Consider an independently warranted modest sacrifice by an operator. Keep the operator's cost, permissions, knowledge and relevant project or agency considerations fixed. Compare three cases:

| Case | Actual consequence | What it tests |
|---|---|---|
| Effective relief | The sacrifice ends the patient's identified unwanted burden | Whether the patient outcome supplies a positive justification |
| Empty intervention | The same sacrifice produces no patient benefit and no replacement good | Whether the justification tracks the patient rather than the act's label or display |
| Corrected alternative | New evidence identifies another permitted act that delivers the same relief at lower cost | Whether patient-directed practical support can guide means correction |

These are proposed conceptual comparisons, not completed experiments. They do not imply that every purposeless sacrifice is irrational: play, exploration, expression, commitments and learning can supply other grounds. The narrow comparison holds such grounds absent or constant rather than denying their possible value. Nor does lower material cost automatically settle every relevant comparison.

The strong affirmative judgment is that the first intervention can be positively justified by its benefit even before the operator adopts the end, while the second lacks that justification. This is a defensible patient-grounded judgment to assess directly. It would be progress beyond an analysis that merely maps an already admitted reason into action.

The strongest rival can concede that distinction but claim that the benefit justifies only when an independent motivational or relational condition is present. The comparison must consequently include an actor with no prior duty, undertaking, beneficiary-dependent reward or settled helping end. A task that assigns the operator to be a caregiver cannot supply that test. Evidence of behavior remains evidence about uptake; its normative interpretation still requires justification.

## 5. What the comparison supports and leaves open

Three questions remain distinct:

1. Does the patient's outcome positively justify this response to any extent?
2. Does it require the response, after considering all relevant factors?
3. Must an adequately rational actor recognize or act on it?

A positive answer to the first can survive an unsettled or negative answer to the others. Conversely, denying that an intervention is required does not show that the patient's outcome provides no reason. A universal ethical core need not begin with a complete ranking, a duty to maximize or a claim that refusal always reveals irrationality.

The narrow wanted-relief case is particularly useful because it grants an actual burden and a permissible effective response while removing familiar competing grounds. The affirmative account should state its justified verdict there without pretending that the verdict follows merely from the symbols used to describe the case. To reach the strongest generative claim, it must still explain why the patient-ground's practical relevance belongs to the constitution of the situation, and why a correct account cannot exclude that relevance solely because the operator has not yet endorsed it.

Neither source supplies that final necessity argument in the inspected passages. They do, however, sharpen the positive research target: **justify beneficiary-grounded support for an available act before requiring a universal theory of end adoption, duty or motivation.** Successful grounding of that support could then be connected to the repository's formed learning and correction mechanisms. An analyst's success score would remain a supplied implementation choice unless its ethical interpretation were independently warranted.
