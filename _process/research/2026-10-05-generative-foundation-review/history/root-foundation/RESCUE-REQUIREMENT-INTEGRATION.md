## Named connection and result

The connection is **objective patient-grounded moral favor → requiring force in a bounded serious-relief case, with legitimate exceptions**. The positive result is a defended duty: an informed capable responder should secure permitted, effective, consent-compatible relief of severe ongoing unwanted suffering when the only opposing ground is a brief replaceable interruption, assessed over the actual history. Mere indifference supplies no exemption. The requirement does not depend on prior friendship, reciprocity, an adopted helping end or private reward.

The result is not deduced from the bare existence of a moral reason. The [argument](RESCUE-REQUIREMENT-ARGUMENT.md) explicitly adds a comparative requirement principle: in this domain, a substantially stronger person-grounded objection defeats refusal absent an independently justified protection. Its defense appeals to what the relevant interests and permissions protect. Maintaining authority over a continuing life does not by itself justify categorical discretion over every brief interval. A rival can still defend a further categorical permission. Absence of a discovered defense is not proof that such a permission is false.

The [review](RESCUE-REQUIREMENT-REVIEW.md) reconstructs that rival without reducing it to selfishness or denying that the patient matters. It distinguishes relief from prevention and improvement, moral duty from enforceability, adequate relief from a specified method, actual alternatives from imagined providers, and previous contributions from automatic immunity. These distinctions constrain the affirmative verdict; they do not establish a complete theory by themselves.

## Revision forced by the stronger comparison

The [primary-source assessment](RESCUE-REQUIREMENT-SOURCES.md) shows why retaining cumulative history is not a solution to demandingness. Ashford counts lifetime losses to projects and relationships while defending further aid. This preserves a serious priority rival to moderate requirements. Fictional compliance cannot discharge the actual unserved patient's claim; unfairness among helpers requires further argument before licensing refusal. Scanlon's later manuscript supplies a separate bounded-rescue argument and contested security considerations. His revised treatment must not be collapsed into the earlier theory Ashford examines.

The accepted correction is therefore precise: reject an argument that continually resets small costs, while leaving a fully history-sensitive demanding view live. The canonical account now distinguishes removal from the bounded argument's scope from a positive permission to stop. It also separates a feasible menu of future lives from a justified permission to choose among them. The local compatibility candidate combines the rescue principle with separately defended grounds of worthwhile activity and latitude; rescue alone proves neither.

## Premises that remain supplied

Applications still require independent identification of the patient and severe still-unwanted experience; efficacy and safety; relevant consent and authority over means; real full-history costs and responsibilities; and warranted alternatives. The underlying patient-to-actor moral contribution, positive goods of self-direction and relationships, comparative priority and permission-defeating force remain ethical grounds under argument. No observed selection process, resource balance or learned reward establishes them merely by being successful.

The generative foundation contributes an account of formation, costs, histories and revision. It can help explain how a responder acquires reliable evidence, whether a relief route is funded, and how provision might become durable. Those contributions retain the supplied ethical premises. A causal model of helpers is not itself a justification of what anyone owes.

## Next central comparison

Compare **a genuine nontrivial self-direction claim with continuing severe need under actual noncompliance**. Test a demanding allocation and a proposed permission to continue a worthwhile project against the same people, histories, capacities and unmet losses. A positive argument must explain why the alleged permission defeats this patient's claim rather than merely noting that projects have value. An argument for greater demands must preserve the helper's actual life and cannot conceal lifetime costs.

Reliable shared provision may change that conflict, but its formation, costs and transitional unmet needs must be included. No imagined equal chance of occupying each position can replace known differences in exposure. Continue the deeper patient-to-actor grounding and the categorical-permission challenge as explicit interfaces. A justified categorical refusal right, mistaken patient identification, or an allocation that destroys defensible plural latitude would revise different parts of the account. The strongest possible universal foundation remains the aim; this narrow duty is one contribution toward it.
