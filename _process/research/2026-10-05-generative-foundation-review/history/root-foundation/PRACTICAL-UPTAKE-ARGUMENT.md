# Practical uptake: a reason can precede adopting the end

6 October 2026. AI-authored continuation of Ren's request to pursue unified grounding. Named connection: **an independently warranted patient burden and an eligible opportunity for relief → an objective contributory reason for an unrelated actor**. This advances and corrects the framing of [the preceding argument, §6](UNIFIED-GROUNDING-ARGUMENT.md#6-fitting-concern-supplies-a-useful-intermediate-target-with-a-further-bridge). It preserves the [genealogical investigation](HUIGEN-SELF-GROUND-AUDIT.md#33-evolutionary-genealogy-and-the-justification-of-concern) and [the accepted conditional foundation](FOUNDATION-ROOT-ACCEPTANCE.md).

**Judgment:** in the narrowly specified relief case, the patient's relief positively justifies the stranger's intervention for the patient's sake. It therefore supplies an objective contributory reason, even if the stranger has not adopted helping as an end. The argument below gives defeasible comparative warrant for that judgment. It does not deduce its truth from causal descriptions, prove universal rational agreement, or establish the necessity of the entire generative root.

**Correction:** we should not require a prior justified adoption of relief as an end before a direct patient-ground can count as a reason for action. That would impose a mediation route on the question we are trying to answer. A reason can justify adopting an end, acquiring concern, or acting directly; its existence need not await any of those events.

## 1. The target is a favoring consideration, not compulsory care

Fix patient p undergoing a severe unwanted experience e. The episode and its adverse character have adequate independent warrant. Actor a has reliable knowledge and a permissible, effective intervention y that would relieve that same episode. No prior responsibility, promise, private benefit or desire to help is assumed. In the clearest case, the available alternatives differ in the burden while relevant costs, competing claims and effects on other patients are held fixed.

Our target R is that **the fact that y relieves p's episode counts in favor of a doing y**. It is an objective contributory reason in the sense used by the current investigation. Whether every philosophical use of “reason” includes this relation is a separate interpretive question. The word is not meant to decide a dispute with a motivation-sensitive account by stipulation.

Keep five verdicts distinct:

| Verdict | What it does and does not establish |
|---|---|
| a may perform y | Bare permission need not identify any positive reason for y |
| y is intelligible given a's adopted end | Conditional instrumental fit does not establish that the end is worth pursuing |
| p's relief positively justifies y for a | The proposed patient-ground is a contributory reason |
| a must perform y | Requires a further account of decisive force, alternatives and relevant permissions |
| a is motivated to perform y | Requires an account of recognition and effective psychological influence |

No inference from the first or second row alone establishes the third. The third does not, by itself, establish either of the last two. Our earlier concern-to-end-to-means sequence was a possible explanatory route and a useful discriminator, not a proof that direct action reasons require all those intermediate achievements.

## 2. The direct argument starts with what justifies this intervention

Consider an actor who learns the patient's circumstances, has no preexisting helping project, and chooses effective relief. Asked what makes the action worth doing, the actor answers that it stops this person's unwanted suffering. We can assess that offered explanation without assuming that any newly formed desire is correct.

Our affirmative assessment is that the explanation identifies a real merit of the intervention. It specifies what improves, whose condition improves, and why this change merits a response. It continues to do that work if the actor receives no pleasure, approval or reciprocal advantage. It supports correcting the intervention when new evidence shows it would hurt the patient instead. It does not justify merely eliminating the actor's own discomfort while leaving the patient suffering.

This is stronger than finding the action allowed or psychologically intelligible. An arbitrary adopted goal could make an action instrumentally coherent while supplying no good patient-directed justification. Here the offered explanation is constrained by a separately identified adverse condition and by the actual difference the intervention makes for its subject. The merit is attributable to that difference, rather than to the bare fact that the actor now intends it.

The next inference concerns order of justification. If the patient's relief is what makes this voluntary response worth choosing, the new choice need not manufacture that merit. The merit can support the choice. Removing a prior helping intention, while preserving the patient facts and available legitimate response, does not by itself remove the offered justification. This is the direct-ground position we defend; it remains a substantive claim about the source of the reason.

A sophisticated attitude-based account can disagree at exactly this point. It may say that relief is valuable for p, while the action is justified for a only because a has or can acquire a suitable desire. It can also locate the relevant desire in a wider evaluative outlook rather than a conscious helping intention. We must therefore not infer desire-independent grounding merely from the absence of one explicit motive. The [existing overdetermination study](HUIGEN-SELF-GROUND-AUDIT.md#26-patient-reasons-motivational-grounds-and-overdetermination) already establishes that limitation.

What favors the direct account is its explanation of correction: the patient's condition is the standard to which the proposed concern and means are answerable. The desire can be newly warranted because it tracks that significance, and unwarranted when it merely projects a false patient need. An attitude-based account can sometimes explain these corrections too; when it does, the comparison must examine the ground of its wider standpoint. Convergence on the same action is not proof that one grounding account is uniquely necessary.

### A positive justification test with a modest cost

The clean costless case leaves an evidential ambiguity: an intervention may be permitted even if nothing favors it. To test positive justification more directly, permit a modest, fully disclosed cost to a. Compare a relief intervention with an otherwise matched intervention that incurs the same cost and benefits nobody. Keep other possible goods of effort, play, exploration or self-expression fixed; do not assume that useless effort is universally impermissible. Neither context supplies an earlier helping intention or special responsibility.

Our comparative judgment is that actual patient relief can make this small sacrifice worth accepting, while the matched empty intervention lacks that particular justification. The difference is what happens to p. This reveals a favorable consideration without claiming that the sacrifice is required. It also permits a discriminating correction: if the apparent relief proves ineffective, that justification disappears even if the actor remains equally eager to help.

This example does not independently refute every attitude-based account. Such an account may locate the contrast in an antecedent wider concern plus the changed promotion relation. The direct account instead takes the patient's improvement to explain why acquiring that concern would be warranted. The comparison supplies a positive evaluative datum and a concrete explanatory disagreement; it does not conceal the grounding dispute in a permission verdict.

The [coordinated primary-source study](PRACTICAL-UPTAKE-SOURCES.md) develops this comparison using Gert's distinction between justifying and requiring strength, while withholding endorsement of his additional zero-requirement thesis. Its Darwall reading similarly separates patient welfare as a putative reason from special authority. These source comparisons illuminate the target; their analyses are not proofs of our grounding claim.

## 3. Optionality leaves room for a real reason

The historical-role rival grants the actual burden, fitting compassion and permission to assist, but denies every assistance reason without a special tie. One attraction of that view may be a concern to protect an actor's freedom to choose projects. Our narrower claim preserves an important part of that concern.

Recognizing a reason for relief does not automatically make relief obligatory. An actor may have competing reasons, legitimate personal latitude, or constraints on what others can demand. The grounding of those permissions and their relation to decisive reasons remains further work. Even in a clean case, the existence of a positive reason and the theory of when reasons require action are distinguishable questions.

Consequently, the argument “a is free not to become p's caregiver, so a has no reason to relieve p” moves too quickly. It must show why freedom requires the total absence of a patient-directed favoring consideration, rather than discretion over whether and how to respond. Likewise, “p cannot demand this intervention from a” does not by itself establish that helping lacks positive justification. Directed claims, authority and accountability can have conditions that a contributory reason does not share.

This yields a limited but substantive result: **arguments establishing only the absence of a duty, an entrusted role, or a patient's authority to command have not yet defended a zero-reason baseline.** They may justify retained optionality or rule out a stronger claim. They cannot do both jobs without a further explanation of why the same restriction extinguishes all practical favor.

The affirmative account still bears its own burden. Showing that one defense of the gate reaches only duty or authority does not prove the direct reason. The positive ground comes from the patient's actual improvement and the merits of the response described in §2. The scope criticism removes a misleading objection to that ground; it does not substitute for it.

## 4. What becomes different when relief becomes available?

Compare two contexts involving the same ongoing burden. In c0, a has no way to affect it. In c1, a gains a known, innocent and costless way to end it, with the patient's willingness preserved. Neither a helping desire nor a special role is added. The patient's adverse condition remains the object of concern in both.

In c0, appropriate concern may have no available remedial expression. In c1, the very difference that made concern appropriate becomes an available consequence of a's choice. The direct account explains the changed practical situation through that new causal opportunity. The patient supplies the point of relief; the actor's new capacity makes that point relevant to an available intervention.

This is not the invalid inference “a can act, therefore a ought to act.” Ability alone does not make an end good. Nor is it an unrestricted principle that every fitting emotion should be promoted or acted upon. The example fixes the wanted relief of the burden that grounded concern. It concerns the practical realization of that same improvement, not producing more sadness, satisfying an arbitrary desire, or choosing any action associated with compassion.

The historical gate leaves the new opportunity without any patient-grounded practical significance until some further tie appears. That position is formally available. Its explanatory cost is now specific: it must explain why an independently recognized reason for patient-directed concern remains entirely excluded from the assessment of an innocent available response to the very condition at issue.

The direct account's advantage is correspondence between the response's merit and its actual beneficiary. We judge that explanatory fit positively supports the baseline. A claim that favoring a world-directed outcome can never address an unconnected operator would need a substantive defense. Its coherence alone supplies none. Yet this is comparative philosophical support; we have not shown that rejecting it violates logic or every independently justified standard of practical understanding.

## 5. Revise ends through the ground; do not validate the ground through the end

The revised route is:

\[
\text{identified patient significance + eligible relief opportunity}
\quad\xrightarrow{\text{defended normative argument}}\quad
\text{contributory action reason}
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\text{possible justified adoption, cultivation, or direct response}.
\]

The first arrow is the contested substantive argument above. The second contains further, context-sensitive practical inferences. It does not promise automatic motivation or make a particular educational process mandatory.

This direction avoids one bootstrapping error. “I adopted helping as an end; therefore helping had patient-directed authority; therefore adopting that end was correct” supplies no independent ground. By contrast, the patient's warranted condition can make a proposed new end worth considering before the actor endorses it. Investigation may begin with uncertainty about the condition or reason, and its warrant must reflect that uncertainty.

There can still be legitimate choice-created reasons. A promise, a joint project or an undertaking can change a later normative situation. The claim here is not that choice never generates reasons. It is that such generation need not be the source of every merit of a choice, and is not shown necessary for this minimal patient-ground.

This also repairs the interpretation of “practical uptake.” In an objective reason claim, uptake is what makes a ground effective in the actor's reasoning and action; it need not be what makes the ground normatively exist. A model of acquired concern should therefore keep the normative relation, warranted recognition, actual motivation and effective relief distinct. Failure at one layer does not automatically establish failure at the others.

The [companion counterreview](PRACTICAL-UPTAKE-COUNTERREVIEW.md#1-correct-the-possible-serial-reading) identifies a concrete inherited case: the accepted repair reason can remain while the learner's acquired connection suppresses helping. Making the reason itself depend on an active helping end would undermine that diagnosis of mislearning. This establishes an ordering already used in the conditional role-bound account. It does not export the role-bound reason to the stranger; the direct patient-ground argument must still do that work.

## 6. Limits that genuinely test this argument

Several familiar examples do not refute the narrow target in the same way:

- A negative controller signal without an identified experiential burden leaves the patient-good premise unsupported.
- Fitting grief does not give a reason to create another loss. That changes the object and direction of the response.
- Symbolic helping that changes the observer's feelings without relieving the patient lacks the claimed efficacy.
- A harmful or refused intervention challenges permission or overall action. It need not eliminate every contributory reason supplied by possible relief; that further claim requires analysis.
- Significant cost or competing duty challenges decisiveness and perhaps the eligible comparison. It does not by itself prove zero patient-directed favor.
- A stable helping disposition induced by reward establishes a causal history, not the validity of the disposition's proposed ground.

The serious counterexample would preserve an independently warranted patient burden, actual permissible relief, relevant actor capacity and the other specified conditions, yet justify the absence of **any** patient-directed reason. Merely asserting the rival gate is not such a justification. Conversely, merely failing to find a counterexample is not proof of our affirmative principle.

A motivation-sensitive account can challenge a different part. It may accept a universal patient-grounded moral claim but reserve “reason for a” for claims reachable through a justified route from a's actual motivations. The investigation must determine whether this is a dispute about the existence of moral favor, its practical accessibility, or the meaning and grounds of reasonhood. Calling every inaccessible actor incapable would conceal rather than solve that question. Current refusal is not by itself evidence of permanent incapacity.

## 7. Adjudication and what the result changes

At the stated scope, we favor the direct patient-ground judgment. Relief is a positively justified response because of the patient's real improvement. Requiring the actor first to adopt relief as an end adds an unnecessary condition to the objective reason claim. Appealing solely to the absence of a special duty or authority does not justify denying all contributory favor. This defends the baseline as a defeasible normative judgment and removes a mistaken route requirement while retaining the dispute about its deepest explanation. It does not report a derivation of the baseline from the causal foundation.

The confidence is philosophical and defeasible. Its support consists in the beneficiary-directed explanation, its correction conditions, and the precise failure of duty-only or permission-only objections to address the target. It does not consist in a new empirical observation, a proof from a sparse causal language, an inherited mechanism's retention, or universal agreement. A rival that independently justifies a necessary actor restriction could revise the judgment. A rival that vindicates the same baseline through a different generative grounding could instead revise its explanation while preserving its content.

For the unified programme, this directs work toward identifying why the patient's actual experiential structure makes relief favor action, and how a learner can apprehend that merit. An irreducible ethical premise remains one possible outcome. This study does not establish that outcome, and it does not require choosing it merely because the positive argument is presently normative. A constitutive grounding may explain why the normative judgment is true; labeling that possibility “supplied today” must not mean “irreducible forever.”

**Supplied and contested:** the identified patient burden, effects and permissions; the objective contributory meaning of the target; and the substantive judgment that this patient's actual relief positively justifies the stranger's available response. The last is defended here rather than derived from causal structure alone. Its universal necessity, unique grounding, full patient coverage, N3 answerability and conflict resolution remain open.

**Next discriminating task:** compare the direct-ground account with the strongest motivation-sensitive explanation in a case where no prior helping intention or special role is present. Keep the source of favor distinct from whatever later motivates the actor. Seek a positive account of recognition or a justified restriction; neither a new reward fixture nor a stipulated irrational stranger will settle it.
