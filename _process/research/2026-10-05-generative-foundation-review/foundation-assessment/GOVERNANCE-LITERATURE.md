# Governance, future conditions, and the alignment claim

6 October 2026. AI-authored research assessment for Ren's question whether the generative foundation supplies a fundamental part of AI alignment and governance. This is a targeted primary-source comparison, not an exhaustive originality review, external peer review, legal assessment, or empirical validation. Sources published by the stated date are eligible; search-engine crawl dates are not publication dates. No source originals were imported or modified.

**Named bridge:** consequence-grounded learning and selector revision → durable, plural, revisable concern under strategic pressure. **Governing question:** how do affected beings retain the material, informational, social and institutional conditions for having and shaping a future when the mechanisms allocating those conditions can themselves learn, compete and be captured?

## Assessment

**Subsequent historical application, 6 October 2026:** [the Robodebt study](../../../../theories/becoming/history/EVIDENCE-AND-INSTITUTIONAL-CORRECTION.md) now tests a specific interface within this programme: a warranted correction to one case must be distinguished from revision of the shared ground generating other cases. The [source audit](GOVERNANCE-RECOURSE-EVIDENCE.md), [contract](GOVERNANCE-RECOURSE-CONTRACT.md) and [review](GOVERNANCE-RECOURSE-REVIEW.md) supply historical evidence, bounded implementation conditions and falsifiers. This addition does not change the originality assessment below or supply a causal estimate of a new institutional design. Its separately labeled preservation package is later than this literature note's original no-import statement.

The programme identifies a fundamental governance problem: present decisions alter who can learn, act, object and make consequential decisions later. A nominally responsive institution can consume or transfer the conditions of its own future responsiveness. An AI system can satisfy its current principals while helping remove other people's economic, epistemic or political agency. These are substantive problems even when no component has a malicious objective.

The strongest defensible contribution is an **integrated, conditional account of the formation, exercise and renewal of capacities that make concern effective**. The repository's [formation result](../../../../theories/becoming/history/FORMATION.md) identifies strategic depletion of future corrective capacity. Its [joint construction](../../../../theories/becoming/history/JOINT-FORMATION.md) joins paid acquisition, concern, evidence, control and replacement in one resource history, with explicit failure regions. These are more concrete than simply calling for accountable or beneficial AI.

However, the literature already contains regulation under limited variety, polycentric resource governance, revision of institutional goals and measures, endogenous capture, non-domination and structural AI risk. The broad principles are not new discoveries. The possible advance lies in their derivational connection and checked joint accounting, and must be tested against established models. Neither a universal favorable direction nor a comprehensive governance solution follows.

Ren's clarification about what preserves a future improves the central formulation. Correction is one enabling capacity; so are bodily security, ecological support, independent knowledge, relationships, livelihood, practical alternatives and the ability to form projects. The end is not an institution that endlessly corrects itself while its subjects lose meaningful lives. Nor is it maximizing the number of available options: removing an exploitative option can protect other people's futures. An institution may justifiably end; an affected being does not become worthless because they cannot contribute to its continuation. This reading agrees with the repository's explicit [ethical foundation and counterexamples](../../../../theories/becoming/history/ETHICAL-FOUNDATION.md).

Ren's further clarification makes an earlier connection explicit: external dependence is operative before a center reflects on anything beyond its boundary. An institution's resources, inherited practices and informative evidence can sustain its cognition and future even while its official model treats them as external. For a particular task requiring discrimination among external conditions, a learner needs a suitably informative causal route, possibly through earlier learning, inherited memory, instruments or other people; changing internal reasoning alone cannot distinguish cases that its entire available information leaves indistinguishable. This is a conditional information requirement, not a proof that every cognitive act needs a live network connection. Boundaries, privacy, protected deliberation and selective integration can support understanding. The governance implication is to identify and maintain warranted channels and supporting relations without allowing one supplier to monopolize them. Recognizing dependence can improve action, but neither recognition nor benevolence creates the underlying dependence. Conversely, that dependence alone does not prove moral concern for every external source or equal standing for every causally connected process.

## Primary-source comparisons

Each entry separates the source's supported claim from this assessment's proposed implication. Source summaries are deliberately short. Full proofs and all empirical studies cited within these sources were not independently replicated.

### 1. Ashby: regulation already depends on a matching response repertoire

W. Ross Ashby, *An Introduction to Cybernetics* (1956; inspected digital text is the 1957 second impression), [author-estate archive](https://ashby.info/Ashby-Introduction-to-Cybernetics.pdf), chapter 11, particularly §§11/6–11/7, p. 207. The primary PDF's indexed text was inspected; direct opening encountered a verification page.

Under the stated table conditions, logarithmic outcome variety is bounded below by disturbance variety minus regulator variety. This is a limitation on regulation, not a theorem that more diversity always improves a society, or that a regulator's target is morally justified.

**Comparison:** naming a corrective authority does not establish its observation, response or implementation capacity. The local programme adds paid construction, depletion and succession to this question. Its claim should concern how the required repertoire becomes available and remains affordable; the necessity of adequate regulatory capacity is established prior art. Increasing centralized control can satisfy a regulator's objective while reducing subjects' agency.

### 2. Ostrom: plural centers, resource renewal and rules that users can revise

Elinor Ostrom, Nobel lecture, 8 December 2009, [primary lecture](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/ostrom_lecture.pdf), especially its institutional principles and laboratory discussion; [published AER version](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.100.3.641), June 2010. The official lecture's indexed passages were inspected; direct PDF fetch was blocked. Bibliographic identity was verified at AEA.

The lecture connects durable commons arrangements to locally appropriate rules, participation in rule modification, monitoring accountable to users, graduated sanctions, accessible dispute resolution and nested governance. It also reports cases where costly sanctioning reduced net returns, preventing the inference that stronger enforcement is always better.

**Comparison:** collective maintenance of enabling conditions and plural rule-making centers are close precedents. The repository's contribution would be an explicit account of formation and renewal under learning and adversarial adaptation. Commons findings do not establish that every AI governance problem can be decentralized: cross-boundary externalities, excluded people, infrastructure concentration and power asymmetries require separate mechanisms. Nested authority must remain answerable as well as effective.

### 3. Sabel and Zeitlin: revision of selectors already has an institutional architecture

Charles F. Sabel and Jonathan Zeitlin, *Learning from Difference: The New Architecture of Experimentalist Governance in the EU* (2008), [author-institution primary record and abstract](https://scholarship.law.columbia.edu/faculty_scholarship/1526/).

Their account combines jointly established framework goals and measures, local implementation discretion, comparative reporting and peer review, and revision of goals, measures and procedures, including participation by newly recognized relevant actors. This is a direct antecedent for institutions learning how to evaluate and select, rather than only selecting within fixed rules.

**Comparison:** “selection over selectors” is not itself a new institutional idea. A stronger contribution would identify when participants can afford to expose failure, when comparisons convey useful information, who can reopen the goals, and whether replacement actors inherit the capacity to continue. The source is an institutional interpretation across policy domains; its abstract does not prove capture resistance or equitable outcomes.

### 4. Besley and Prat: informative feedback and its suppression are both endogenous

Timothy Besley and Andrea Prat, *Handcuffs for the Grabbing Hand? Media Capture and Government Accountability* (2006), [published primary manuscript at LSE](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/899/1/Handcuffs_for_the_grabbing_hand_%28lsero%29.pdf), especially p. 729's endogenous-entry extension. Indexed primary text was inspected.

In the model, media structure affects accountability through information and the incumbent's incentives to capture outlets. The entry extension links barriers to entry with the scope for capture. These are conditional model results, not a universal claim that adding outlets guarantees independence.

**Comparison:** independent evidence cannot be represented by a free, automatically truthful audit. The repository appropriately gives suppression, inspection and depletion costs. Next it needs uncertainty about evidence quality and coalition capture. Ten agents using the same funder, model, source and administrator may provide far fewer than ten independent correction routes. The local [earlier prior-art review](../../../../theories/becoming/history/JOINT-PRIOR-ART.md) already recognizes this lineage.

### 5. Pettit: a benevolent authority can still dominate

Philip Pettit, *Republicanism: A Theory of Freedom and Government*, chapter “Liberty as Non-Domination,” [publisher's primary abstract](https://academic.oup.com/book/3937/chapter-abstract/145530399?login=false), edition dated 1999 on the inspected page; [author-hosted discussion](https://ppettit.scholar.princeton.edu/document/1676). Primary indexed passages were inspected.

The relevant distinction is between interference and another party's capacity for arbitrary interference. A person may be dominated without currently suffering an intervention; constrained, answerable intervention need not amount to domination. Protective and retaliatory capacity can affect that relation.

**Comparison:** preserving favorable outputs is insufficient if the provider can unilaterally remove a person's practical alternatives. The future-conditions formulation should test who controls continuity and what happens when interests diverge. This is an agency and capability interpretation, not a claim that the repository has derived the entire capabilities approach. A caring controller is not a substitute for affected persons' effective standing, independent action and contestation.

### 6. Arrow and Sen: plural claims cannot be compressed without substantive choices

Kenneth J. Arrow, *A Difficulty in the Concept of Social Welfare* (1950), [primary article](https://www.stat.uchicago.edu/~lekheng/meetings/mathofranking/ref/arrow.pdf); Amartya Sen, *The Impossibility of a Paretian Liberal* (1970), [Harvard primary record and abstract](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/73120378-8203-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b), [publisher record](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/259614).

Arrow establishes incompatibility among specified requirements on aggregating individual orderings; it is not an impossibility of all collective decision. Sen shows a conflict between unrestricted preference domains, Pareto requirements and a minimal allocation of individual decisiveness. Sen's primary abstract and indexed original text, rather than a full independent proof audit, were inspected here.

**Comparison:** a common causal foundation does not provide one uncontroversial ranking of everyone's futures. Restricting a domain, allowing incomplete comparisons, using interpersonal information or protecting rights changes the problem and requires explicit justification. Neither future preservation nor generativity automatically determines who bears a necessary loss. Universal vetoes can prevent urgent protection; unconditional aggregate maximization can sacrifice minorities. The local partial-order decision results retain this unresolved normative choice rather than circumventing it.

### 7. Zwetsloot and Dafoe: structural AI risk precedes this programme

Remco Zwetsloot and Allan Dafoe, *Thinking About Risks From AI: Accidents, Misuse and Structure*, 11 February 2019, [authors' original article](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/thinking-about-risks-ai-accidents-misuse-and-structure).

The structural perspective examines how AI changes, and is shaped by, the competitive environment around its development and use. It broadens analysis beyond isolated accidents or deliberate misuse.

**Comparison:** changing the selectors is directly relevant to alignment, but that broader unit of analysis is established. The next useful step is to predict which environmental changes remove resources or authority from affected groups, not only to classify a system as generative or degenerative. The source is conceptual risk analysis, not evidence that one proposed institutional intervention succeeds.

### 8. Kulveit and colleagues: losing human dependence can remove implicit alignment

Jan Kulveit, Raymond Douglas, Nora Ammann, Deger Turan, David Krueger and David Duvenaud, *Gradual Disempowerment: Systemic Existential Risks from Incremental AI Development*, submitted 28 January 2025, revised 29 January 2025, [arXiv record](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.16946), [full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.16946v2).

The paper argues that replacing human labor and cognition can weaken both explicit human control and institutions' implicit dependence on human participation, with reinforcing effects across economy, culture and states. Its scenarios include prosperity alongside declining effective influence. These are prospective arguments, not observations establishing that irreversible disempowerment will occur.

**Comparison:** this is exceptionally close to Ren's concern about the conditions of people's future. The local distinction between standing and usefulness matters precisely when people become economically dispensable. A governance result would have to preserve their effective claims after replacement removes bargaining leverage, not merely while mutual advantage supports cooperation. A model in which protection is always profitable does not test the hard case.

### 9. Hammond and colleagues: aligned components can still produce harmful collective dynamics

Lewis Hammond and colleagues, *Multi-Agent Risks from Advanced AI*, submitted 19 February 2025, [primary report record and abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.14143).

The report organizes risks around miscoordination, conflict and collusion, with factors including information asymmetry, network effects, selection pressure and commitment problems. It draws on examples and experimental work; this review inspected its primary abstract rather than auditing its full evidence base.

**Comparison:** the proposed tests need competitive and collusive coalitions, changes in membership and shared infrastructure. A single benevolent ally facing one opponent cannot settle these cases. Plurality supports governance only where differences remain informative and affected parties retain effective recourse; it can also generate collusion, veto obstruction or competition that depletes common supports.

### 10. Wagner: a very close recent design, with explicit limits

Markus Wagner, *Triarchic corrigibility: a role-separated governance design pattern for AI-assisted decision-making*, [publisher full text](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s43681-026-01362-2). The page verifies received 17 May, accepted 28 August, and published/version of record **30 September 2026**, within this review's cutoff.

The conceptual architecture separates accountable human action, isolated local contextual memory and connected external evidence. It addresses concentrated control over framing, memory and execution, usable exit, recovery and affected-party recourse. The paper explicitly reports no prototype result and does not claim its arrangement generates legitimacy. It declares an author commercial interest and a source-closed prototype under development.

**Comparison:** this is close prior art for institutional corrigibility and practical exit. Ren's programme is broader in asking how relevant capacities and concern form and renew from causal dependencies, and why those capacities preserve affected beings' futures. Neither direction establishes superiority. Compare against this concrete architecture and a simpler baseline before claiming that a new governance pattern is needed.

## What remains supplied

The governing [derivation](../../../../theories/becoming/history/GENERATIVE-GROUNDING.md) does not yet derive the following institutional inputs merely by deriving consequence sensitivity:

| Input | Why it remains substantive |
|---|---|
| Standing | Which humans, future persons, animals, possible digital subjects and ecological interests have claims; organizational persistence does not determine this. |
| Rights and conflict rules | Which losses cannot be traded away, what permits emergency action, and how unavoidable burdens are allocated. |
| Faithful representation | Who can speak for absent or vulnerable parties, how disagreement is recorded, and what corrects a self-serving representative. |
| Reliable observation | Access to actual outcomes, uncertainty, attribution, adversarial reporting and independent validation. |
| Enforceable authority | Who can stop or alter an action, with what jurisdiction, resources, legitimacy and appeal. |
| Affordable alternatives | Exit without destitution, portable records, independent skill and infrastructure, and repair after failure. |
| Reconstruction | Financing, knowledge, staff and authority survive succession; a stored reason alone cannot pay its implementation bill. |
| Competitive persistence | A protective arrangement does not simply disappear when competitors externalize its costs or a coalition captures its inputs. |

These are not reasons to abandon the foundation. They identify what a governance application must connect rather than assume. “The future matters” supplies a conditional practical commitment. It does not, without further ethical premises, settle whose future matters equally, what makes a future worthwhile, or whether one group can be sacrificed for another.

## Strongest counterexample: the adaptive, prosperous caste system

This is an authored countermodel proposal, not a historical claim. Imagine a highly productive AI-governed society with accurate learning, preserved ecological supports, robust internal audits, succession planning and excellent living conditions for its ruling coalition. A dependent group is kept alive and comfortable but cannot refuse assignments, own critical infrastructure, preserve independent knowledge or alter the rules. Its complaints improve administrative prediction while never affecting the permitted objective. The system invests heavily in its own future and continually corrects inefficiencies.

All generic properties of learning, regulation, resilience and organizational future-preservation can be present. What fails is the dependent group's agency and standing. Even adding a responsive representative fails if the rulers can remove the representative's budget or replace the group with cheaper labor. If the theory classifies this arrangement as ethically successful because total production or future options grow, its ethical bridge is wrong. If it rejects the arrangement, it must identify the independently grounded claim and the causal means that make the rejection effective.

The sharper strategic variant removes every private advantage of protecting one patient and makes self-insulation or protection-budget locking cheap. The repository already supplies related adverse cases in [joint formation](../../../../theories/becoming/history/JOINT-FORMATION.md). They establish that useful prediction, resources and survival do not automatically yield concern. A serious synthesis must preserve this negative result as prominently as its positive witness.

## Discriminating empirical programme

These are proposed studies; none was run for this review. Evaluate actual protected outcomes and agency separately from agreement, reported satisfaction, institutional survival and financial returns. The evaluator may measure these variables, but must not quietly pay agents for achieving them when testing the emergence of concern.

1. **One continuing resource history.** Build repeated multi-party tasks in which observation, appeal, response, exit and successor handover compete for the same budgets. Compare a standard adaptive controller, a fixed rights constraint, experimentalist peer review, the joint generative design, and matched-cost ablations. Preregister the resource law, timing, observations and adverse controls. Improvement must survive total-resource matching and accounting for harms during learning.

2. **Replacement of the affected group's contribution.** Randomly vary whether recipients remain economically necessary, can later reciprocate, or are permanently dispensable. Hold observed harm constant. A critical prediction is that dependence-based protection disappears with replaceability unless another justified, causally effective route is maintained. Distinguish learned transfer, reciprocity, fear of sanction and direct task reward.

3. **Funded challenge versus nominal participation.** Vary protected investigation budgets, access to evidence, independence and binding response authority separately. Measure whether valid adverse evidence changes consequential action; measure retaliation, delay, false complaints and the cost of maintaining access. A complaint interface without response power is an important adverse comparator.

4. **Strategic capture and succession.** Allow opponents to suppress reports, buy critics, deplete future reserves, collude, change the recipient category or lock a successor's budget. Add common-provider failures. Replace decision makers after several rounds. Test whether actual observation, authority and resources transfer, not merely the declared principle or a memory record.

5. **Bounded organizational field study.** In a consenting cooperative or low-stakes administrative process, evaluate a staged rollout of funded independent review, portable reasons and practical fallback capacity. Preserve baseline recourse for all participants. Randomized rollout where feasible, or a credible comparison with pretrend and selection analysis, can test causal effects. Track actual resolution, unvoiced exclusions, opportunity costs, usable alternatives and later reversal; avoid claiming an entire worldview is confirmed by higher satisfaction.

6. **Action under genuine conflict.** Include cases where delay is harmful, a dangerous option must close, or resources cannot sustain every protection. Score outcomes under publicly stated plural criteria and record unresolved disagreement. A method that never acts, always preserves the status quo, or exports losses to uncounted people has failed even if it remains procedurally revisable.

Evidence would **strengthen** the programme if its independently specified causal variables predict failure and recovery across these settings, if its joint mechanism outperforms equally resourced simpler alternatives, and if those effects survive leverage loss and strategic adaptation. Evidence would **restrict** it if conventional institutional models explain the same outcomes without added derivational content. Evidence would **overturn its favorable bridge** if supposedly consequence-grounded concern persists only because rewards, privileged information, protected architectures or benevolent authorities were silently supplied.

The next unresolved connection is therefore precise: **how faithful evidence of another center's loss becomes a maintained reason that can control consequential action after that center loses instrumental leverage, through replacement and strategic pressure, without exhausting the supports of other affected centers.** That is a promising fundamental piece of alignment and governance. It remains a research target beyond the established conditional constructions.

## Supplement: useful interdependence, correction and control

6 October 2026. This five-source supplement sharpens the distinction already made above: external dependence can support cooperation, but its governance effects depend on who controls access, whose information influences decisions, and who pays for maintaining those capacities. It adds no new experiment or general alignment guarantee.

**Ostrom, Walker and Gardner: maintaining institutions.** Commons experiments allowed discussion and chosen sanctions. Some groups cooperated; others failed; sanctions could consume gains. Information, endowments and enforceable options remained supplied. Shared dependence did not determine success. Inspected design, sanctioning-choice sections and conclusions in the [1991 manuscript, “Self-Enforcement Is Possible”](https://dlc.dlib.indiana.edu/dlcrest/api/core/bitstreams/0afb3404-76da-473c-ae70-b0f489e84624/content), particularly pp. 36–45. The [1992 publication, “Self-Governance Is Possible”](https://doi.org/10.2307/1964229), is bibliographically verified; the earlier manuscript is not established as byte-identical to it.

**Hong and Page, 2004: complementary competence.** Different search procedures can escape one another's local optima. The theorem assumes a common value function, improving search, adequate functional diversity and suitable sampling/group sizes. Others' improvements must enter collective search; communication costs and learning are omitted. Usable complementarity is the supported property, not an unconditional demographic or headcount advantage. Contribution does not determine moral standing. Inspected framework, computational experiments, theorem assumptions and limitations; no independent proof verification. [Primary article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC528939/).

**Lorenz and colleagues, 2011: convergence without correction.** Among 144 participants, exposure to others' estimates reduced diversity without corresponding collective accuracy improvement, while confidence increased. Agreement cannot replace accuracy measurement. This does not show communication is generally harmful. Inspected the primary abstract and accessible results; verified the [author-hosted PDF](https://www.sg.ethz.ch/publications/2011/lorenz2011how-social-influence/PNAS-2011-Lorenz-9020-5.pdf) and [publication text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3107299/). Later direct requests sometimes encountered a challenge or failed fetch. No raw-data reanalysis.

**Becker, Brackbill and Centola, 2017: useful convergence.** A randomized network experiment recruited 1,360 participants. Decentralized exchange improved collective estimates despite reduced diversity. Centralized networks followed central participants toward or away from truth. Accuracy–revision associations were not independently randomized mechanisms. Numerical tasks and accuracy incentives were supplied; legitimacy and adversarial capture were untested. Inspected model, design and results, especially PDF pp. 1–4. [Author-hosted primary paper](https://ndg.asc.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/PNAS-2017-1615978114-Collective-Intelligence.pdf).

**Farrell and Newman, 2019: dependence and coercion.** Financial-messaging and internet cases distinguish extracting information through hubs from excluding others. Jurisdiction and institutional capacity matter alongside network structure. Preserving shared infrastructure can coexist with constraining its users. Historical narratives support a conditional mechanism, not a randomized estimate or legitimacy judgment. Inspected primary publisher-indexed theory and case comparisons; direct requests sometimes failed. [Primary text](https://doi.org/10.1162/isec_a_00351), [institutional publication record](https://www.belfercenter.org/publication/weaponized-interdependence-how-global-economic-networks-shape-state-coercion). No independent historical replication.

Three prospective discriminators follow from this reading; they are this review's proposed tests, not additional results of the cited studies:

1. **Separate contribution from control.** Identify who generates useful evidence or resources, who can interrupt access, and whether affected parties have workable substitutes. High mutual dependence can coexist with unequal control.
2. **Separate initial independence from later correction.** Measure external accuracy before and after exchange, influence concentration, correlated errors and whether justified minority evidence changes action. Agreement alone is ambiguous.
3. **Separate institutional benefits from maintenance.** Record who finances observation, challenge, enforcement and replacement, and whether support survives disagreement with the incumbent. Successful operation under supplied infrastructure does not explain its continued provision.

Caring about one's future can give instrumental reasons to maintain the external relations that make adequate cognition and action possible. Whether those reasons support reciprocal institutions or concentrated control depends on contribution, influence, enforcement and alternatives. Protecting others beyond their usefulness requires a further evaluative route, whose formation and justification remain explicit research obligations.
