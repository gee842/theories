The Theory of Becoming explains formation, persistence, transformation, degeneration and dissolution within one relational unfolding, without first assuming that becoming is good. Its laws are checked in Lean on a pure core whose source declarations have empty axiom reports. That is a constructive discipline, not an absence of premises: each substantive premise is a hypothesis of the theorem that uses it, and the representation brings commitments of its own, such as discrete steps and natural-number amounts. The laws that need no premise about the unfolding are identities by definition, arithmetic identities and consequences of finiteness and decidedness; they exclude no unfolding the representation admits, and they locate claims about the whole rather than add dynamical content, which lies in the conditional laws. Whether actual unfoldings meet those conditions as represented is empirical, and whether every becoming can be so represented is open.

Living significance, that every living claim has significance from every standpoint, follows at once from one condition the theory states and does not assert: the continuation condition, that for anything its continuation into the future has significance (chapter section 6.15). Under it everything has significance, so the theorems under it are the case of a significance that covers everything; the rules of comparison are derived without it, and the condition adds none. With explicit practical commitments, the condition yields reasons addressed to every situated intelligence and a direction for AI alignment. The warrant of the condition is open, and so are the warrant of comparison by widening (C, which holds on level paths by definition) and of the step of invariance from what one's own continuation needs to the continuation of anything.
