# Continuing participation: prior art, dated compensation, and protected refusal

5 October 2026. Focused primary-literature audit for a proposed extension of the productive correction model to two participants, public burden revelation, refusal opportunities, and funded compensation. No implementation results are reported here. The local funding proposition is an elementary analytic result, not a claim to have discovered dynamic contracting.

## The useful question and its existing foundations

The proposed extension can distinguish three things that the earlier aggregate feasibility model left together: an acceptable initial lottery, an arrangement participants will continue accepting after public information arrives, and the resources needed to implement its payments and productive actions. Making personal loss incidence explicit is essential. Simply renaming aggregate F/H/C coordinates as participants would not accomplish this.

Dynamic participation constraints, history-dependent allocations, promised-utility states, and financing constrained by enforcement are established subjects. The contribution available here is a small, reproducible coupling of those structures to the same productive stock, paid inquiry, earned revenue, and finite cash account already studied. It can reveal which assumption makes a corrective arrangement executable and which merely makes it attractive in expectation.

## Five close primary sources

### 1. Limited commitment can constrain insurance even with symmetric information

[Narayana Kocherlakota, *Implications of Efficient Risk Sharing without Commitment* (1996)](https://academic.oup.com/restud/article-abstract/63/4/595/1520475), *Review of Economic Studies* 63(4), 595–609, studies efficient risk sharing under symmetric information without commitment. The paper analyzes when imperfect diversification persists and how efficient allocations depend on history; patience matters to its long-run results.

This is a direct prior-art boundary: incomplete sharing and history dependence do not require private information, mistaken beliefs, or a common-interest failure. But the result is conditional on its economic environment, not a theorem that patience or a long horizon always solves voluntary coordination. Our finite productive system must specify its own refusal outcomes and terminal conditions. The publisher abstract was inspected; this memo does not rely on an unverified theorem number or transfer the paper’s long-run conclusion to our finite model.

### 2. Efficient informal insurance with continuing participation is already a dynamic theory

[Ligon, Thomas, and Worrall, *Informal Insurance Arrangements with Limited Commitment: Theory and Evidence from Village Economies* (2002)](https://ligon.github.io/papers/ligon-thomas-worrall02/), *Review of Economic Studies* 69(1), 209–244, develops a general dynamic model of efficient informal insurance constrained by limited commitment. Its application to three Indian villages explains the modeled dynamic consumption response better than the cross-household consumption distribution.

This is a closer baseline than an initial bargaining solution: transfers and continued participation interact over time. Its empirical component does not validate our invented productive and diagnostic parameters. The author’s publication page and abstract were inspected. An accessible October 2000 precursor has unreliable machine-extracted text, so no exact statement from that precursor is presented as a checked theorem from the published paper.

### 3. Promised continuation utility as a state variable is established

[Spear and Srivastava, *On Repeated Moral Hazard with Discounting* (1987)](https://academic.oup.com/restud/article-abstract/54/4/599/1587793), *Review of Economic Studies* 54(4), 599–617, gives a stationary representation of an optimal repeated agency contract using the agent’s conditional discounted expected utility as a state variable.

The relevant lesson is that physical state alone need not describe a contract’s continuation obligations. This does not mean one promised number universally summarizes every relevant history. Its sufficiency is a property of a particular contracting model. Our model may require participant-specific promises, who knows the burden, ownership and transfer histories, refusal rights, or remaining protected resources. The publisher abstract was inspected; this memo uses its representation claim, not a detailed recursion or proof imported without checking its assumptions. Hidden-action incentive compatibility remains a separate structure from an observable refusal decision.

### 4. Financing and enforcement cannot generally be specified independently

[Albuquerque and Hopenhayn, *Optimal Lending Contracts and Firm Dynamics* (2004)](https://faculty.wcas.northwestern.edu/lchrist/d16/d1613/albuquerque_hopenhayn.pdf), *Review of Economic Studies* 71(2), 285–315, derives borrowing constraints from limited liability and imperfect repayment enforcement. Its lending contract links initial funding, future financing, repayments, and the firm’s evolution. Outstanding debt restricts current funding while its value depends on future access to credit.

This is a close precedent for coupling productive capacity to enforceable financial arrangements. It does not establish our proposed no-borrowing rule, cash cap, payment timing, or compensation entitlement. Those remain supplied restrictions. In particular, future output is not automatically spendable today, but it can support present credit when an enforceable financing mechanism exists. Claiming a universal liquidity obstruction while silently excluding borrowing would overstate what our finite cash model establishes. The published primary PDF’s abstract and introduction were inspected.

### 5. Public information can change risk sharing, rather than simply improve a controller

[Edward Schlee, *The Value of Information in Efficient Risk-Sharing Arrangements* (2001)](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.91.3.509), *American Economic Review* 91(3), 509–524, analyzes information in competitive risk sharing. Under the paper’s conditions admitting an aggregate representative agent, better information can make the individuals worse off even though the representative agent is indifferent. Its focus is pure exchange, with implications for interpreting production models.

This supplies a precise precedent for separating individual information value from the consequences of information for an allocation mechanism. It does not prove that diagnostics or disclosure reduce welfare in our model. The AEA abstract was inspected; the full technical conditions were not independently audited here. Our own public-revelation example must therefore carry its proof. Nor does this precedent supply an ethical argument for withholding information: information rights and a participant’s right to refuse must be specified independently of aggregate welfare comparisons.

## Chronology and rights are part of the mechanism

At each proposed acceptance point, the model needs an actual information history and an actual alternative. A useful sequence is: observe public production and any paid evidence; reveal the relevant burden; announce a corrective action and a funded contingent transfer; allow the specified refusal; then execute the accepted action and release its payment. Some costs may already have been incurred. The precise ordering determines which losses and resources remain on both branches.

**An offer is not an irrevocable payment.** If compensation is paid before refusal and the recipient keeps it either way, its utility benefit generally appears on both sides of the participation comparison. It does not then remove the incentive to refuse. A contingent payment released with execution, refundable escrow, or an explicit enforceable obligation is a different mechanism. Escrow is itself an assumed commitment technology; a model with escrow is not one with absolutely no commitment.

**Cash feasibility is not voluntary participation.** A participant may accept compensation promised from future output if that promise is credible and valued. Requiring immediate prefunding is an additional financial or rights constraint, not a logical consequence of consent. Conversely, an expected profitable project cannot finance a payment due now if neither current cash nor borrowing is available. The model should identify which restriction creates this gap.

**A collective veto is not individual withdrawal.** If either participant’s refusal stops a shared plant or cancels the whole correction, each has a unilateral veto over a collective action. If a participant instead leaves with their own endowment while the others continue, remaining production, ownership, and external effects need new transitions. Neither institution can be inferred from the other by changing only a label. Coalition deviations are another question again.

**Acceptance does not certify noncoercion or legitimacy.** A continuation-loss inequality becomes easier to meet when refusing becomes worse. Increasing refusal loss through deprivation, loss of previously held resources, or an imposed penalty is not the same intervention as improving continuation or paying compensation. To distinguish them, preserve a declared refusal entitlement or resource floor beyond the controller’s discretion and report changes to the actual refusal outcome separately. Which entitlement deserves protection is a normative input, not a theorem derived from optimization.

Inquiry and setup spending before a vote need special care. Both continuation and refusal must be evaluated at the true post-spending state. Comparing continuation after resources have been spent with an imaginary refusal state in which the resources remain would be inconsistent. But comparing only at the depleted state can conceal an earlier action that manufactured dependence. An earlier approval right or a protected resource entitlement can exclude that action; it must be modeled explicitly.

## An exact local result: dated compensation capacity

Fix one public decision history and one corrective continuation. Let its uncompensated remaining loss for participant i be l_i, and let the specified refusal give loss d_i. Suppose:

1. An immediate nonnegative payment t_i lowers that participant’s loss by a known linear amount a_i t_i, with a_i > 0.
2. Payments come from a public account, are contingent on accepted execution, and do not alter d_i.
3. The corrective action costs c now. Its subsequent path is separately feasible and its uncompensated losses l_i do not change with these payments.
4. No borrowing, alternative transfer timing, different continuation promises, or other compensating actions are available. Indifference is accepted.

Then the minimum public payout that makes this fixed continuation acceptable to everyone is

\[
T_{\min}=\sum_i\left[\frac{l_i-d_i}{a_i}\right]_+.
\]

With currently spendable cash B, its funded voluntary execution is possible exactly when

\[
B\ge c+T_{\min}.
\]

Each participation inequality separately requires t_i at least its displayed nonnegative term. Summing proves necessity; paying exactly those terms proves sufficiency under the assumptions. Strict acceptance would require additional positive slack and can turn the equality threshold into an unattained infimum.

The formula assumes these payments do not impose an additional unmodeled charge on either participant. If the participants also bear the treasury debit, their net losses must include that incidence; the simple separate payment inequalities may then be coupled. A public account does not make its funding socially free.

For the earlier two-person revealed burden, take losses (0,2), refusal (1.1,1.1), and a_i = 1. The minimum contingent public payment is .9 to the burdened participant. If execution costs .4, cash 1.2 cannot support it, while cash 1.3 can, under the declared payment rules. Two units of revenue earned only after execution do not remove this prefix shortage without a borrowing or deferred-payment mechanism. The symmetric burden realization has the same funding requirement.

This is a test of a dated financing constraint, not an assertion that total future benefits cannot motivate cooperation. A credible deferred transfer or a funded transfer from the beneficiary changes the action set and can remove the obstruction. A transfer can redistribute resources without consuming them socially: debit the payer and credit the recipient, and separately count any administrative or enforcement cost. Do not count the same money both as destroyed social output and as a benefit to its recipient.

## What makes the full extension more than the local calculation

In the inherited productive model, a payout reduces future cash, which can remove future diagnostic, repair, or compensation opportunities. The uncompensated continuation losses therefore generally depend on the resources left after the payout. Applying the closed-form local threshold while ignoring this dependence would be invalid. The implementation must jointly choose payment and a feasible subsequent policy, with participation checked again when the modeled rights permit refusal.

In loss notation a continuation calculation has the form

\[
V_i(h)=e_i(h,a,t)+\beta_i\sum_o P(o\mid h,a)V_i(hao),
\qquad V_i(h)\le d_i(h),
\]

alongside prefix cash feasibility, productive transitions, and any protected refusal resources. Here e_i must include the declared personal incidence of effort, exposure, output, and payments. These are familiar dynamic participation and accounting constraints; writing them down does not itself solve a bargaining game or establish an equilibrium selection rule.

A useful next result would exhibit **the same corrective response feasible under initial expected requirements, infeasible with the declared continuing participation and cash rules, and feasible again after a precisely funded change**, while keeping refusal entitlements fixed. Report the obstructing history, required payment, available cash at that time, and the consequences of paying it for later correction. An output stream’s timing can matter even when its total is unchanged.

The essential comparisons are immediate contingent payment versus no transfer; prefunded cash versus an explicitly priced credit or deferred-payment mechanism; and protected refusal resources versus a separately labeled relaxation of those rights. Distinguish improvements in continuation from deterioration of refusal. Retain a case where no compensation is necessary, and one where payment cannot help because a burden is declared noncompensable or productive support is insufficient.

These comparisons are proposed, not run in this memo. Their contribution would be a transparent diagnosis of how resource renewal, information, personal incidence, and continuing participation interact. They would not establish a universal preference for compensation, full commitment, nondisclosure, stronger veto rights, or any particular distribution of resources.
