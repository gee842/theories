## A lived scene

The morning path passes bedrooms, a shaded breakfast table, and a shallow planted basin. A resident stops to watch water insects. Someone else is still asleep. Further along, children are testing a temporary boardwalk with an older neighbor who wants the path to remain easy to navigate. A machine is tending plants behind a marked work boundary. Nearby, a gardener works by hand because she likes knowing this particular patch of earth.

Tonight the community will choose whether an underused paved court becomes a wetland, a dance floor, or a seasonal combination. None of the people attending must earn the right to live here by attending.

## A day whose security survives a changed mind

Mira has spent a season helping a group plan a habitat garden. This morning she tells her friend she wants to pause. They arrange a handover of the part she understands; someone else may choose to continue, or a proposed addition may wait. The conversation concerns a shared commitment. It does not alter her home or the services she can use.

She leaves her own doorway, takes breakfast beneath the kitchen awning, and brings a bowl to a neighbor who is recovering from an illness. A care practitioner arrives by arrangement; Mira does not become the neighbor's compulsory substitute carer. Across the court, the provision room has food, household supplies, and a place to request something missing. The ordinary daily offer is ample enough to choose among meals and household goods. Personal possessions and a private kitchen remain possible. This scene should show the home's threshold, breakfast, care visit, and provision room as one reachable place rather than four unrelated entitlements on a diagram.

Later, Mira walks into the instrument library wearing a teal shirt and carrying an orange bag. She borrows a small telescope: a friend has invited her to spend the evening looking at the sky. They can use an available model immediately; a more specialized instrument requires a reservation because another group is using it. Nobody asks whether their inquiry will earn money or whether Mira completed her garden project. She may return the instrument unused tomorrow. In the evening she closes her door and is alone.

**Visual sequence:** Carry Mira and the same doorway through leaving a project, ordinary provision, a tool-room choice, and an evening at home. A small conversation and the unchanged availability of things make the freedom perceptible. Shared provision includes creative means; scarce equipment and human attention still need arrangements that people can understand.

## How dependable provision is constructed

The proposed neighborhood is connected to a wider provision network: homes, food, water, energy, transport, care, household goods, tools, and repair have identified providers and maintenance responsibilities. Residents have a durable claim to ordinary provision independent of employment, project membership, volunteering, or game standing. A move between communities carries access continuity; a dispute with a local group has a route to an independent service contact. These are institutional design choices, not consequences that machinery supplies automatically.

The physical basis is the [automation-to-abundance program](../foundations/automation-to-shared-abundance.md): useful production helps equip further capacity, learned methods can spread, and a share of the resulting capability supplies everyday life and tools for new undertakings. Availability must be tested in complete service terms: what arrives, what breaks, what waits, whose time is required, and whose need is going unmet. The garden's own appearance cannot establish abundance elsewhere in its supply network.

An illustrative interruption makes the arrangement visible. A kitchen washing machine develops a fault. Clean reserve vessels and an agreed nearby washing service keep meals available while a willing, qualified maintenance team handles repair. The service record distinguishes machine time, remaining human effort, and actual recovery. A robot's measured assistance may reduce lifting or repetitive handling; it is not described as having eliminated a task merely because it appears in the scene.

Remaining essential human work uses explicit, acceptable arrangements: people choose trained roles with manageable commitments, the support and terms they accept, and a way to hand over or leave. Planning provides spare capacity and continuity before depending on a person. During partial automation those terms may include compensation, shared staffing, or reciprocal agreements; none can make another person's basic security conditional on taking the role. If insufficient people want to perform a necessary task, the community must face the gap—simplify the service, improve its terms and tools, obtain assistance, or defer an optional expansion. It cannot declare the work unnecessary or quietly assign it to a less protected group. Care also includes valued human relationships whose presence should be chosen by everyone involved.

## Assumptions, values, and proposal

- **Speculative assumptions:** In the broad-automation version, routine construction, material recovery, horticultural support, and utility maintenance can be carried out reliably with substantial machine assistance. Food and housing security are separately established social arrangements; machines alone do not guarantee them.
- **Desired values:** A materially secure ordinary life; ecological care; privacy; accessible shared space; room for both solitude and collective creation. Rest is legitimate without a promise of future contribution.
- **Proposed design:** Housing organized around connected garden rooms, with a legible public circulation system and a service network that keeps daily logistics from dominating the landscape.

## Purpose and people

This is a neighborhood first. Its residents include children, older people, disabled people, people who love communal life, and people who mostly want a quiet home. Gardens offer food, habitat, play, sensory pleasure, and changing seasons; they are not assumed to supply the entire neighborhood's diet. Visitors can enter designated public spaces without becoming members of a gardening culture.

## Spatial organization and adjacencies

Private dwellings open through personal thresholds into small shared courts. Courts connect to a public garden walk, a common kitchen, care rooms, indoor gathering spaces, workshops, and a transit edge. Quiet rooms have a route home that avoids performances and communal meals. Children's play overlooks everyday occupied spaces without making every resident a supervisor.

A continuous, sheltered, step-free route links entrances, homes, gardens, and services. Firm surfaces, resting places, clear landmarks, and tactile or audible information complement planting. This route works without glasses, accounts, spoken commands, or participation in an immersive experience. Stairs and adventurous paths offer additional experiences, not the only way through.

Wet areas, productive plots, wild habitat, and heavily used lawns remain distinct where their requirements conflict. A service access edge connects workshops, stores, waste sorting, and equipment repair; maintenance can reach garden systems without routinely crossing private homes.

## Visible and invisible infrastructure

Water movement, seasonal planting, and some material reuse are visible enough to understand and question. Pipes, power distribution, communications, drainage, and service movement mostly occupy accessible utility spaces. Machine work areas have understandable boundaries. Residents can discover what is being sensed and request a human explanation. A beautiful pond is not presumed to be potable-water treatment; each function needs its own evidence and design.

## Governance, access, refusal, and exit

Baseline housing and essential services do not depend on volunteering or agreement with the neighborhood's aesthetic. Residents can propose reversible trials; decisions affecting noise, privacy, disability access, habitat, or displacement require affected people to be heard. Public access rules and scarce-space allocations are inspectable and appealable.

People can decline garden labor, social events, behavioral sensing, and personalized displays while retaining a usable home and route through the neighborhood. Moving away should preserve material security rather than function as punishment. An independent route for complaints matters when the dispute is with popular community members.

## When the power to build meets a place worth keeping

A group proposes another shared room. Recoverable components and automated assembly could make the addition easy to commission compared with earlier construction. Their first footprint takes the shaded ground around a mature tree. At a model of the proposal, a neighbor traces the seat where her father rests each afternoon; another points out the roots and the habitat that would be disturbed. The group shifts and reshapes its proposed footprint, keeping a familiar patch of shade and examining the new route and material requirements. The tree and its living conditions still need ecological and construction assessment; moving a line on the model is not proof of protection.

This is a proposed episode of the culture understanding its own power. Technical capacity makes a request executable, while the people affected help determine whether and how it should happen. A room can be reassembled faster than a mature living environment can recover. Components may remain available for another configuration; relationships and habitat need their own continuity. The visual sequence should carry the same tree, seat, and changed footprint through discussion and the resulting place, so the generative principle becomes a choice a reader can see.

## Dependencies and real tensions

The neighborhood relies on energy, imported materials and some food, watershed relationships, spare parts, ecological expertise, and accountable maintenance. Habitat and water conditions must be observed over time; a rendering cannot establish ecological success.

More visitors can mean better public life and less resident quiet. Dense housing can preserve regional land while reducing private outdoor space. Hand gardening can be meaningful and exclusionary if it becomes a prestige hierarchy. A failure branch turns the project into an immaculate, sensor-heavy enclave whose ecological appearance hides waste and whose residents informally police belonging. Early signs include inaccessible detours, unexplained surveillance, and maintenance work displaced onto people with little choice.

## Partial and broad automation

With partial automation, keep systems simpler, phase garden conversion, and explicitly fund skilled maintenance, care, and accessible transport. Do not make unpaid resident labor the hidden operating model. With broad automation, more frequent adaptation becomes plausible, but shared decisions and ecological recovery still take time. Construction speed is not a reason to replace established habitats or residents' attachments casually.

## Material life: a cup returns

**September 7 addition:** AI design interpretation of [Ren's regenerative-material direction](../archive/004-ren-regenerative-materials.txt), developed in [Regenerative material civilization](../foundations/regenerative-material-civilization.md).

After breakfast, a visitor leaves a shared metal cup at a return shelf beside the garden path. Someone else takes a favorite cup home; keeping a personal object remains ordinary. The shared service is useful because clean cups are easy to find and return, including for someone unable to carry a tray. Access needs no personal consumption record. A neighborhood can evaluate aggregate losses without tracking everyone's movements.

Behind the common kitchen, returns enter a dirty receiving area, then washing and inspection, then clean storage. A dented cup goes to an appropriate repair process; one that cannot safely serve again joins a separated material stream for a suitable recovery facility. It is not casually melted and reprinted beside breakfast. The material, fabrication method, food-contact suitability, washing demand, losses, and actual lifetime remain evaluation questions. Metal is the proposed example, not an automatic environmental verdict.

The same neighborhood maintains a shared store of tools and compatible replacement parts, using public designs where appropriate. A worn seat can receive a replacement component while its familiar surface and memories remain. Choosing a new shape includes accounting for energy, transport, unusable residues, and the useful stock tied up during repair. Expanding the service faster than its washing, storage, or recovery capacity would make abundance unreliable.

This arrangement expresses long-horizon care through ordinary convenience. Technical material recovery runs alongside observation and care of soil, habitat, and water; a repaired object does not compensate automatically for a damaged ecosystem.
