## The elevator becomes a passage between worlds

Two friends leave a warm forest court. One is visiting an ocean world for the first time; the other has helped build its soundscape. They choose the same shared experience at an ordinary lift. Inside, the familiar doorway appears to fill with dark water. Forest sounds recede; a slow field of blue light and distant ocean sound prepares the arrival. Nobody needs to believe that their bodies have teleported. For the duration of the passage, vertical travel becomes crossing into another world.

The doors open. An enormous underwater landscape seems to extend beyond a real hall. The ocean horizon, lighting, and sounds continue from the cabin into the room; a physical curved bench they can both touch appears to sit on a reef ledge. People are gathered around real tables while a projected school of fish passes through an opt-in overlay. This is a place with its own ongoing culture: an embodied movement group meets the ocean-observation community, and tonight their work becomes a public performance. The hosts can distinguish actual observations, reconstructions, and invented creatures when someone wants to understand what they are seeing.

The effect depends on coherent arrival. A decorated lift followed by an unrelated corridor would lose the crossing. Doorway, destination sightlines, acoustics, physical surfaces, and a shared spatial anchor are designed as one sequence. Specific display techniques, tracking quality, comfort, and integration remain prototype questions. The building does not claim literal teleportation or a perfected seamless AR system.

| Physical sequence | Chosen experience |
| --- | --- |
| Forest court and legible lift lobby | Departure from a canopy settlement; the next world and its sensory conditions are introduced |
| Conventional cabin moves to a known floor | A brief passage through ocean darkness; ordinary controls and a quiet setting remain available |
| Doors open onto a step-free landing and a real gathering hall | The same ocean horizon continues across the threshold, making arrival feel spatially continuous |
| Shared seats, tables, acoustic surfaces, and people | A reef commons for conversation, study, movement, or watching |
| Ordinary return controls and accessible circulation | Leave the ocean world without completing a story, earning access, or explaining why |

People who choose different overlays still share identifiable bodies, surfaces, and destinations. Someone who removes their glasses arrives in a complete blue-lit civic hall with a bench, companions, and usable wayfinding. Shared sensory effects accommodate the people actually traveling together; one rider's spectacle preference does not override another's need for a quiet journey. A display failure ends the overlay, while conventional transport and physical arrival remain independently designed obligations.

## The culture authors its environment

World-making becomes a public creative practice. A group can begin with one room, share its reusable components and experience assets, invite visitors, learn from their responses, and propose a larger home. Ocean study, movement, quiet contemplation, and myth can meet without all participants interpreting the place identically. The building supports the creation of new ways of being together.

Its generative test is whether a world leaves other people more able to create, understand, and participate. That might mean a public sound library, an accessible spatial convention, a repaired room, or relationships through which the next group can begin. Groups learn to name the physical, social, and ecological dependencies beneath their imaginary landscapes. Decisions about expansion consider those dependencies and the lives already present. An immersive world's admirers gain an audience, not permanent authority over the building.

The [City of Shared Quests](06-city-of-shared-quests.md) develops the related but distinct possibility of a persistent town whose residents live through guilds, shared lore, roles, and adventures. The House concentrates on passages between different experienced realities within an architectural commons.

## Assumptions, values, and proposal

- **Speculative assumptions:** Reliable spatial displays and accessible interfaces can support optional shared overlays; machine assistance may eventually make some physical reconfiguration easier. A lift moves bodies conventionally. It can stage a narrative transition but is not a literal teleporter.
- **Desired values:** Expressive freedom, embodied pleasure, shared physical legibility, consent, friendship, and ordinary refuge. A person need not enjoy immersion or performance to belong.
- **Proposed design:** A mixed-use building with a stable public core and adaptable experience rooms. Elevators and threshold chambers connect different atmospheres while preserving continuous, unmediated access.

## Purpose and people

The House supports dancers, storytellers, game makers, visiting families, quiet spectators, and people passing through. Workshops allow residents to make worlds together. Some experiences are elaborate; others are a carpet, changing light, and people moving without explanation. Neither an audience nor a measurable output is required.

## Spatial organization and adjacencies

A welcoming entrance leads to an orientation hall, daylight court, quiet lounge, and clearly identifiable circulation core. Experience rooms cluster around shared preparation spaces: changing areas, accessible toilets, equipment support, storage, and places to recover. Acoustically demanding rooms sit apart from quiet rooms and any residences. Messy fabrication has its own service connection and appropriate separation from public circulation.

Lifts create ordinary accessible vertical travel as well as optional narrative transitions. Step-free circulation connects the public program; unmediated wayfinding remains available throughout. Stairs provide additional routes but do not substitute for accessible vertical access. Life safety, evacuation assistance, and failure of vertical transport require later specialist design; no proposed display system carries those responsibilities.

Every experience has a legible physical threshold, an accessible exit, and a nearby place to pause. An ordinary bypass lets someone cross the building without entering a performance or story. Physical doors and circulation stay identifiable when displays fail. Different overlays may transform appearances, but should not misrepresent obstacles, access conditions, or another person's consent.

## Visible and invisible infrastructure

The building openly presents its shared physical map, occupied rooms, accessible routes, and experience conditions. More elaborate worlds use power, cooling, networking, acoustic treatment, tracking, storage, and repair facilities. These occupy real space rather than disappearing from the brief.

Interfaces explain what is recorded and allow meaningful refusal. A shared room may need limited sensing to operate; people who decline it need an equivalent route and ordinary gathering options, not a coerced yes. Physical reconfiguration requires clear closure and reopening procedures. An invitation to an experience is not permission to alter participants' movement, record intimacy, or impersonate people they know.

## Governance, access, refusal, and exit

Basic public access includes unmediated rooms and circulation. Community groups can reserve experience spaces under published rules, with room for newcomers as well as established creators. Popularity does not confer ownership of the public core.

Participants learn an experience's demands before entering, can leave without narrative punishment, and can request assistance without performing a role. Moderation and complaints have appeal routes. Residents retain private domestic space outside the building's public stories. Personal accounts, acquired objects, and social history should be portable where feasible; loss of platform access must not mean loss of a home or civic identity.

## Dependencies and real tensions

Material security does not make attention unlimited. Equipment access, desirable booking times, acoustics, energy demand, cleaning, sensory needs, and social moderation remain practical constraints. Some experiences depend on external platforms or specialized components; preserving simpler, locally usable modes reduces fragility.

Creators want surprise; participants need consent. Collective immersion can deepen relationships or make refusal socially costly. A failure branch makes the House an attention business: every corridor promotes an experience, status governs access, and leaving feels like abandoning one's friends. Warning signs include hidden recording, neglected unmediated spaces, and rewards designed to keep people present beyond their intentions.

## Partial and broad automation

With partial automation, use fixed architecture, ordinary lighting, projections, sound, and optional overlays; skilled staff operate and maintain experiences. Do not assume automated moving rooms or unattended safety-critical operation. With broad automation, adaptable sets and personalized accessibility may become more capable, but the stable core and human right to stop remain essential. Emotional intensity is not a proxy for well-being.

## Material life: a room becomes another room

**September 7 addition:** AI design interpretation of [Ren's regenerative-material direction](../archive/004-ren-regenerative-materials.txt), developed in [Regenerative material civilization](../foundations/regenerative-material-civilization.md).

A festival ends. Its cloud harbor becomes a movement studio, while a beloved bench stays where friends expect it. Reusable panels and fittings travel to the component store; some return next season in another world. A workshop keeps public interface specifications and serviceable parts available so changing an experience need not require replacing its whole support system.

For a later, broader-autonomy branch, explore a building assembled from reusable steel elements with accessible connections. Robots would assist assembly and disassembly within a planned construction zone. This is a concept to investigate: structural capacity, connection wear, fire performance, inspection methods, temporary stability, and safe automated operation remain unresolved. Reusing an element in another role requires an assessment of its history and suitability; matching dimensions alone is insufficient.

The store becomes a kind of material library. Components arrive with records of origin, alterations, condition, and permitted uses, then pass through inspection, repair or preparation, and storage before allocation to a new design. Parts unsuitable for direct reuse need a separate recovery route. Standard interfaces support varied rooms; their governance should allow alternative suppliers and justified revisions.

Reconfiguration respects stable dwellings, familiar paths, and affected people's consent. Greater construction power can amplify a fashionable group's will into continual noise, displacement, and ecological damage. Decisions therefore consider total transformation demand, recovery capacity, and energy, not merely how quickly robots can build. Replacement should not become the default celebration of abundance. Keeping a place can be an equally deliberate expression of freedom.
