## Six moments inside the House

**1. You arrive beneath the world halls.** The approach is long enough for the building's scale to become bodily apparent. Three broad, stacked world halls rise over a logistics base. From the principal approach and the section, a dark passenger core marks the left edge and a dark service core the right; amber technical bands separate the occupied volumes. People disappear into different threshold halls. You came to meet a friend in a forest and spend the afternoon in a cloud harbor. At the arrival room you choose a shared experience and device setting; a tactile model explains the actual route. The anticipation is of entering a world that other people have been living in all day.

**2. The forest holds your weight.** Past the threshold, the distant structure becomes a canopy. You follow a real, gently curving deck. The rail under your hand has the thickness and texture of a branch; the perceived branch stays exactly where the rail is. A friend turns toward you beside a physical planted pool. The forest's apparent depth extends far beyond the hall, but your next footstep always lands on a real surface. A warm moving breeze and the sound of leaves make the place feel inhabited before anyone speaks.

**3. You cross the portal.** A broad, trunk-like opening receives you and your friend. It is a passenger lift embedded in the world's architecture. The doors close, separating the forest's sound from the cabin. A wash of light carries the canopy upward; its leaves seem to become small shapes in a luminous sky. You feel ordinary lift motion as the cabin travels to another actual level. Its sequence fits the journey's duration, without pretending that a destination can be reached before the machinery gets there.

**4. The cloud harbor is already continuing.** The doors open directly onto a broad curved promenade. The light, spatial horizon, and airy sound begun in the cabin continue through the threshold. Your hand meets a real balustrade above an apparent ocean of clouds. Far beyond it, impossible harbor structures hang in the sky. People are gathering along the same curved walkway; a couple sits on a bench beneath passing shadows. Your friend is still beside you, lit by the same moving daylight. The arrival feels like relocation because the destination surrounds you and continues beyond the doorway.

**5. The First Arrival begins.** It is the evening when an imagined vessel brings the first weather of a new season. Around a physical music table, a player taps a rhythm on a prepared surface. A friend answers with a melody, and someone joining for the first time adds a simple repeated note. The approaching vessel's apparent lights answer their shared rhythm. You can feel the table under your hand while the cloud bank changes beyond the rail. People have been rehearsing, trading phrases, and anticipating this evening for weeks; a familiar song now has a passage a newcomer helped make. Others watch from the curved amber bench or continue their conversation. This is a proposed recurring tradition with room to belong through listening, presence, or making.

**6. The place remains available in another mode.** When your eyes need rest, you and your friend enter a quieter adjoining room. You remove the device; a calm room at the quiet edge of the hall holds a bench and a view toward a planted lightwell. On the ordinary circulation route, you can see a section model of the halls and the lift you rode. After tea, you might return to the harbor or leave the House. Rest, service access, and unmediated navigation sustain prolonged world inhabitation rather than becoming a substitute purpose for the building.

## Architecture as a programmable spatial medium

The primary building elements are **large world halls** with substantial clear volume, terrain decks, robust overhead support zones, and interfaces for changeable surfaces. Some halls support long walks, some open gathering grounds, some vertical landscapes reached through real lifts and ramps. Halls connect through threshold chambers and passenger-lift groups whose destinations can be staged as passages between worlds. A megastructure can hold several experiences at once without requiring them to share the same acoustic or climatic character.

For this edition the common spatial reference is **three broad stacked halls above a logistics base**, with the **passenger core on the left, service core on the right, and amber technical strata between halls**, as seen from the principal approach and concept section. The middle hall contains the cloud harbor: the passenger landing leads to a broad curved promenade, a real rail, an amber bench, and the physical music table. Its quieter adjoining edge connects to ordinary circulation. This is one proposed study arrangement; exterior, paired views, and section should carry these anchors without introducing a different dense column forest or staggered tower scheme. A further model must resolve actual structure, spans, escape, sightlines, and route lengths. The section must show the space behind the appearance: occupied halls alternate or adjoin service strata for air distribution, power, data, acoustic separation, lighting equipment, inspection, and maintenance. Freight lifts and continuous handling routes connect fabrication shops, prop assembly, device repair, cleaning, inspection, and large component stores. Turning, loading, and staging space must be drawn at a plausible scale when equipment is selected; a thin line labeled backstage cannot carry large terrain modules.

Passenger routes, apparatus movement, and hall reconfiguration are separately planned. A scenery module changes position while its work zone is closed and checked. The ordinary scene assumes fixed occupied floors and touch surfaces. Moving environments or haptic mechanisms would require a separately bounded design, not a promise that unseen robots can safely rearrange occupied terrain.

The building's useful capacity is world inhabitation: places to walk together, spend time, make friends, rehearse other lives, and create environments. Entry, refuge, personal care, equipment support, and ordinary routes are enabling parts of that program. Megastructure scale is the user's direction; this arrangement is an architectural proposal to investigate.

## What perception replaces, and what the body encounters

Full replacement of a visible building envelope requires control over the light reaching the eye. This proposal therefore considers **opaque video-mediated mixed-reality displays or an optical system with effective active occlusion**, together with calibrated physical lighting. It does not claim that ordinary transparent AR glasses can make bright walls disappear or produce an opaque night sky. The lightweight form factor, field of view, contrast, comfort, latency, and resolution needed for long shared visits remain design and research questions.

The chosen display path must support trustworthy awareness of physical surfaces and other people. Hand, body, prop, and route registration must agree across participants. A shared world-state service coordinates where events occur and what companions can see or touch together; it is distinct from each device's private presentation. Creative variation is possible around common contact geometry. The following distinctions govern the concept:

| What physically happens | What may change in experience |
| --- | --- |
| The passenger lift moves between actual floors | The passage feels like crossing an ocean, opening a rift, or traveling through a forest threshold |
| Feet follow real decks, ramps, landings, and steps where appropriate | The deck appears as a canopy path, cloud harbor, reef, or another authored landscape |
| Hands meet fixed rails, furniture, and prepared tactile props | Their visual materials and narrative identities change while contact locations remain aligned |
| The hall supplies air movement, temperature, lighting, sound, and selected physical effects | Weather and atmosphere are composed around those effects within an established operating range |
| People share a bounded occupied volume | Horizons, sky, distant scenery, creatures, and apparent extent can exceed its real envelope |
| Closed halls receive new modules and checked configurations | The next season can inhabit a materially different terrain as well as a different appearance |

Apparent water does not permit unsupported walking; an imagined distance does not create physical room for more bodies. The concept does not rely on imperceptibly redirecting everyone through an infinite landscape. Real routes, booking, hall capacity, and travel remain part of the spatial composition.

Each world has its own acoustic treatment, controllable sound field, lighting, and environmental zone. Tracking infrastructure, calibration references, network coverage, local control, and compute/cooling access occupy real locations. A quiet harbor and a loud festival need genuine separation. Emergency lighting, escape routes, communication, and assisted evacuation require independent architectural and engineering work; perceptual continuity does not carry those responsibilities.

## The culture that makes worlds

The House is a shared means of creation enabled by the [production-to-abundance program](../foundations/automation-to-shared-abundance.md). Productive capacity supplies structural components, apparatus, maintenance, and reusable set materials. Public process knowledge lets another group adapt an experience rather than rebuilding its technical foundations. Common access lets inhabitants make something whose purpose is friendship, expression, inquiry, or wonder.

A world begins with people choosing what it should feel like to be there. They compose routes, gathering places, movement, sound, and social invitations together, prototype a part, and learn by inhabiting it. Designers share assets and physical interface records; practitioners preserve techniques; newcomers can propose another world. A successful harbor culture might give another group the tools and relationships to begin a sky archive. This is the [generative orientation](../foundations/generative-worldmaking.md): maintain conditions from which further experience and life can arise. It does not require every visitor to author or perform.

The [City of Shared Quests](06-city-of-shared-quests.md) remains a distinct branch: a persistent physical city inhabited through guilds, lore, and optional media. The House devotes its architecture itself to sustained AR world inhabitation. The earlier ocean/reef arrival remains a possible alternate realm; the cloud harbor is the main visual branch for this edition.

The First Arrival gives this world a continuing calendar. Some people come to play a phrase they have kept for years; others come because a particular friend always sits at the quiet end of the bench. A small making studio holds rehearsal sessions, sound tools, shared scene assets, and a physical mock-up of the table. Participants can propose a new arrival passage without becoming professional performers. The next season's weather is authored through trials in a smaller room before the hall changes. A visitor can enjoy the gathering without knowing its history, wearing a role, or being asked to perform.

The table's contact surfaces remain physical. The proposed instrument interface relates touch to sound and to a shared event state so that the vessel's lights can answer the music for everyone. Acoustic design, sensing, processing delay, environmental effects, and display registration must be tested together for this particular interaction. The scene does not require an actual vehicle to enter the building or a moving floor beneath the audience. Its image caption should identify the imagined distant vessel and the proposed occlusive display path where readers encounter them.

One season the harbor's ceremonial passage grows longer and louder. Friends at the quiet edge begin leaving before its end. The next rehearsal tests a shorter central procession with a quieter parallel listening area, and those who left are invited to say whether it helped. The tradition changes through experience while preserving the song people care about. This modest feedback concerns the environment that lets several kinds of participation coexist; it does not ask each resident to become more creative.

## The material and operational life of a hall

After a season, a closed hall's terrain modules travel along the freight route to inspection. Surface skins, connectors, electronics, and structural frames have different repair and recovery routes. Useful modules enter storage with configuration, load, condition, and compatibility records; rejected stock goes to identified material processes. The next world can begin from these holdings and public designs. Storage volume, repair queues, cleaning, energy, and loss determine how quickly the material can return to use.

Follow one curved walkway-edge module through the concept section. When its zone closes, a handling device moves it from the middle hall into staging beside the **right service core**. The freight lift takes it to the logistics base's receiving and inspection area. A worn surface is detached; a qualified frame waits in a labeled storage bay, while repairable surfaces and rejected material take their own routes. A later approved configuration moves the prepared module back through the same core, then checks its installation and alignment before reopening. The adjacent passenger core continues to serve occupied halls; visitors never need to cross the receiving floor to reach the harbor. These are proposed adjacencies and a route to test, not established handling clearances or structural performance.

Reusable steel assemblies are a proposed architectural direction. Connection wear, temporary stability, structural qualification, fire performance, and repeated disassembly need specialist investigation. The primary structure may last through many changes of scenery. Freedom to create different worlds need not mean rebuilding the entire shell each season.

Scarce hall time and configuration capacity are allocated through a public process with opportunities for newcomers. An admired world may be beloved enough that people want it to remain, while another group wants its hall. Smaller trials, alternating seasons, or an additional compatible hall are concrete possibilities to compare. Shared provision remains independent of participation; the House's meaning depends on people entering freely and continuing to have other lives.

The House draws its maintenance and creative means from the wider provision network, rather than requiring its residents to monetize visitor attention. An author can end a season, a performer can leave the ensemble, and a visitor can stop using mediated environments while retaining food, home, care, and unrelated tools. Calibration, cleaning, care, and repair still need an explicit operating account: measured automated assistance, willingly undertaken qualified roles, workable commitments, and available replacements. Hall availability can reduce during a staffing or equipment gap; an immersive attraction cannot claim priority over basic provision merely because people love it.

With partial automation, the same architectural purpose can begin with fewer halls, fixed terrain, staffed calibration, and slower fabrication. Broad automation could expand maintenance and reconfiguration capacity. Neither branch establishes seamless optics, structural feasibility, or safe autonomous operation by assertion. Specific prototypes and measured operating records must carry that evidence.
