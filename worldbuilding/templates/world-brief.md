## A lived scene

Write a short ordinary moment from inside this place. Include someone who is not performing, producing, studying, or leading. Show a relationship, a physical threshold, and a small friction. What can a person hear, touch, avoid, or choose?

## Embodied journey and visual sequence

Write a continuous arrival-to-inhabitation sequence through a small cast and identifiable route. Give the reader a body in the place: the first threshold, a surface touched, changing sound or air, another person's response, a reason to remain, and a departure or ordinary continuation. First-person or second-person scenes can help, provided their experiences remain proposed fiction. Do not make the whole case a short list of amenities or a description of a distant skyline.

Specify several connected frames, including a scale/arrival view, close contact, sustained activity, a change of condition, the enabling system, and ordinary continuation. Keep camera direction, physical anchors, people, and time coherent. Distinguish sequential events from events separated by long construction or travel. Link [the six-world journey guide](../worlds/embodied-journeys.md) when adapting an existing case.

State the primary architectural purpose faithfully. If the user asks for a building devoted to a medium or activity, make its volumes, routes, apparatus, and support spaces serve that purpose. Do not quietly replace a purpose-built AR megastructure with an ordinary mixed-use building decorated by overlays. Rest, accessibility, and everyday support should make the intended life workable at the chosen scale.

## Assumptions, values, and proposal

- **Speculative assumptions:** What technical, institutional, ecological, and economic conditions does the scenario assume? Distinguish automated capability from who controls it and who receives its benefits.
- **Desired values:** What should life here protect or enable? Do not turn material security into a reward for contribution, conformity, wellness, or good taste.
- **Proposed design:** What spatial and institutional arrangements might serve those values? Mark these as choices that can be challenged.
- **Evidence needed:** Which assumptions would need research, trials, or specialist validation before implementation? Avoid invented engineering precision.

## Purpose and people

Who lives here, visits, works when work is still necessary, rests, or leaves? Which forms of flourishing does it support? Which people might the attractive central story overlook? Explain how this place connects to other ways of living rather than defining a compulsory identity.

## The culture and the world form one another

Use the [generative-worldmaking foundation](../foundations/generative-worldmaking.md) to make the meta level concrete. What do inhabitants understand themselves to be doing together? What stories, rituals, institutions, and arrangements shape attention, expectations, and conduct? How can lived experience revise those arrangements and the understanding behind them? Show one constructive feedback sequence through specific people and practices, alongside a plausible way it could narrow. Let inhabitants notice what a rule or environment is doing, change the conditions of participation, and examine what happens next. Distinguish access to a scarce activity from unconditional essentials.

Explain who can help author this world, how a newcomer can begin something, and what one community's achievement makes available to others. The purpose may be exploration, expressive experience, study, play, care, or a life without a grand mission. Give the positive culture its own substance: humor, friendship, skill, longing, memory, and the things people look forward to.

Ask what can continue to grow in this environment, including forms its founders cannot predict. Generativity concerns these enabling conditions; it is not compulsory creativity, individual productivity, continual rebuilding, or maximization of possible experiences. Include rest, continuity, and people who do not share the featured ambition. Interdependence is a reason to examine consequences, not proof that everyone wants the same life.

## Spatial organization and adjacencies

Describe the arrival sequence, public and private areas, shared thresholds, noisy and quiet zones, and major adjacencies. Specify a continuous accessible route and meaningful unmediated use without accounts, glasses, or compulsory social participation. Show refuge, ordinary bypasses, service access, and connections beyond the site. Treat life safety and specialist accessibility requirements as design obligations, not solved by a sentence.

## Visible and invisible infrastructure

What must inhabitants understand or control? What is concealed for comfort, and how can it still be inspected and maintained? Identify water, energy, communications, movement, storage, repair, sensing, and waste systems relevant to this place. Aesthetic invisibility must not erase physical requirements or accountable operation.

## The supporting section

Pair an experienced scene with a section, plan, or service sequence that explains specific enabling technology. Show real volumes for apparatus, storage, maintenance access, staging, environmental control, and separate public/service movement where relevant. The scale of these spaces should follow a declared equipment and program hypothesis; a thin line labeled service must not conceal substantial machinery or handling needs. Mark architectural and engineering choices as proposals until qualified work establishes them.

For mediated worlds, state the display/occlusion approach and its unresolved performance rather than assuming ordinary transparent glasses can replace every visible surface. Distinguish what physically moves, what stays as shared contact geometry, and what changes only in appearance. Match camera and anchors between physical and experienced views. Explain how shared world state, tracking, sound, light, and environmental conditions support the particular experience. Preserve the building's intended scope while testing an attainable prototype of its critical passage or room.

## Material lives and transformation

For relevant physical systems, use the [regenerative-material foundation](../foundations/regenerative-material-civilization.md) to follow a useful object, component, or material through acquisition, use, care, return, inspection, reuse, and eventual reprocessing. Show actual destinations, energy and process inputs, contamination or quality limits, losses, and material held in use or waiting. Separate a theoretical recovery route from an operating one.

Identify public design/repair knowledge and compatible interfaces, with a way to revise standards. Compare shared provision with appropriate alternatives as a whole service. Preserve personal attachment, privacy, and meaningful access. State what comes apart, what requires assessment before reuse, and what remains difficult to recover. Keep ecological restoration distinct from material recirculation. For rapid transformation, show whose decisions are amplified, who can object, and what retains continuity.

## Governance, access, refusal, and exit

Who controls essential infrastructure? How is ordinary access guaranteed? How are scarce places, equipment, and times allocated? Describe disagreement, appeals, reversible trials, and minority protections. Follow one person who changes commitments and show how their home, food, care, and usable creative means remain available. State what a person can decline and still retain a usable life. Explain how they leave an experience, group, platform, or residence without losing unrelated essentials.

## Dependencies, tensions, and a failure branch

List material inputs, maintenance work, care, knowledge, outside systems, and ecological conditions. Include disruptions and a plausible fallback. Name at least two tensions that remain despite abundance. Describe how the same appealing design could become exclusionary, brittle, coercive, or environmentally damaging, with observable early warning signs. Do not resolve every conflict through benevolent AI or universal agreement.

## Partial and broad automation

- **Partial automation:** What works with substantial human labor and present kinds of physical infrastructure? Who performs and funds remaining work, under what voluntary or otherwise acceptable arrangements, and how can they stop without losing essentials? What happens when nobody takes a necessary task? What should be simplified or postponed?
- **Broad automation:** What changes if the scenario's stronger capabilities arrive? What remains limited by energy, ecology, land, attention, relationships, or collective choice?

Neither branch should imply that the technology alone produces fair access or good governance.

## Next spatial artifact

Define one bounded **blockout brief**, not an actual model: its test question, declared site hypothesis, main masses or rooms, circulation and accessibility traces, service spaces, required views, and contrasting use conditions. Include ordinary operation and one failure or maintenance condition. Where relevant, add an assembly/disassembly view or a return-to-reuse route, including storage and rejected components. Name unresolved technical decisions and the evidence needed next. Decide what the blockout must teach before adding detail or visual polish.

Choose views that show how the culture inhabits the architecture. A rocket community needs public learning and mission relationships as well as a vehicle; a persistent quest city needs coauthors and an ordinary morning as well as a festival; a portal needs an experienced departure and coherent arrival as well as its physical lift. For an AR study, match physical and experienced anchors across views. A single beautiful public-space image cannot stand in for every world or for its underlying principles.

For reader-facing artifacts, plan a complete readable narrative and useful descriptions of images alongside the visual sequence. A promised companion is not a verified reading route; check the exported files and their links.
