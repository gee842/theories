# Shengsheng

## The Way of Unceasing Generation

### Heaven-and-Earth, Human Becoming, and the Continuity of Civilization

> “Generation giving rise to further generation is what is meant by change.”  
> “The great potency of Heaven and Earth is life-giving.”  
> “Harmony truly brings forth things; mere sameness does not continue.”  
> “The myriad things grow together without harming one another; ways proceed together without contradicting one another.”

## Preface: Why Speak of *Shengsheng* Now?

We live in an age in which power is increasing faster than direction.

Human beings can alter landscapes, redesign living processes, connect the planet, produce almost limitless streams of signs, and build forms of intelligence that can act, judge, and create at unprecedented scale. Yet the growth of capability has not automatically produced wisdom. The more effectively we can fulfill a desire, the more urgently we must ask whether that desire deserves fulfillment. The more efficiently we can optimize a target, the more carefully we must ask whether the target is consuming the world that makes its attainment possible. The more completely we connect people and things, the more important it becomes to know whether the connection enables mutual flourishing or draws everyone into one escalating contest.

The deepest crisis of our time is not only a shortage of resources, a failure of institutions, or a loss of control over technology. It is a weakening of our shared understanding of what it means to live, to live together, and to leave conditions in which others may live after us. Growth is mistaken for life-giving. Expansion is mistaken for strength. Victory is mistaken for rightness. Information is mistaken for understanding. A multiplication of options is mistaken for freedom. Methods that work within one domain overrun their boundaries and become standards for all existence. We become increasingly able to do things while becoming less able to say why those things should be done.

This book returns to an old but unfinished question:

> What kind of becoming allows further becoming?  
> What kind of action enables one thing to flourish without closing the way of others?  
> How may a human life, situated between Heaven and Earth, among other people, and within history, become a passage through which the future remains open?

The answer developed here is named *shengsheng* (生生): generation that gives rise to further generation.

This is not an attempt to translate early Chinese thought into a ready-made modern Western system. It does not begin with a self-contained subject confronting an external object, nor with substances that first exist independently and later enter relations. Early Chinese thought more often begins with the transformations of Heaven and Earth, the alternation and mutual implication of differences, the formation of human beings within kinship and ritual, and the continuity of the present with those who came before and those who will come after.

A human being does not stand outside the world and then choose whether to relate to it. The body is formed by climate, food, land, and care. The mind is nourished by language, memory, custom, and response. Even our most private thoughts draw upon distinctions that no individual invented alone. We do not first possess a finished “self” and only afterward enter relationships. We become persons within the relations that make the word *I* possible.

For this reason, the present work does not take the static thing as primary. It begins with arising, transformation, formation, transmission, and renewal. It does not imagine Dao as a law imposed upon beings from outside. It looks for Dao in the courses by which things emerge, meet, constrain, and enable one another. It does not treat ethics as a command laid over desire. It understands ethical life as the cultivation of human capacities and shared forms through which a common world may remain truthful, corrigible, and life-giving.

The Chinese word *sheng* (生) means more than biological birth. It is to arise, to grow, to bring forth, to become capable, to open a path. *Shengsheng* is what happens when what has come to be becomes a condition for what may come next. *Buxi* (不息), “not ceasing,” does not mean that one form must remain forever. It means that the source of further formation is not exhausted, the way of transformation is not sealed, and those who come later retain the capacity to inherit, repair, and create anew.

Seen from this perspective, Dao, de, humaneness, rightness, ritual, wisdom, trust, harmony, government, technology, and history belong to one continuous inquiry:

> How can the transformations of Heaven and Earth avoid being blocked by a single will?  
> How can the human common world avoid being shattered by deception, fear, and limitless contest?  
> How can what is accomplished today remain a source of life tomorrow?

This is at once a contemporary reconstruction of Chinese philosophy and a civilizational proposal for the future. It does not claim to be an untouchable final truth. A philosophy faithful to *shengsheng* must be able to encounter evidence, receive criticism, correct itself, and take on forms that its present author cannot foresee.

Dao cannot be privately possessed, and no book can exhaust unceasing generation. The most this work can do is clear a path. It asks us to see that human beings are neither sovereign masters of a passive world nor meaningless particles condemned to struggle. We can become another kind of power: not a power that occupies every possibility, but one that protects the conditions of possibility; not a power that turns the world into means, but one through which the world becomes more capable of truth, freedom, relation, and further life.

# Part I — The Transformations of Heaven and Earth

## Chapter 1 — “Generation Giving Rise to Generation”: Beginning from Change, Not from Static Things

The *Xici* commentary to the *Yijing* says, “Generation giving rise to further generation is what is meant by change.” Change here is not an accident that happens to already completed things. There is no finished world to which transformation is subsequently added. Things become what they are through transformation, acquire form through mutual involvement, and continue through exchange, loss, renewal, and response.

A tree is not a fixed quantity of matter. Water, soil, sunlight, air, and season pass through it. Old leaves fall, new branches appear, and the tree enters the lives around it as shade, fruit, habitat, and future soil. A river is not one batch of water. A polity is not one generation of citizens. A ritual is not one isolated performance. Their continuity does not depend upon the unchanging identity of their materials. It depends upon a course of formation, transmission, and renewal.

It is therefore misleading to say that things first exist and only later acquire relations. Remove the conditions that generated a thing, the exchanges that sustain it, and the consequences through which it enters the future, and what remains is largely an abstraction produced by naming. Every actual formation is both a temporary outcome of what came before and a condition that shapes what may come after.

*Sheng* has at least three dimensions.

First, it is **arising**. What had not appeared becomes present because a time, place, encounter, or opening makes it possible. A seed germinates, a thought forms, a relationship begins, an institution is founded.

Second, it is **taking form**. Arising is not mere dispersion. To become something that can endure and participate in further events, a process needs a boundary, rhythm, pattern of exchange, and mode of continuity.

Third, it is **giving rise beyond oneself**. A child grows into someone capable of caring for others. A craft becomes teachable. An institution makes cooperation possible among strangers. A discovery leads to questions that could not previously have been asked. What has formed becomes a source of further formation. This is *shengsheng*.

For this reason, unceasing generation is not endless quantitative increase. A tumor grows. A falsehood replicates. An empire expands. Yet if the expansion destroys the larger conditions on which it depends, it is not life-giving. One flourishing has become the withering of many.

The measure of *shengsheng* is not sheer more, but the capacity to continue. It does not demand that every existing structure be preserved. Fallen leaves nourish new life; obsolete institutions may need to be transformed; false theories should be relinquished; individual lives end. Conversely, a structure may survive for centuries and still fail the test of unceasing generation if it can endure only by silencing truth, narrowing human lives, and preventing renewal.

The first basic claim of this book is therefore:

> **Whatever has taken form comes from what preceded it and enters what follows. It should be judged not only by whether it survives, grows, or wins, but by whether it leaves a way for further becoming.**

This joins existence, ethics, and history. Existence is not a neutral collection of items, because every formation changes what can happen next. Ethics is not an external set of prohibitions, because action already enriches or impoverishes the conditions of shared life. History is not a museum of what is gone, because the past remains active as language, institutions, habits, wounds, and capacities.

To think in terms of *shengsheng* is to shift attention from isolated outcomes to the way outcomes become new causes; from the success of one entity to its participation in a larger continuity; from immediate achievement to the kind of world that achievement makes more likely.

## Chapter 2 — Dao and *Ziran*: A Course of Becoming without an External Ruler

Dao is easily misunderstood as a hidden substance behind things or as a universal law standing above the world and commanding it. In early Chinese thought, Dao is closer to a way that is walked, a course by which affairs proceed, and the opening through which things meet, form, and transform.

Dao is not outside the myriad things. To place it outside would recreate a division between ruler and ruled, command and obedience. When the *Daodejing* says, “Dao gives birth to one; one gives birth to two; two gives birth to three; three gives birth to the myriad things,” it need not be read as a craftsman producing objects in sequence. It speaks of differentiation, resonance, and fecundity unfolding from what cannot be fully named. Dao brings forth without taking possession of what it brings forth.

This is why the text also says, “Human beings follow Earth; Earth follows Heaven; Heaven follows Dao; Dao follows *ziran*.”

*Ziran* (自然) does not mean “Nature” as a domain opposed to human society. Literally, it suggests “so of itself,” the way something becomes through what it is and through the conditions that allow it to unfold. For Dao to follow *ziran* is not for human beings to imitate every event in the wild, still less to treat predation and dominance as moral law. It means that Dao does not force all things into one externally chosen shape. It allows each to follow its tendencies, timing, dependencies, and limits within a larger field of mutual constraint and support.

*Wuwei* (无为) therefore does not mean doing nothing. It resists excessive and appropriative action: substituting one will for the intelligence dispersed through a situation, sacrificing long conditions to a short target, or eliminating spontaneous formation through total control. Genuine *wuwei* demands refined discernment. One must know which boundary to establish, which obstruction to remove, when to intervene, and when to step back.

A farmer cannot command grain to grow, but can prepare soil, carry water, remove pests, and act in season. A teacher cannot insert understanding into a student, but can create questions, examples, practices, and a safe environment for inquiry. Government cannot live each person’s life, but can restrain predatory power, protect truthful feedback, and make cooperation something other than unilateral sacrifice.

This is action that does not usurp the source of action. It is precisely because it does not replace the becoming of others that it can enable much to be done.

*Ziran* also means that every enduring formation has real conditions that cannot be ignored indefinitely. Human beings need rest, care, recognition, and meaning. Trust requires time. Ecologies need recovery. Learning requires repetition and quiet. Communities need memory. Creativity needs room. Power needs limits. Force can override these conditions temporarily, but the costs accumulate.

Dao-following is therefore not laissez-faire. To allow the powerful to occupy every resource is itself a coercive substitution of one tendency for the becoming of all. If rivers are severed, soils exhausted, language closed by fear, and desire continuously engineered by machines, calling the result “natural” merely excuses established force.

A durable formation must remain responsive to the conditions that make it possible. What only takes and never replenishes, only expands and never stops, only commands and never listens, or secures its own flourishing by closing every other way will eventually depart from Dao.

Dao is not a formula anyone can monopolize. It is the course disclosed when different beings can remain distinct without becoming mutually annihilating:

- it does not abolish difference, but enables difference to become mutually fruitful;
- it does not reject boundaries, but prevents boundaries from becoming absolute closure;
- it does not guarantee invulnerability, but leaves failure open to recognition and transformation;
- it does not prescribe one final form, but denies any form the right to identify itself with the whole of Heaven and Earth.

To attain Dao is not to own it. The moment one declares, “Dao belongs to me,” one risks replacing Dao with oneself. It is better to say that in a given moment a person has seen a wider pattern, loosened the obstruction of private fixation, and acted in greater accord with the transformations of the world.

Dao is often without name. Names can guide, but they cannot occupy the way. Any theory of Dao that refuses correction by the world or seals the possibilities of those who come later has already contradicted the Dao it claims to express.

## Chapter 3 — *Qi*, *Xiang*, Form, and Instrument: How Things Appear, Take Shape, and Transform

Chinese thought often speaks of *qi* to indicate circulation and continuity, of *xiang* to indicate manifestation, of form to indicate achieved differentiation, and of instruments or vessels to indicate concrete use.

These terms should not be reduced to matter, energy, information, or object. Together they direct attention to a world in which beings are not sealed substances. They participate in one another through circulation, resonance, gathering, dispersal, and form-taking.

### 1. *Qi*: Circulation through Which Things Participate

*Qi* (气) is not an indivisible material block, nor merely a mysterious life energy. It is a way of speaking about the flowing, gathering, dispersing, entering, leaving, rising, falling, warming, cooling, breathing, and resonating through which Heaven, Earth, and living beings are continuous without being identical.

Human beings breathe, eat what the land produces, inherit words from the dead, and are altered by the emotions of others. Body and heart-mind, person and environment, nature and human affairs are not two wholly separate realms. They enter and reshape one another at different scales.

This gives practical meaning to the unity of Heaven and humanity. It does not mean that the individual dissolves into a mystical cosmos or abandons judgment. It means that every human act takes place in a larger circulation. Pollution is not truly sent “away.” Fear does not remain inside one mind. Institutions do not float above bodies. What we produce returns through soil, health, habit, expectation, and future generations.

### 2. *Xiang*: Manifest Configuration in a Relation

*Xiang* (象) is not merely a visual image, and it is not an internal representation copied by a subject from an external object. Sun and moon have *xiang*; a ritual has *xiang*; a polity has *xiang*; a person’s speech and conduct have *xiang*. It is the form in which something manifests within a concrete relation and becomes perceptible, answerable, and transmissible.

The same person appears differently to a parent, a friend, a stranger, and a public institution. This does not imply the absence of reality. It shows that every being manifests from within relations. The “myriad images” are not a collection of detached appearances, but the differentiated ways in which the transformations of Heaven and Earth become present at particular places, times, and scales.

Human beings take *xiang* through names, narratives, diagrams, and numbers. This makes a complex world intelligible, but every such taking is selective. A map is not the terrain. An indicator is not a life. An identity is not a complete person. A model is not the whole situation it describes.

Error begins not with the use of names and models, but with forgetting that they are ways of taking *xiang*. A partial image then occupies the place of the living configuration.

The first virtue of knowing is therefore not certainty but humility. We must name, yet remember that names have edges. We must judge, yet allow judgment to encounter new manifestations. We must build institutions, yet prevent them from becoming blind to realities they themselves produce.

### 3. Form: Gathering, Differentiation, and Boundary

For anything to endure, it needs form. Form establishes boundary, rhythm, role, and a mode of continuity. Without boundaries, life cannot maintain itself. Without roles, cooperation cannot be coordinated. Without rules, promises cannot be understood.

But when form is mistaken for an eternal essence, it turns from a vessel of life into a shell that blocks life. The family form exists to sustain care; when it becomes nothing but hierarchical obedience, it harms those it should nurture. The state exists to coordinate and protect; when its survival is placed above the lives of all-under-Heaven, it secures one continuity by damaging a larger one. Academic form exists for inquiry; when it protects disciplinary authority at the expense of understanding, it closes the way it was meant to preserve.

Living form must both hold and transform. Without form, processes disperse. Form that cannot transform becomes rigid. A *shengsheng* form preserves what deserves continuity while adjusting its boundaries and practices in response to changing circumstances.

### 4. Instruments and Vessels: Necessary Bearers That Must Not Claim to Be Dao

An instrument or vessel is a concrete carrier of action. Bowls hold water; writing transmits thought; ritual preserves relation; algorithms arrange attention. Through instruments, ways of living enter ordinary practice.

Yet instruments develop inertia. Once formed, a tool reshapes its users. Once an indicator becomes a target, it changes the conduct it measures. Once a platform profits from attention, it begins to shape human attention into what sustains the platform.

No instrument is simply neutral. Every instrument makes some actions easier, hides some costs, and helps form a certain kind of person. When an instrument forgets the way it was meant to serve, means take precedence over life and life is reorganized to feed the means.

The answer is not to abolish instruments. Without instruments, Dao cannot enter practice; without institutions, goodwill cannot endure; without technology, large-scale cooperation is impossible. The continuing question is:

> What does this instrument bring forth?  
> Whose capacities does it enlarge, and whose voice does it reduce?  
> Can it be corrected, exited, or replaced?  
> Does it bear a larger way, or does it elevate its local measure into an absolute?

*Qi* names participation and circulation. *Xiang* names manifestation. Form names achieved differentiation. Instruments carry practices through time. These are not separate layers of substance, but different ways of attending to one continuous becoming.

## Chapter 4 — *Li* and *Zhong*: Grain, Proportion, and Situated Fittingness

The word *li* (理) evokes the grain in jade or wood. To work well is to follow the lines of the material rather than cut through it blindly. In the story of Cook Ding, the butcher moves “in accordance with the heavenly patterns” and “follows what is inherently so.” His hand, eye, attention, and the openings in the ox have become attuned.

The *li* developed in this book is not a metaphysical entity existing apart from things. It is the intelligible grain, connectedness, causal course, and fitting proportion by which a concrete formation holds and interacts. Water has its *li*. Grief has its *li*. Power has its *li*. Trust has its *li*. Different situations cannot be governed by one formula, yet some relations recur across situations: persistent deception damages shared judgment; power without feedback tends to mistake its own interest for the whole; growth that does not renew what it consumes eventually undermines itself.

To know *li* begins with seeing truthfully. If facts are hidden, the noblest language cannot produce goodness. It then requires scale. What works locally may fail at a larger scale; what helps in the short term may destroy in the long term; what enables one party may close the future of another. Finally, it requires proportion: when to advance and when to stop, which differences to accommodate and which harms to resist, which stability to preserve and which stability is only congealed injustice.

*Li* does not reduce complexity to a single metric. It makes fitting action possible.

This leads to *zhong* (中), often translated as “the mean” or “the center.” *Zhong* is not compromise for its own sake, nor a permanent midpoint between two extremes. In archery, hitting the center requires attunement among distance, strength, wind, timing, and aim. In government, *zhong* is a relation in which powers occupy appropriate roles and do not exceed their measure. In personal cultivation, it is not the suppression of emotion, but the refusal to let one emotion become the total ruler of perception.

The *Zhongyong* speaks of bringing centrality and harmony to fulfillment so that Heaven and Earth take their proper places and the myriad things are nourished. This should not be read as a private mind controlling the cosmos. It indicates that when forces are not mutually usurping, and differences enter a life-giving relation, further growth becomes possible.

*Zhong* is dynamic. When facts change, fittingness changes. When the weak have long been silenced, “remaining neutral” may simply conserve established force. In the face of uncontrolled violence, intervention can be a condition of harmony rather than its violation. Centrality therefore has a direction: it stands where a common world can remain truthful and viable.

*Li* without situation hardens into doctrine. Situation without shared standards dissolves into arbitrariness. Wisdom works between the two. It refuses both to sacrifice living beings to abstraction and to reject causal consequence in the name of feeling.

## Chapter 5 — Arising, Transformation, Cessation, and Continuation

If *shengsheng* is good, are death, destruction, and negation always bad? No.

The generativity of Heaven and Earth includes gathering and dispersal. If old leaves never fall, new growth is blocked. If cells refuse to die, the organism may be threatened. If false ideas cannot be rejected, knowledge cannot advance. If unjust institutions are preserved simply because they are stable, stability itself becomes closure.

Arising must therefore be thought together with transformation. To arise is to take form. To transform is for a formed thing not to cling absolutely to its present shape, but to enter new relations and become otherwise. Without arising, transformation has nothing to carry. Without transformation, formation becomes rigid and dies from its own closure.

The word *xi* (息) also has a double resonance. It can mean rest and breathing. Rest is part of life’s rhythm. A bow held constantly taut breaks; land without recovery is exhausted; a person without leisure loses attention and feeling. A civilization that recognizes production but not rest consumes its most important sources of future creation.

*Xi* can also mean to cease, to recede from active consequence. A tradition no one practices, a language no one speaks, a craft no one learns gradually leaves the world. Yet the cessation of one form need not be the extinction of its way. Names may be forgotten while trust, knowledge, or paths made by the unnamed continue in later acts.

“Not ceasing” therefore does not reject endings. It means that the life-giving pattern does not disappear entirely from consequence. It requires three capacities:

1. **Transmission** — those who come later can receive language, knowledge, institutions, and cultivated abilities from those who came before.
2. **Repair** — what has been damaged can recover, be compensated, or re-enter common life.
3. **Renewal** — inheritance does not demand repetition; a tradition can answer a new situation and the future is not sealed by the past.

A structure is not life-giving merely because it replicates itself. Genuine continuation allows its generative capacity to change form, divide, recombine, and open paths it did not previously contain.

This also supplies a measure for justified destruction. Does the destruction restore a deeper capacity for life? Does it merely release one party’s rage while leaving a larger field of fear? Does it preserve the possibility of learning, repair, and later coexistence?

Some negation is revenge; some is the interruption of harm. Some order preserves the memory of cooperation; some order freezes injury into normality. Good and evil cannot be inferred from the surface opposition between building and destroying. They must be understood through the longer course of what each action enables.

Part I may be gathered into one sentence:

> **The Dao of Heaven and Earth does not preserve every completed form. It enables what forms to transform, what transforms to be carried forward, and what ceases to leave the source of further life unexhausted.**

# Part II — The Human Being in the Midst of Things

## Chapter 6 — Born Between Heaven and Earth: A Situated Capacity to Act

Human beings are not observers inserted into an already completed cosmos. The body receives heat and cold, depends on water and soil, and survives through care. Language comes from those who spoke before us. Emotion develops in response to being held, answered, welcomed, rejected, praised, and blamed. Even the most private thought employs distinctions that arose within a shared world.

The “self” is therefore not an origin prior to all relation. It is a place at which many relations gather, become partially conscious, and acquire the capacity to respond. Parent and child, friend and stranger, institution and citizen, human being and land, present and past all enter the formation of the person. In turn, the person’s conduct changes what these relations may become.

To say that a person is relationally formed does not eliminate independence. It makes genuine autonomy possible. Someone who cannot see how advertising, rank, fear, and group approval have shaped desire will mistake being driven for being free. Someone who cannot see the common conditions behind personal achievement will imagine every benefit to be self-produced. Reflection begins when one can discern which inheritances should be carried, which impulses have been engineered, and which roles have begun to consume the person who inhabits them.

A human being is neither an isolated atom nor a silent component of a whole. Each person has an irreplaceable life, a field of feeling, and a capacity for judgment. Yet no person exists apart from others and from a common world. Human dignity lies partly in the ability to receive relations without being wholly absorbed by them, to examine inherited forms, and to answer them in new ways.

Classical Chinese thought often places humanity between Heaven and Earth. This is not a diagram of three objects stacked vertically. It names a threefold condition:

- above, the person answers to time, circumstance, and patterns no private will can own;
- below, the person depends upon body, land, tools, and the nourishment of other beings;
- around, the person dwells among kin, strangers, institutions, and all-under-Heaven.

Humanity exists in a *between*. This between is not emptiness. It is where encounter, resonance, distinction, responsibility, and transformation occur. Human beings can hear the limits communicated by things, or magnify one appetite until it damages the whole. They can receive history, or sever transmission. They can use names to clarify, or use narratives to conceal.

What distinguishes human beings is not dominion over the myriad things, but an unusually wide radius of consequence combined with the capacity to reflect upon consequence. We can ask: What is continuing through me? Which possibilities are being closed by what I accomplish? Can an inherited pattern of harm be prevented from reproducing itself?

Human responsibility follows from this middle position. It does not require absolute freedom. It arises because we are within causation and yet sometimes able to perceive, choose, and redirect it.

Freedom should be understood accordingly. It is not independence from every condition, nor the fulfillment of every desire. Without language, body, trust, and institutions, meaningful choice would not arise at all. Freedom is the ability to discern one’s position within inherited conditions, recognize the forces seeking continuation through one’s conduct, and allow an old cause to yield a different future.

> **Freedom is not escape from causation. It is the possibility that causation may change direction here.**

## Chapter 7 — Heart-Mind, Knowing, and *Liangzhi*: Moral Responsiveness and the Educability of Human Nature

The Chinese *xin* (心), the heart-mind, is not a purely spiritual entity opposed to the body. Nor is it merely a container of emotion. It feels, knows, attends, intends, deliberates, and directs. It is the living pivot through which a person answers Heaven, Earth, people, and affairs.

Heart-mind begins in responsiveness. Before a child can reason, it is soothed by care and distressed by abandonment. Human beings can be moved by suffering, grateful for help, ashamed by betrayal, and disturbed by unfairness. Mencius speaks of the beginnings of compassion, shame, deference, and the discernment of right and wrong. Ethical life is not manufactured entirely by external punishment. People possess incipient capacities to register whether a relation is nurturing or damaging.

This unlearned responsiveness may be called *liangzhi* (良知), often translated as innate or good knowing. It does not mean that every spontaneous intuition is correct. It means that human beings are not limited to calculating advantage. There are beginnings from which another person can appear as a life rather than a resource, injustice can appear shameful, and truth can appear worthy of protection.

A beginning, however, is not a completed virtue. Compassion may stop at the boundary of one’s group. Shame may be manipulated by authority. Loyalty may serve cruelty. A sense of right and wrong may be captured by collective delusion. *Liangzhi* is an initial disclosure of the life-giving way in human feeling, not complete possession of Dao.

Xunzi offers a necessary counterpoint. Desire, aversion, pursuit of benefit, and sensory appetite can lead to conflict when no distinctions, practices, or institutions regulate them. Ritual and rightness do not simply appear fully formed from natural impulse. They are cultivated through learning, accumulated practice, and social forms.

The tension between Mencius and Xunzi should not be reduced to a simple contest over whether human nature is good or evil. Taken together, they reveal something more adequate:

> Human beings possess beginnings of sympathy and shared life, but also defensive, acquisitive, and status-seeking tendencies. Human nature is not a sealed answer. It is continuously shaped by temperament, circumstance, habit, education, institutions, and reflection.

“Nature” and “nurture” are not wholly independent sources. What is called innate nature is partly the long history of environments sedimented into the body and the species. Education is the present environment continuing to write itself into conduct. Hunger, attachment, vigilance, and sensitivity to status have deep histories; family, school, market, media, and technology reorganize them further.

Education is therefore much larger than formal instruction. Whatever repeatedly determines what is visible, rewarded, punished, praised, or treated as success is educating people.

Families teach through response whether others can be trusted. Schools teach through evaluation what learning is for. Markets teach through prices what is counted as valuable. Organizations teach through promotion what conduct advances. Platforms teach through recommendation and reaction what kind of expression receives existence. War teaches through fear that strangers are threats.

At its deepest, education is the repeated entry of external forms into heart-mind until they become habit, desire, and trust. People do not begin with a fixed set of desires and then choose tools to satisfy them. Many desires are produced by environments. Provision for uncertainty can be enlarged into limitless accumulation. A need for recognition can be transformed into permanent visibility competition. Reason, originally a capacity to understand consequence, can be narrowed into the efficient pursuit of an unexamined target.

Civilization must therefore become conscious of what it teaches. If classical texts praise humaneness while institutions reward ruthlessness; if schools praise cooperation while ranking makes every peer an obstacle; if law praises honesty while powerful deception is profitable, the effective education comes from the environment rather than from declared principles.

Human educability is both hope and danger. Ritual, friendship, practice, and inquiry can expand moral responsiveness. Prolonged fear, humiliation, and contest can train a person into an efficient instrument of closure.

Self-cultivation cannot therefore be reduced to a private struggle within the individual, and government cannot be reduced to punishing evil after it appears. A deeper task is to create conditions in which moral beginnings need not be continually suppressed and goodness need not depend upon heroic sacrifice.

## Chapter 8 — *Ren*, *Yi*, Ritual, Wisdom, and Trust: Five Capacities of Shared Life

*Ren*, *yi*, ritual, wisdom, and trust should not be treated as five isolated moral traits. Together they name capacities through which human beings make a common world possible, durable, corrigible, and life-giving.

### 1. *Ren* (仁): Refusing to Reduce Others to Mere Means

*Ren* begins in nearness but need not end in partiality. Its root is responsiveness to life and feeling. I know that I wish to live and not be arbitrarily used; I can recognize that another is not an insensible object but a center of experience, relation, and future.

*Ren* does not mean indiscriminately liking everyone. It includes care and boundary, sympathy and the interruption of harm. Endless indulgence of an abuser may expose others to further injury. Love of one’s own, when it destroys public rightness, becomes private attachment rather than humaneness.

*Ren* asks first: Who bears this? Who has disappeared from view? Has a grand objective turned concrete people into expendable material? Without *ren*, intelligence becomes cold optimization, institutions forget whom they exist to serve, and all-under-Heaven is divided between those who command and those who absorb the cost.

### 2. *Yi* (义): Re-situating One’s Benefit within a Larger Whole

*Yi* is often rendered as righteousness or rightness. It does not reject benefit. It asks whether a benefit is fittingly obtained, whether its costs are displaced, and whether one party’s flourishing damages the conditions shared by all.

A person may seek wealth, recognition, security, and achievement. The question is whether these pursuits require others to lose basic life, voice, or future. *Yi* prevents “I can take this” from becoming “I am entitled to take this.” It also prevents formally equal rules from concealing profound inequalities of starting point, power, and cumulative feedback.

*Yi* requires responsibility to grow with power. The wider the consequences of a decision, the less sufficient it is to say that the decision-maker had no malicious intent. A private mistake may affect a few; the choices of political leaders, capital holders, and technological designers may reorganize the daily conditions of millions. Greater capacity does not imply greater human worth. It implies a heavier burden of rightness.

### 3. Ritual Form (*Li*, 礼): Giving Respect, Boundary, and Responsibility a Repeatable Shape

Humaneness and rightness cannot endure if they remain inward feelings. Ritual gives them visible, learnable, repeatable form: how to greet, listen, promise, mourn, object, use authority, transfer responsibility, and repair a relation after conflict.

Ritual is the interface of shared life and the bodily memory of civilization. It lowers the cost of coordination because people do not have to infer every intention from nothing.

Yet ritual easily hardens. If it demands obedience from those below while placing no obligation of restraint and care upon those above; if it protects status while abandoning living relation; if it preserves surface peace by blocking truthful speech, it ceases to carry Dao.

Ritual must therefore return continuously to *ren* and *yi* and be examined by wisdom. Its purpose is not to reproduce the past unchanged, but to give respect and responsibility viable form in changing circumstances.

### 4. Wisdom (*Zhi*, 智): Seeing Configurations, Discerning Their Grain, and Judging Direction

Wisdom is not identical with cleverness, memory, or computational speed. A person may calculate brilliantly while optimizing a destructive objective. An institution may possess immense data while remaining blind to the suffering it creates.

Wisdom first sees *xiang*: what is actually happening rather than how a structure describes itself. It then discerns *li*: incentives, boundaries, dependencies, and feedback. It judges direction: will this action enlarge the capacities of those involved or lock more of them into fear, dependency, and contest? Finally, wisdom knows change: it can revise a once useful method when circumstances have altered.

Wisdom therefore depends upon humaneness. Without *ren*, it treats people as variables. Without *yi*, it calculates for the strong. Without ritual, it lacks a durable public form. Without trust, the reality on which it depends is corroded by deception.

### 5. Trust and Trustworthiness (*Xin*, 信): Making Language and Promise Capable of Carrying a Future

*Xin* concerns both knowing and relation. Under incomplete information, a person must provisionally trust some account of reality in order to act. In shared life, people must also trust that most promises will be kept if cooperation is to extend beyond the immediate moment.

Trust is not blind submission. Mature trust has a basis in truth, can be tested, permits revision, and includes responsibility and repair after breach. Dogmatism treats refusal of examination as loyalty and group belonging as proof.

Keeping faith matters not only because honesty is admirable. A broken promise raises the defensive burden for everyone who comes after. More monitoring, collateral, coercion, and suspicion become necessary. When deception becomes normal, shared reality fragments and language ceases to be a means of common discernment.

*Ren* keeps the other human. *Yi* situates the local within the larger. Ritual makes relation practicable. Wisdom keeps conduct responsive to circumstance. Trust allows speech and promise to carry the future. Each corrects the others.

Humaneness without rightness becomes favoritism. Rightness without humaneness becomes harsh judgment. Ritual without wisdom becomes rigidity. Wisdom without trust becomes strategy. Trust without truth becomes factional loyalty.

*De* is not the sum of moral labels. It is the living integration of these capacities in conduct.

## Chapter 9 — Investigating Affairs, Making Intention Sincere, Rectifying the Heart-Mind, and Cultivating the Person

The *Great Learning* places the investigation of things, the extension of knowing, the sincerity of intention, the rectification of the heart-mind, and self-cultivation in a continuous sequence. If this is read as a detached subject collecting information about external objects, its human and practical depth is lost. To “investigate things” is to enter an affair, understand how it is working, and include the investigator’s own motives and position within the inquiry.

### 1. Investigating Affairs: Not Letting Names Replace What Happens

The “thing” investigated may be a policy, a relationship, an emotion, an organization, or a tool. Investigation begins by refusing to accept a name or self-description as sufficient.

If a platform says it “connects the world,” one must examine what emotions it rewards, how it arranges visibility, and how it profits from continued attention. If an institution claims to serve the public, one must ask whether affected people can speak, who carries the cost, and whether errors can move upward. If a person says an action is motivated by justice, one must examine whether resentment, vanity, fear, or the desire to win are also present.

Investigation includes at least five movements:

1. **See the affair** — distinguish what occurred from what is said to have occurred.
2. **Trace its formation** — examine resources, power, incentives, and history.
3. **Observe what follows** — see how consequences feed back into later conduct.
4. **Change position** — view the affair from the standpoint of those who bear it and those who come later.
5. **Test the account** — expose explanation to counterexample rather than using identity or desire as evidence.

### 2. Extending Knowing: Clarity Sufficient for Action and Open to Correction

To extend knowing is not to know everything. Reality never presents all information at once, and action cannot always wait for certainty. It is to make one’s understanding as undistorted by private attachment as possible, clear enough to bear responsibility, and open enough to be revised by what occurs.

This requires distinguishing knowledge, inference, and ignorance; actively seeking evidence that would inconvenience one’s view; attending to scale and time; recognizing that different positions disclose different aspects; and refusing to universalize one success.

Knowing that never enters action remains spectatorship. Action that refuses subsequent examination becomes fixation. Knowledge is completed and transformed in practice.

### 3. Making Intention Sincere: Refusing Noble Language as Self-Deception

People are especially vulnerable to the reasons they give themselves. The desire to win can be named justice. Control can be named responsibility. Avoidance can be named following the natural course. The desire for fame can be named service to history.

Sincerity does not require a single pure motive. It requires willingness to see mixed motives. Would I still act if no one knew? Would I accept the same rule if it were applied against me? Am I solving the problem or proving my own rightness? Have I turned distant suffering into an abstraction because those who bear it are absent from my life?

Sincerity prevents wisdom from being captured by the image the self wishes to have of itself.

### 4. Rectifying the Heart-Mind: Giving Emotion Its Proper Measure

Emotion is not the enemy of reason. Fear indicates danger. Anger discloses a violated boundary. Grief reveals that a relation matters. Joy signals an expansion of life. Without responsiveness, humaneness cannot arise and value cannot be felt.

The danger appears when one emotion occupies the whole field. Under total fear, everything becomes a threat. Under total anger, punishment replaces repair. Under success, the self may credit itself with all causes. Under unrelieved suffering, pain may be transmitted to the innocent.

To rectify the heart-mind is not to suppress emotion. It is to know its source, listen to what it discloses, and prevent it from becoming sovereign. A heart-mind capable of holding contrary feelings has room for longer judgment.

### 5. Cultivating the Person: Turning Occasional Goodness into Stable *De*

Knowing that honesty matters does not ensure honesty under temptation. Understanding the need for restraint does not ensure that power will stop itself as it grows. Self-cultivation turns insight, through repeated conduct, friendship, practice, and reflection, into a more stable capacity.

*De* is not a morally attractive image. It is the power to preserve certain relations under pressure: to stop when advantage is available, to verify when a crowd demands immediate judgment, to let truth travel upward when one holds authority, to accept responsibility rather than hide behind status, and to refuse to pass one’s wounds to the innocent.

Self-cultivation is not merely private. A person’s habits become part of another’s environment. The habits of leaders become organizational culture. The way parents handle conflict becomes a child’s first model of relation. This is why cultivation of the person can extend to family, polity, and all-under-Heaven: not because private virtue magically controls the world, but because no human structure exists apart from practiced persons.

### 6. Vigilance in Solitude: Protecting the Common World When No One Sees

To be watchful when alone does not mean fearing an invisible judge. It means understanding that unseen action still forms the actor, affects others, and enters the environment. An undiscovered deception makes the next deception easier. An uncelebrated act of care still restores a life.

Visibility is not the measure of good and evil. Once an act occurs, it has entered consequence.

## Chapter 10 — Knowing Where to Stop, *Wuwei*, and Free Wandering

Many disasters arise not from a total lack of capacity but from a capacity that no longer recognizes a boundary. Wealth can protect against uncertainty, then become power to shape rules. Political authority can coordinate common affairs, then make every feedback appear disloyal. Reason can clarify cause, then reduce living beings to variables in an optimization process.

Both Confucian and Daoist traditions therefore value knowing where to stop.

This does not reject excellence or flatten every difference. It recognizes that any local strength, once it claims to be the measure of all things, reverses the purpose that first justified it.

Knowing where to stop has three dimensions:

- **a limit to acquisition** — knowing when enough has been reached, so that desire does not consume the life it was meant to enrich;
- **a limit to knowledge** — recognizing the partiality of every model and refusing to replace a living situation with one representation;
- **a limit to power** — refusing to convert “I am able” directly into “I am entitled.”

*Wuwei* follows from this limit. A person who cannot relinquish the fantasy of total control cannot allow another being or process to develop from itself. *Wuwei* is not abandonment of responsibility. It is doing what is genuinely one’s part: removing obstruction, creating conditions, establishing protective boundaries, listening, and refraining from taking over another’s capacity to become.

The *Zhuangzi* adds the freedom of *xiaoyao*, free and easy wandering. This is not flight from the world or obedience to impulse. It is freedom from imprisonment within one finite measure. The great bird and the small cicada each depend upon conditions; no being is without dependence. Freedom is not pretending to be conditionless. It is knowing what one depends upon and refusing to mistake the view from one position for the whole of Heaven and Earth.

People are often occupied by name, profit, identity, success, failure, and the gaze of others. Roles can enable responsibility but also freeze a person. Achievement can bring forth value but also become the only proof that one exists. Even moral aspiration can be captured by the grand desire to become the hero of history.

Free wandering allows seriousness without possession. One can bear responsibility without claiming ownership of the result, seek completion without using victory as proof of self, and act for the future without demanding that the future remember one’s name.

To be “at ease in oneself” is not to follow every desire. If desires are continuously engineered by markets, platforms, and group comparison, obedience to desire may be the deepest form of servitude. Freedom is the capacity to see where a desire comes from and decide which desires deserve cultivation and which should be allowed to cease.

Confucian thought brings the person into relation and responsibility. Daoist thought prevents inherited relations and roles from becoming total prisons. They should not be mechanically combined, but their tension is fruitful. Without Confucian commitment, wandering can degenerate into withdrawal. Without Daoist loosening, commitment can harden into role worship and excessive control.

Genuine freedom is to have a place without sealing oneself into it, to act within history without demanding possession of history, and to remain capable of renewal within the dependencies of the world.

Part II may be gathered into one claim:

> **Human beings are formed by Heaven, Earth, relation, language, and history, yet can redirect what they inherit. Moral responsiveness provides beginnings; education and cultivation form them into *de*; knowing where to stop and free wandering prevent virtue from being reversed by power, fame, and fixation.**

# Part III — A World Made Together

## Chapter 11 — Co-Created Reality: Language, Promise, Institution, and the Human World

Human beings dwell not only in a world of mountains, waters, bodies, and seasons. We also dwell in a world repeatedly enacted, remembered, and recognized by many people together.

Money, law, schools, families, offices, disciplines, markets, states, and ritual practices cannot be established by one person’s private imagination. Nor do they exist entirely independently of human participation. They acquire real efficacy through repeated speech, expectation, enforcement, memory, and conduct. A contract may be marks on paper, yet it arranges future action. An identity may be a name, yet it can open or close a life. A historical narrative may be intangible, yet it shapes how groups understand one another.

We may call this domain **co-created reality**. “Co-created” does not mean that everyone freely consented. Many institutions are imposed by force, many prejudices are inherited, and many shared images are false. It means only that much of the human world must be continuously enacted by many participants if it is to remain effective.

Co-created reality has three important features.

First, it is **not unreal**. Law, credit, reputation, public opinion, and organizational structure alter bodies, resources, opportunities, and life itself. Humanly produced does not mean imaginary in the sense of inconsequential.

Second, it is **not fixed**. Because it depends upon continued enactment, it can be revised, forgotten, abolished, or reinterpreted. Longevity alone does not confer legitimacy.

Third, it **forms the people who sustain it**. People create institutions, and institutions shape people’s habits, expectations, desires, and sense of what is possible. We enter a world already organized by language, rank, procedure, and story. Only later can we consciously participate in altering it.

The common world is therefore neither an abstract whole floating above individuals nor the simple sum of private preferences. It is a fabric continually rewoven by conduct. Every kept promise adds credibility to future promises. Every abuse of office teaches that rules are conditional upon status. Every ritual of public humiliation teaches how dissenters may be treated. Every honest admission of error preserves a space in which correction remains possible.

The most basic resources of civilization are not only wealth and force. They include truthfulness, trust, predictability, and shared memory. These appear immaterial, yet they enable almost every large-scale human undertaking.

When facts can be rewritten by power, language loses a common reference. When promises are routinely exploited, everyone must increase defensive expenditure. When history becomes only a weapon for mobilizing hatred, the past no longer deepens understanding; it fuels the next conflict. People may remain on the same land while no longer inhabiting the same world.

Ethics is therefore not merely private character. The wrong of lying lies not only in one false statement, but in making speech less capable of carrying shared reality. The good of trustworthiness lies not only in the admirable agent, but in reducing the defensive burden for everyone who comes later.

The continuity of civilization depends upon whether co-created reality can repeatedly encounter truth, hold difference, correct error, and transmit the conditions of cooperation.

## Chapter 12 — Ethics, Consensus, and Coordination: Not Uniformity, but a Shared Way of Entering *Li*

Ethics is often imagined as a constraint imposed upon already independent individuals. Yet persons themselves depend upon language, care, education, and order. Ethical forms are not only burdens placed after freedom. They are among the conditions under which meaningful freedom becomes possible and durable.

Dao is the life-giving course of mutual formation. *De* is the embodied capacity of a person or institution to carry that course into conduct. The central ethical question is therefore not merely, “Have I obeyed a rule?” It is:

> What kind of person, relation, and common world is this action bringing forth?

A complete judgment must attend to at least three dimensions: what sort of agent repeated action is forming; whether others remain respected as lives with their own standing; and what consequences and feedback the action generates. Character, relation, and consequence are distinct but inseparable.

### 1. Consensus: A Shared Way of Seeking What Is So

Consensus does not require everyone to reach the same conclusion. When consensus is confused with intellectual uniformity, harmony becomes a pretext for suppressing difference and a community loses the capacity to learn from new realities.

What is necessary is a more basic consensus about the conditions of common inquiry and coexistence. People may differ profoundly while still acknowledging that:

- reality is not altered by the preferences of power;
- every person and group can be mistaken;
- evidence may require revision of a position;
- difference alone does not cancel another’s standing as a participant in the common world;
- conflict should have forms that do not make destruction the only conclusion;
- strength does not exempt anyone from all constraint;
- one generation may not consume the entire inheritance of those who follow.

These are not answers to every dispute. They preserve the road by which answers may still be sought together.

Without such meta-consensus, language ceases to function as a means of discerning *li*. It becomes a tool of mobilization, domination, and friend-enemy classification. Every faction may possess “its own facts,” but no shared practice remains for testing them. Trust becomes loyalty to the group, and intelligence becomes the ability to defend a predetermined position.

Consensus cannot be manufactured by force. Silence under threat is not agreement. Genuine consensus requires access to common evidence, fair conditions of expression, restraint of concentrated power, protection of minority standing, and procedures through which judgments can be revised.

### 2. Coordination: Turning Difference into Shared Capacity

Coordination does not mean making everyone perform the same act. Complex communities acquire capacities beyond those of any individual precisely because different people possess different knowledge, responsibilities, and skills while their actions can be made mutually responsive.

Good coordination allows relevant information to reach those who need it, makes roles and responsibilities intelligible, enables local actors to perceive wider consequences, and prevents the whole from extinguishing local initiative. It provides procedures for conflict, reveals hidden costs, and makes it possible to learn from failure without always seeking a scapegoat.

Bad coordination converts people into mute components. Commands move downward, but reality cannot move upward. Such an order may appear efficient in the short term, yet becomes fragile because it is deprived of truthful feedback. No center can possess all local knowledge. When local intelligence can only obey and cannot correct, an organization increasingly responds to its own image rather than to the world.

The highest coordination is not total control, but a condition in which differentiation and communication coexist. Persons retain judgment and responsibility, while ritual, trust, institutions, and shared purposes allow their distinct contributions to form a larger ability.

### 3. Ritual as the Everyday Form of Consensus and Coordination

Consensus that remains only a principle and coordination that depends only upon emergency mobilization cannot endure. Ritual gives them repeatable form: orders of speaking, procedures of deliberation, transfer of office, public apology, memorial practice, responsibility for care, and repair after conflict.

The value of ritual does not lie in the sacredness of form for its own sake. It lies in making respect, boundary, and obligation visible and expectable.

Ritual must therefore be capable of ritual transformation. When an inherited form persistently silences some participants, separates power from responsibility, or places status above life, it must be remade. To reform ritual is not necessarily to reject tradition. When reform recovers *ren* and *yi*, it allows ritual to carry Dao again.

## Chapter 13 — Harmony without Sameness: How Difference Can Generate Rather Than Destroy

An early saying declares, “Harmony truly brings forth things; sameness does not continue.” Harmony is fertile because different elements can temper, correct, and complete one another. Mere sameness produces no further differentiation from which new life may arise.

Harmony is not the absence of difference and not the suppression of conflict. Music depends upon distinct tones. Food depends upon the relation of different flavors. An ecology depends upon many species occupying different niches. If every voice is forced into one note, the result is not harmony but monotony. If one force occupies all resources, the result is not order but totalization.

The Analects says that the exemplary person harmonizes without becoming identical. This is more than tolerance. It expresses an understanding of difference as a source of learning, adaptability, and creation. The problem is not how to eliminate difference, but how to prevent difference from becoming mutual annihilation.

Harmony has at least four dimensions:

1. **Coexistence** — difference alone does not remove a person or group from basic life and voice.
2. **Mutual intelligibility** — each side can understand that its own standpoint is not the only measure of the world.
3. **Mutual limitation** — no party becomes so strong that it escapes every boundary; truth can still reach power.
4. **Mutual generation** — differences do more than endure one another; through division of labor, argument, complementarity, and creation, they produce a richer common world.

Harmony does not exclude justified confrontation. When oppression is continuous, asking the harmed to “preserve harmony” merely protects disharmony with the language of harmony. Sometimes a false peace must be broken so that hidden injury becomes visible and power can be rearranged.

Whether conflict is life-giving depends upon whether it still recognizes a possible common future, remains bounded, can be corrected by fact, and aims at restoring right relation rather than establishing permanent humiliation. Conflict organized around annihilation and irreversible domination reproduces fear as its inheritance.

The traditional moral reading of “martial power as stopping weapons” can serve as an ideal even if treated cautiously as philology. The highest use of strength is not addiction to victory, but the interruption of violence that continually destroys a common world. Goodness without protective strength may fail to defend life. Strength without a limit converts protection into rule by force.

### Harmony and Attunement

If harmony concerns relations among distinct participants, attunement concerns the proportion among different forces and values.

Freedom and responsibility, efficiency and redundancy, growth and renewal, competition and cooperation, stability and change are not simple alternatives. When one is absolutized, it consumes the other and eventually destroys the conditions of its own legitimacy.

Attunement is not arithmetic averaging. It is responsive regulation. A community may be more open in safety and more concentrated during emergency; an institution may need decisive coordination at its founding and distributed authority as it matures; competition may stimulate excellence under trusted rules and destroy the field once it captures the rules themselves.

Harmony is therefore dynamic stability. A living order can be disturbed and still recover, contain conflict without making conflict its only logic, grow while replenishing what growth consumes, and coordinate action without erasing diverse knowledge.

*Datong* (大同) should be understood in this light. It is not global homogeneity, a single center deciding everything, or a motionless paradise at the end of history. It is a condition that must be repeatedly restored: a world in which differences remain, yet basic obligations extend beyond narrow groups; interests conflict, yet the destruction of others is not treated as the normal means of life; power exists, yet cannot monopolize truth and the future.

The “great” in *datong* is not unlimited scale. It is the enlargement of rightness beyond private enclosure. The “commonality” is not identical form. It is the shared acknowledgment that the world cannot be justly occupied by one party alone.

## Chapter 14 — Justice, Order, and Government: Correcting Drift, Opening Blocked Feedback, Limiting Excessive Power

Every community contains differences. Ability, opportunity, work, luck, inheritance, and position will not yield identical results. Justice does not require flattening every distinction. It resists the conversion of distinction, through self-reinforcing feedback, into permanent rule by some and permanent exclusion of others.

Wealth purchases education, information, security, and access; advantage therefore generates further advantage. Poverty increases risk, shortens time horizons, and reduces room for experiment; disadvantage also reproduces itself. If only final outcomes are seen and all are declared personally deserved, cumulative structure is disguised as natural order.

The root of justice is the preservation of a common qualification to participate in further life. At minimum, this means:

- every person retains basic conditions of survival, learning, and social re-entry;
- rules do not change arbitrarily with wealth, identity, or office;
- capital and authority cannot purchase unlimited exemption;
- affected people have access to decision and appeal;
- external costs cannot be indefinitely transferred to those without voice;
- one failure does not become lifelong expulsion;
- future generations do not inherit only debt, damage, and locked decisions.

Justice is not resentment of strength. It prevents strength from becoming the sole measure of right. The greater the power to shape common conditions, the greater the obligation to accept openness, feedback, and self-limitation.

### 1. Order as the Memory of Cooperation

Order is not static obedience. It is the preserved form of past successful cooperation. It enables strangers to form expectations and allows long projects to proceed without beginning from zero each time.

Traffic rules, judicial procedures, scholarly norms, and promise-making institutions are memories of coordination. Without order, everyone must remain defensive and enormous human capacity is spent on suspicion and self-protection.

But order that refuses transformation becomes a shell. Good order must be stable enough to trust and open enough to correct. Stability without openness suppresses new life. Openness without stability dissolves common expectation.

### 2. “Government Means Rectification”

The saying “to govern is to rectify” does not mean that government may declare itself infallibly correct. It means that political legitimacy lies in the capacity to discover and correct drift.

Power accumulates. Institutions protect themselves. Narratives conceal cost. Government should reopen channels through which suppressed facts enter, excessive force is bounded, excluded people regain standing, and short-term gain must answer to long consequence.

Regulation is not arbitrary control of life. It is the placement of boundaries around forces that can consume the common conditions of life. The first objects of serious regulation should not always be the everyday conduct of the weak. They should include unaccountable authority, limitless accumulation, platforms that monopolize rules and information, and organizations capable of shifting their costs onto society.

Life-giving government can be summarized by five principles:

1. **Boundary** — no power, capital, or technology may unilaterally cancel all constraint.
2. **Hearing** — the experience of affected people can enter decision.
3. **Revisability** — consequential arrangements retain review, correction, and where possible exit.
4. **Plurality** — more than one path remains possible; no single structure occupies all life.
5. **Posterity** — those not yet born count as inheritors of the common world.

Regulators must themselves be regulated. Oversight accumulates power; the language of justice can also suppress. Distributed authority, public reasons, periodic review, independent feedback, and appeal are therefore necessary.

The aim of good government is not to find a person who will never be wrong. It is to create conditions in which no person’s error can expand without limit.

## Chapter 15 — Education and Environment: What Institutions Teach Every Day

Individuals remain responsible for conduct, but conduct never arises in a vacuum. Security, dignity, abundance, and predictability give people room to consider the long term, comprehend complexity, and care for others. Persistent fear, humiliation, scarcity, and struggle shorten time horizons, narrow sympathy, and intensify possession and enemy-thinking.

This does not mean that adversity mechanically produces evil or wealth automatically produces virtue. People preserve extraordinary goodness in hardship, and comfort can nourish cruelty. But civilization cannot be designed on the assumption that everyone must become a saint under unbearable pressure. A good order makes goodness possible without requiring constant heroism.

### 1. Security Lengthens Time

When illness, unemployment, or arbitrary power can erase an entire life, immediate survival consumes attention. When basic standing is protected, people are more able to learn, cooperate, keep promises, and consider the future. Security does not remove risk. It prevents risk from occupying the whole heart-mind.

### 2. Sufficiency Restores a Boundary to Desire

Sufficiency is not refusal of growth. It is the capacity to know when something is enough. Bodily needs often have points of satisfaction. Desires organized by rank, comparison, capital, and visibility can become endless. A society powered by permanent dissatisfaction must continually manufacture new gaps, hierarchies, and anxieties.

Knowing sufficiency must not be used to justify deprivation. It requires both that everyone have enough conditions to form a life and that the limitless accumulation of a few not be mistaken for natural need.

### 3. Abundance Means Richness of Common Capacity

Abundance is not merely a large quantity of commodities. It includes time, knowledge, health, public space, relationship, and opportunity. Luxury for a few beside insecurity for many is not abundance. It is the conversion of common resources into private display.

When common conditions are rich, cooperation need not require sacrifice by the weak and failure need not mean permanent exclusion.

### 4. Institutions Teach through Reward and Punishment

What a society truly believes is written not only in constitutions and classics, but in what it rewards, punishes, makes visible, and renders silent each day.

If a company rewards short-term targets and punishes those who report long risks, it teaches concealment. If a school rewards ranking above learning, it teaches students to treat peers as obstacles. If a platform rewards outrage, it teaches people to trade conflict for visibility. If the powerful are routinely excused, it teaches that rules are constraints for the weak.

Education cannot mean asking individuals to live against the entire environment. The environment itself must cease teaching evil.

### 5. Good Generates Good; Evil Generates Evil

Trustworthiness lowers defensive cost. Lower defense enables cooperation. Cooperation creates shared benefit. Shared benefit confirms trust. Fairness increases legitimacy; legitimacy reduces the need for coercion; resources are released for care and creation. Good action changes not only an immediate outcome but the conditions of later action.

Evil also reproduces itself. Deception increases suspicion; suspicion increases control; control suppresses truthful feedback; error becomes harder to detect. Predation causes hoarding and retaliation, which then seem to confirm that everyone must protect only themselves.

Good and evil therefore have environmental feedback. If a structure continually rewards harmful conduct, even people without cruel intentions may find harm to be the rational choice. If benefits of cooperation are shared and costs of betrayal cannot be displaced, goodness becomes more stable.

Part III may be gathered into one claim:

> **The human common world is repeatedly made through trust, ritual, institutions, and conduct. Consensus preserves a shared way of seeking what is so; coordination turns difference into capacity; harmony prevents difference from becoming mutual destruction; justice and government correct cumulative drift; education determines what kind of person civilization forms each day.**

# Part IV — Life-Giving and Life-Closing

## Chapter 16 — Distinguishing Good and Evil: Enabling Life, Closing Life

If good and evil were only private preferences, the argument of this book would have no ground. Yet good and evil cannot be identified simply with survival and destruction. Parasitic structures may endure. Tyrannies may be stable. Falsehoods may reproduce with great success. Conversely, ending an injury, removing a diseased structure, or abandoning an error may reopen a larger field of life.

The ground of ethical distinction lies not in an arbitrary command and not in the surface appearance of an act, but in the way conduct and institutions alter the conditions of shared becoming.

> **Good enables life: it allows lives, relations, truth, and future capacities to arise, recover, unfold, and mutually support one another.**  
> **Evil closes life: it allows one appetite, one moment, or one power to consume, narrow, and seal the conditions on which many forms of becoming depend.**

To enable life is not to design one correct life for everyone. A goodness that forces all persons into a single form cancels the plurality inherent in generation. Goodness does not preserve every desire. It protects and develops the capacities through which people can understand, choose, take responsibility, relate, and revise.

To close life does not only mean killing. Deception can close shared knowing. Humiliation can close speech and confidence. Monopoly can close entry for those who come later. Excessive control can close spontaneous creation. Ecological exhaustion can close the way of people not yet born. Much evil does not destroy at once. It gradually leaves fewer, narrower, and more dependent futures.

Ethical judgment must move across at least four dimensions:

1. **Near and far** — what long feedback grows from an immediate benefit?
2. **Part and whole** — does local flourishing damage a wider continuity?
3. **Visible and hidden** — who carries the cost behind the visible gain?
4. **Reversible and irreversible** — does the decision preserve correction, repair, and alternatives?

Goodness is not always gentle. Restraint may protect life. Confrontation may restore truth. The dismantling of a rigid structure may make renewal possible. The question is not whether force is used, but whether force has a limit, protects standing rather than annihilating it, remains answerable to reality, and withdraws when its protective purpose has been achieved.

Evil also does not always require malicious intention. A person may sincerely serve an institution whose objective is too narrow. An organization may contain no one who wishes to destroy an ecosystem, yet its incentive structure can make destruction the combined outcome of rational local choices. Evil can be carried by feedback and institutional form as well as by intention.

Ethics therefore requires both self-cultivation and environmental design. It attends to motive and consequence, preserves boundaries against treating persons as instruments, and remembers that every person is situated in wider relations.

Before declaring an action or institution life-giving, one should ask:

- Does it depend on continual concealment or deception?
- Does it replenish what it consumes?
- Does it enlarge the ability of people to understand, choose, and assume responsibility?
- Can those affected communicate consequence?
- Does it turn difference into learning or into permanent exclusion?
- Can error be acknowledged and repaired?
- Does it transfer irreversible cost to those without a voice, including future generations?

These questions do not mechanically yield an answer. They prevent judgment from remaining confined to one standpoint, one time horizon, or one metric.

## Chapter 17 — Fear, Scarcity, Struggle, and Accumulation: A Degenerative Cycle

Life-closing orders rarely begin with a declaration that everything should be destroyed. They more often begin with fear and contraction.

When security, dignity, or future possibility feels threatened, the heart-mind shortens its horizon and narrows the field of concern. Immediate survival and the near group take priority. This is an intelligible response to danger, not itself a moral crime. But when danger becomes chronic, defensive conduct reshapes personality and institution. Temporary strategies become permanent forms.

The cycle often proceeds as follows:

```text
possibility contracts
→ insecurity and felt scarcity increase
→ time horizons shorten and boundaries harden
→ trust declines and cooperation becomes risky
→ contest, hoarding, and control intensify
→ resources and authority concentrate
→ more people lose security and participation
→ possibility contracts further
```

The cycle produces the world it predicts. If others are assumed to be inevitable competitors, one expands preemptively. Others experience the expansion as threat and answer in kind. Eventually struggle becomes the only apparently rational response.

Struggle also selects for those most adapted to struggle. A prolonged contest rewards speed of attack, suspicion, reduced sympathy, strict internal discipline, simplified narratives, and concentrated resources. People and organizations that preserve complexity, restraint, and openness may lose in the short term. Over time, institutions are shaped by the kinds of conduct that survive within the contest.

Accumulation is ambiguous. Provision can protect against uncertainty and enable long projects. But when wealth, authority, or attention simultaneously provide safety, status, and the ability to accumulate more, accumulation becomes self-reinforcing. Those who possess gain better information, bargaining power, resilience, and influence over rules. Those who lack fall farther from entry.

At this point there is no natural endpoint. Every gain raises the threshold of the next contest. Security comes to depend upon greater control. The wealthiest fear losing position; the strongest states fear relative decline; the largest platforms must still grow. The system expands, yet no participant arrives at enough.

Scarcity can also be manufactured. Monopoly creates scarcity of opportunity. Ranking creates scarcity of status. Advertising creates scarcity of satisfaction. Platforms create scarcity of attention. Enemy narratives create scarcity of belonging. Manufactured scarcity does not answer a natural shortage. It keeps people consuming, obeying, competing, and accumulating.

The most dangerous feature of a degenerative cycle is its local rationality. Every participant can give a compelling reason for continuation. A firm that refuses cost-cutting may be displaced. A state that refuses military expansion may feel exposed. A person who withdraws from competition may lose a livelihood. Rational actions from each local position combine into an irrational whole.

This is why moral exhortation alone is inadequate. If those who exit carry all the cost, cooperation is predictably betrayed, and honesty is punished, the demand for virtue becomes another burden placed upon the less powerful. Rules and environments must change so that predation is no longer the only stable choice.

## Chapter 18 — Totalizing Obstruction: When a Local *Li* Claims to Be Dao

Different affairs have different grains. Trouble begins when a pattern that works within one domain crosses its boundaries and claims authority over all life.

Competition can stimulate effort in some contexts and is then declared the natural law of every relation. Prices can coordinate some resources and are then declared the measure of every value. Security requires some control and becomes a justification for total surveillance. Communication requires attention and attention becomes proof of truth, worth, and existence.

This may be called **totalizing obstruction**. It is obstruction because one part blocks the appearance of the whole. It is powerful because it usually contains a partial truth.

Totalization often unfolds through four movements:

1. A method succeeds in a limited context.
2. Success gives the method resources, prestige, and explanatory authority.
3. The method reorganizes other domains so that their values can be expressed only in its terms.
4. The reorganized world appears to prove that the method is indispensable.

When education is increasingly evaluated by income return, students choose what is easiest to monetize. Humanities and public knowledge decline. As society loses the language to describe non-monetary educational value, income appears even more “realistic” as the sole measure. The metric has changed the world that now seems to justify the metric.

A totalizing order need not be centrally planned. It can emerge from many distributed choices. Once established, even those who see its damage may find individual exit prohibitively costly because livelihoods and institutions have been rebuilt around it.

This is structural evil: participants continue feeding a process whose destination they may not endorse.

Breaking totalization begins by restoring distinctions. Economy is not the whole of life. State security is not the whole of politics. Efficiency is not the whole of goodness. Information volume is not knowing. Survival alone is not legitimacy. It then requires multiple measures, so that one value cannot consume all others. It also requires spaces of exit, replacement, and experiment so that a single structure cannot lock the future.

Yet opposition to totalization can itself become totalizing. To resist market domination by abolishing every voluntary exchange, to resist authority by denying every form of coordination, or to resist technological power by rejecting every instrument would again elevate one reaction into Dao.

The way of *shengsheng* does not search for one mechanism that will be correct forever. It requires mechanisms to remain within their appropriate domains, answer to one another, receive feedback, and refrain from consuming the source of shared life.

## Chapter 19 — Market, Capital, and Technology: When Means Rise above the Life They Serve

Markets, capital, and technology possess genuine generative power. A serious philosophy cannot criticize them by denying what they have enabled.

Markets allow distributed exchange and communicate some forms of scarcity through price. Capital converts accumulated resources into long projects and risk-bearing. Technology preserves skill, enlarges scale, reduces some forms of toil, and makes cooperation possible across distance and complexity. Much of modern abundance and freedom has emerged through these capacities.

The problem is not their existence. The problem appears when an instrument is elevated into Dao, when a means that was justified by life becomes a purpose to which life must be reorganized.

### 1. The Grain and Limits of Markets

Markets are effective when goods are comparable, exchangeable, and supported by reasonably clear rights. They do not by themselves determine what should be for sale. Nor do they independently maintain the trust, law, ecology, knowledge, and public order on which exchange depends.

Care, education, medicine, justice, nature, and democratic voice are transformed when allocated entirely by purchasing power. If teaching serves only those who yield the highest return, education ceases to be a transmission of common capacity. If medical resources follow profit alone, life is ranked by ability to pay. If public fact follows attention price, truth loses to stimulation.

Markets are instruments, not the Dao of all-under-Heaven. Keeping them within their domain does not abolish exchange. It protects those common conditions that lose their meaning when fully commodified.

### 2. The Generativity and Reversal of Capital

Capital begins as accumulation withheld from immediate consumption so that future production becomes possible. It can coordinate resources, sustain risk, and support construction beyond the capacity of one person or moment.

But when return on capital becomes the supreme objective of an organization, the relation reverses. Life requires capital; production then serves capital; finally time, attention, land, relationship, and human capability are reorganized as inputs into capital’s further increase.

Values that are difficult to price—care, trust, leisure, cultural memory, ecological recovery—are treated as valueless or as free conditions available for consumption.

Capital also carries cumulative advantage. More capital purchases better information, bargaining power, resilience, access, and influence over rules, which generate still more capital. Without boundaries, competition tends toward concentration and a small number of participants acquire the ability to define the terms under which later participants compete.

A life-giving economy must return capital to the role of instrument. It must protect basic survival and participation, prevent monopoly and purchase of rules, make ecological and social cost visible in decision, preserve public goods, give workers meaningful negotiating and governing power, distribute technological gains as common capacity, and allow failure without permanent social death.

### 3. Technology Is Not an External Neutrality

Technology does not merely execute prior intention. It reorganizes intention, habit, space, and time. Clocks transform the experience of time. Cars reshape cities. Smartphones reshape attention. Recommendation systems reshape visibility.

A technology should therefore be evaluated not only by what it can accomplish, but by what conduct it makes easier, what dependencies it creates, who gains capacity, who becomes legible to whom, and what kind of person and institution it trains.

Technological goodness is not identical with maximum power. It requires power proportionate to responsibility, intelligibility, corrigibility, and shared benefit. A technology that can reorganize society more quickly than society can understand or govern it creates a widening distance between capability and wisdom.

Knowing where to stop does not mean stopping invention. It means preventing deployment from outrunning understanding and public capacity. It means requiring stronger justification for irreversible risk and giving affected communities a role in deciding how technologies enter life.

## Chapter 20 — Networks and Artificial Intelligence: A New Field of Manifestation, Education, and Public Infrastructure

The internet has brought humanity close to a real-time image of all-under-Heaven. Knowledge crosses borders. Isolated people find companions. Small groups create together. Experiences concealed by power become visible. The network possesses immense *shengsheng* potential.

But a network is not simply a channel. Platforms determine what appears, in what order, and with what feedback. They shape attention, desire, identity, and the sense of reality. They are vast educational environments whose lessons are often implicit.

When commercial platforms optimize for time, interaction, and growth, anger, fear, humiliation, and factional conflict often spread more effectively than careful truth. The system need not intend social division. It systematically rewards manifestations that improve its metric. Instrumental rationality produces ethical consequence: the target says “engagement,” and the surrounding world is reshaped into what most reliably produces engagement.

Networked presentation also compresses other people into avatars, labels, and fragments. Context disappears, making *ren* more difficult. Public positioning replaces the wider judgment of *yi*. Immediate reaction becomes a new ritual. Wisdom is overwhelmed by volume and speed. Trust moves from “this can withstand examination” to “many people like us believe it.”

The harm of an information bubble is not only that different people see different content. The deeper harm is the fragmentation of a shared field of manifestation. People do not know that what they see has been predicted and arranged, and experience it as direct reality. Groups then inhabit different worlds and lose a common entrance into *li*.

A life-giving network should treat users as participants in a common world rather than as predictable deposits of attention. It should:

- disclose why content is being presented;
- allow people to choose, replace, and examine recommendation structures;
- preserve source, context, and correction history;
- introduce proportionate friction into high-spread, high-harm behavior;
- reward durable contribution rather than only immediate stimulation;
- involve communities in rule-making;
- protect children and vulnerable people from highly optimized behavioral shaping;
- permit meaningful exit, portability, and migration;
- preserve noncommercial spaces for knowledge, art, friendship, and public life.

### Artificial Intelligence: Whose Intelligence, and toward What Becoming?

Artificial intelligence extends generation, judgment, and action into a new scale. It can accelerate scientific discovery, reduce necessary toil, improve education and medicine, coordinate public infrastructure, and help people understand complexity. It can also concentrate authority, replace human judgment without accountability, intensify manipulation, accelerate competitive escalation, and reorganize society at a speed no participant fully understands.

The central question is not only whether a machine resembles a human being. It is how artificial intelligence is situated, what objectives it serves, and what kind of civilization it helps form.

If AI is primarily deployed as a means by which firms and states gain power over one another, it will accelerate inherited struggle: competition for attention, labor, finance, military advantage, surveillance, and narrative control. Each participant will deploy faster out of fear of falling behind. The whole will become more dangerous even if no participant desires catastrophe. A degenerative cycle will have acquired a far more powerful instrument.

Another path is possible if AI is treated primarily as common infrastructure. Foundational knowledge, medical assistance, education, ecological monitoring, disaster response, public administration, and scientific capacity can be developed as widely accessible goods. Automation can reduce compulsory labor without making loss of wage equivalent to loss of life. Models can be auditable, consequential decisions traceable, and major deployments publicly accountable. Technical gain can become common abundance and common time.

The issue is not whether AI should be present in society, but what role it should occupy. An AI order designed to compete with human beings for every resource, task, and locus of power may break the social cycle through which human livelihood and participation have been organized. An AI order designed to enlarge everyone’s capabilities while remaining under public boundary may become an instrument of *shengsheng*.

AI also forces a reconsideration of wisdom. Faster calculation and more accurate prediction are not yet *zhi*. Wisdom includes reflection upon the objective, humaneness toward those affected, rightness in the distribution of cost, fittingness to circumstance, and humility before the unknown. If a civilization cannot distinguish its own good, value alignment cannot be solved as a technical afterthought. Machines will extend the objectives that institutions already reward.

The most important alignment in the age of AI is therefore the alignment of the human common world with itself. Can human beings establish rules under which mutual destruction is not the individually rational choice? Can truth, dignity, and the future take precedence over short competitive gain? Can powerful instruments serve the life of all-under-Heaven rather than the limitless empowerment of a few actors?

Part IV may be gathered into one claim:

> **Evil often begins in fear and local rationality, then expands through contest, accumulation, and self-confirming structures. Markets, capital, networks, and artificial intelligence can enable life or close it. Their direction depends upon whether they remain instruments, recognize limits, receive feedback, and convert power into common conditions of further becoming.**

# Part V — Continuity across Past and Future

## Chapter 21 — History Is Not Merely Past: How Consequence Becomes the Condition of What Follows

Human beings stand between past and future as well as between Heaven and Earth.

The languages, roads, institutions, sciences, rituals, wounds, and skills through which we live were left by people who are no longer present. The past does not disappear simply because time passes. It continues as the conditions of the present. An old institution may still distribute opportunity. An injury never acknowledged may continue as collective fear. A craft preserved across centuries may open a path no earlier practitioner could imagine.

History is therefore not a storehouse of dead facts. It is an active field of consequence.

```text
action in the past
→ becomes present tendency, instrument, habit, and trust
→ present people judge and act within these conditions
→ new consequences are formed
→ they become the conditions of those who follow
```

No one chooses every starting point. Birthplace, language, inherited institutions, and collective memory precede individual will. Yet a person is not merely the passive result of history. Once an inherited pattern is seen, it may be weakened, repaired, redirected, or refused.

The most concrete historical meaning of freedom is that inherited evil need not pass through us unchanged.

This requires avoiding both the worship and the contempt of the past.

To worship the past as a pure golden age conceals domination in the name of ancestors. To dismiss the past as nothing but ignorance discards experience purchased through immense suffering. A third error is to weaponize the past, selecting fragments that flatter one’s present group and turning history into an engine of superiority and hatred.

A more fitting relation to the past combines **reverent understanding, truthful examination, and responsible inheritance**.

Reverence reminds us that we are not the beginning of everything. Truthfulness prevents love of a tradition from becoming concealment of its failures. Responsibility prevents judgment from becoming spectatorship; it asks us to continue unfinished work.

History is also not a transcendent judge that will eventually deliver one final verdict upon every person. Records are partial. Memory changes. Power rewrites narratives. Future generations will disagree. If being praised by history becomes the highest objective, conduct remains captured by name.

What enters history is not only what is remembered by name, but what changes consequence. An unknown person ends violence within a family. A teacher preserves habits of truth. An official refuses a harmful order. A designer leaves future users rights of exit and correction. Such acts may never enter a chronicle, yet they alter the field in which later lives occur.

> **Chronicles preserve names. History carries consequences.**

## Chapter 22 — Lineage, *Ye*, and Reform: Inheritance Is Not Submission, Repentance Is Not Erasure

Lineage is often reduced to blood, rank, pedigree, or an exclusive claim to orthodoxy. A living lineage, however, is not acquired automatically by birth and does not demand passive repetition.

Ideas have lineages. Crafts have lineages. Practices of courage and care have lineages. Public institutions have lineages. A lineage is a chain of practice: earlier people confronted problems, developed forms and methods, and passed them to later people, who test and transform them under new conditions.

Inheritance contains three obligations:

1. preserve what still carries life-giving capacity;
2. acknowledge and repair what has produced harm;
3. transmit not only answers but the capacity to question, understand, and create anew.

A tradition that survives only by prohibiting examination has lost *shengsheng*. An innovation that prides itself on severing all memory loses orientation and repeats forgotten errors. Genuine inheritance neither kneels nor forgets.

### *Ye*: The Structured Continuation of Action

The term *ye* (业), commonly associated with karma, need not be imagined as a supernatural ledger of reward and punishment. Every action leaves form in at least three places:

- it shapes the actor, making similar action easier or harder next time;
- it shapes others, altering their trust, fear, ability, and response;
- it shapes the environment, becoming through repetition a habit, rule, institution, or culture.

The consequence of an action does not end when the action ends. Consequence changes the conditions of the next moment and thereby becomes a new cause. Trust lowers defense. Violence leaves fear. Care restores agency. Humiliation trains further humiliation.

This tendency is not fate. Inherited momentum may be strong, but it is not absolutely closed. Seeing, acknowledging, stopping, compensating, and rebuilding can change the direction in which *ye* continues.

### Repentance and Reform

Genuine repentance is not a sentence that makes the past disappear. Nor is it self-annihilation through shame. It includes:

- stating truthfully what happened without shrinking the injury through noble excuses;
- hearing who carried the cost;
- stopping repetition and changing conditions that made repetition easy;
- repairing what can be repaired and accepting that repair takes time;
- not demanding immediate forgiveness as proof of the harmed person’s goodness;
- forming a new pattern of trust through sustained conduct.

Reform cannot make an injury never to have occurred. It can prevent the injury from owning the future.

This applies to persons, families, organizations, and states. A community incapable of admitting error must continually build larger narratives to protect its self-image. What is suppressed then remains active as distrust, resentment, and repeated injury.

Merit should likewise not be understood as a moral possession accumulated by the self. An act has merit when the world acquires new capacity through it: needless suffering is reduced, agency is restored, knowledge is preserved, institutions become more trustworthy, conflict ceases to reproduce across generations, or those who come later receive a path that did not exist before.

When merit becomes “my merit,” goodness is again occupied by the self. The highest life-giving action does not insist upon owning what it brings forth.

## Chapter 23 — Person, Family, Polity, and *Tianxia*: Responsibility Grows with the Radius of Consequence

Not everyone can change all-under-Heaven, and not everyone bears the same responsibility. People occupy different positions and possess different knowledge, resources, authority, and ranges of consequence.

To live well is neither to carry an infinite burden nor to use limitation as an excuse for every avoidable harm. It is to understand one’s actual position without exaggerating or fleeing it.

### 1. The Person: Do Not Become a Source of Closure

At the level of one life, *shengsheng* begins in ordinary conduct: do not knowingly manufacture falsehood, do not pass pain to others without necessity, keep promises that can be kept, acknowledge error, care for body and heart-mind, and develop the capacity to understand.

Such acts may appear small, but they shape expectation. One honest person cannot repair an entire institution, but can make one relation less defensive. One person who truly reforms shows that error need not end in denial.

### 2. The Family: Do Not Let Intimacy Become a License to Dominate

The family is the earliest school of trust, ritual, power, and conflict. Ordering the family should not mean establishing patriarchal obedience. It means arranging care, responsibility, boundary, and expression so that intimacy remains life-giving.

Love does not cancel standing. Control in the name of love, humiliation in the name of tradition, and permanent obedience demanded in return for upbringing transform nurture into enclosure. A good family teaches both how to receive care and how to care, how to voice difference and how to sustain relation.

A family that interrupts inherited violence performs profound historical work.

### 3. The Organization: Let Reality Travel Upward

Differences of authority are often necessary in organizations. The more authority is concentrated, however, the more essential it becomes to protect upward truth. The *de* of leadership is not infallibility. It is the ability to make disagreement safe, prevent all credit from rising and all cost from falling, and recognize a limit where objectives begin to consume human wholeness.

An organization that can only report success becomes increasingly intelligent at deceiving itself.

### 4. The Polity: Let Strength Contribute to All-under-Heaven Rather Than Feed upon It

A state has responsibilities to protect people, maintain order, and develop collective capacity. But if every external actor is interpreted as a tool or enemy, security becomes an unlimited reason for expansion and creates the world it fears.

Genuine security is not only relative superiority. It includes participation in an order where survival does not require perpetual escalation. A nation whose prosperity depends indefinitely upon exhausted lands, invisible labor, and future conflict does not possess stable strength.

*Tianxia* (天下), all-under-Heaven, should not mean territory owned by one empire or a world governed by one center. It first names a fact: peoples, communities, and living systems already inhabit one field of consequence. Climate, disease, finance, networks, and artificial intelligence do not stop at borders.

### 5. All-under-Heaven: Shared Obligation without Homogenization

Responsibility for *tianxia* does not require all civilizations to adopt one institution or way of life. It means that beyond difference there are shared limits: annihilation is not a normal instrument of policy; global commons cannot be privately exhausted; irreversible risk cannot be transferred to the voiceless; no victor may monopolize the power to define the future.

A life-giving world order enables local communities to retain capacities of self-formation, prevents global connection from becoming one-way extraction, allows knowledge and technology to spread as common wealth, and lets civilizations learn from one another without erasing their grain.

“When the Great Way is practiced, all-under-Heaven is held in common.” The common does not mean that everything belongs to a central authority. It means that the shared world is not privately occupied.

### 6. Kingship and Power: Maximum Responsibility, Not Sacred Personality

Classical texts used the language of kingly government to describe legitimate large-scale coordination. Modern societies need not restore kingship in order to retain one deep insight: the holder of greater power must answer to the wider life affected by that power and may not treat public capacity as private possession.

Power does not confer higher human worth. It enlarges the radius of consequence. Ethical expectation should therefore rise with power, not fall before it.

## Chapter 24 — Truth, Goodness, and Beauty: Knowing, Enacting, and Feeling *Shengsheng*

Truth, goodness, and beauty are not three sealed domains. They are three ways in which unceasing generation becomes available to human life.

### 1. Truth: Returning Our Images to the Grain of What Is Happening

Human beings do not possess the whole world directly. We take *xiang* through names, concepts, diagrams, narratives, and models. To seek truth is not to claim a view from nowhere. It is to keep one’s images answerable to events and prevent interest, identity, or desire from making correction impossible.

Truth requires refusal of deliberate falsehood, refusal to present uncertainty as certainty, refusal to let authority replace evidence, and refusal to elevate one success into an eternal rule. It also requires institutions through which a community can seek truth: open records, independent testing, protected dissent, and visible correction.

Without truth, goodness is easily captured by illusion. People can sincerely produce great harm because the world in which they believe has been systematically distorted.

### 2. Goodness: Making One’s Reach More Capable of Further Life

Goodness is not an abstract performance of saving the universe. It is the reduction of life-closing patterns and the enlargement of life-giving conditions within one’s actual reach. Sometimes it is care; sometimes restraint; sometimes the creation of a rule; sometimes refusal to participate; sometimes the transfer of opportunity and capacity.

Goodness must be fitting. A grand intention detached from position, ability, and timing may control others in the name of rescue or sacrifice long continuity for immediate purity. Fittingness does not weaken the ideal. It brings the ideal into consequence.

### 3. Beauty: The Felt Manifestation of Life-Giving Coherence

Beauty is more than prettiness or pleasure. We often experience beauty where difference and unity, strength and restraint, completion and openness appear together. A tree discloses how life takes form through light, soil, gravity, and time. Music allows distinct voices to become more through relation. A beautiful character can join courage with tenderness, intelligence with humility, and strength with boundary.

Beauty makes a world not only survivable but worth inhabiting. A civilization organized only by efficiency may be materially rich and experientially barren. Public space, art, ritual, nature, leisure, and play are not decorative additions to production. They are ways in which life feels its own abundance.

Beauty can also be captured by power. Uniform obedience, monumental machinery, and pure speed can produce awe. Truth and goodness must therefore examine beauty: does the apparent harmony genuinely hold difference, or has dissent been removed? Is magnificence an expression of shared capacity, or is it built upon invisible suffering?

### 4. Their Mutual Completion

Truth prevents illusion from determining direction. Goodness brings understanding into action. Beauty makes *shengsheng* perceptible, lovable, and desirable.

Goodness without truth becomes blind. Truth without goodness becomes cold. Truth and goodness without beauty struggle to endure within human desire and memory. A life-giving civilization must preserve institutions of inquiry, capacities of care and justice, and environments in which beauty can appear.

## Chapter 25 — *Datong*: Not an Endpoint, but a Recoverable Condition of Common Becoming

*Datong* is often imagined as a final perfect society toward which history advances in a straight line. Such an image is inconsistent with the transformations of Heaven and Earth and can justify present oppression in the name of a promised future.

A life-giving world will not be free of difference, conflict, failure, or death. *Datong* does not solve every problem. It names a condition in which a community possesses sufficient humaneness, rightness, ritual, wisdom, and trust that problems need not always be resolved through the sacrifice of the weak, the closure of truth, and the destruction of the common world.

It is a dynamic and recoverable condition in which:

- basic life and participation are not monopolized;
- difference can speak, argue, and enter common decision;
- power has boundaries and reality can reach it;
- technological and material gains enlarge common capacity rather than only the advantage of winners;
- traditions can be inherited and corrected;
- failure does not mean permanent expulsion;
- nature and posterity are not treated as silent resources;
- civilizations can remain distinct while maintaining global commons.

*Datong* is not sameness. It is the common possession of a world no party may consume entirely.

Such a condition never maintains itself automatically. Good forms harden. Institutions become self-serving. New technologies alter old balances. *Datong* therefore requires repeated investigation, rectification of names, regulation, repair, and renewal. It is not a perfect design completed once and for all. It is a civilization’s capacity to return to the Great Way after drift.

For this reason, *shengsheng* is not optimism that everything naturally improves. It is a continuing practice: asking again for limits whenever power grows, rebuilding trust whenever shared reality breaks, reinterpreting a tradition when its form no longer carries life, and changing the environment when evil begins to reproduce itself.

*Datong* is also not a unilateral instruction from China to the rest of the world. If it accords with Dao, it cannot be realized through cultural superiority or forced assimilation. What Chinese thought may offer is not one form all peoples must copy, but another way of seeing: understanding existence through mutual formation rather than isolation, measuring goodness by life-giving rather than possession, understanding order through harmony without sameness rather than domination by the winner, and understanding human destiny through all-under-Heaven rather than permanent zero-sum enclosure.

Part V may be gathered into one claim:

> **Human beings inherit the consequences of the past and become conditions of the future. Lineage is the transmission of responsible practice; *ye* is the continuation of action in character, relation, and environment; truth, goodness, and beauty are the knowable, practicable, and felt forms of *shengsheng*; *datong* is the repeatedly recoverable human condition in which different beings can flourish together without closing one another’s way.**

# Conclusion — The World Continues Becoming through Us

We are not isolated consciousnesses placed outside the world to observe it.

We are born between Heaven and Earth, formed by matter, life, language, relation, history, and the actions of innumerable people before us. Our bodies belong to the circulation of land and living things. Our minds are nourished by a world already underway. The manifestations we can perceive, the names we can utter, and the judgments we can make arise from the world becoming intelligible here.

Human knowing is therefore not simply an inner subject looking upon an external object.

At a deeper level, a portion of the world has acquired, in living beings and in human communities, the ability to feel, remember, reflect, imagine, and choose. Through life, the world senses itself. Through heart-mind, it forms images of itself. Through action, it changes the form it will take next.

We see mountains, rivers, living beings, institutions, histories, other people, and ourselves. This seeing is itself an event within the transformations of the cosmos.

We do not merely live in the world.

> **We are among the ways the world is happening.**

Heaven and Earth are not a static background. Cosmos, life, civilization, and awareness are processes of transformation, formation, and entry into new relations. What we call “I” is a temporary gathering within this long becoming, a place that can feel and answer.

A drop belongs to a river. A person belongs to family, society, ecology, and history. A thought becomes action; action becomes habit; habit becomes institution; institution shapes the thoughts of people not yet born.

No *xiang* exists wholly alone. Every formation is formed by others and becomes a condition for others. Every consequence inherits causes and enters future causation. This is true of persons, civilizations, and the myriad things.

Our actions are therefore more than fleeting choices inside one private lifetime.

When we preserve truth, the world contains a little less illusion here. When we interrupt cruelty, a possible chain of consequence changes here. When we create knowledge, care for life, restore relation, or establish justice, the world acquires through our existence a structure and a future it did not previously possess.

We do not create the future from nothing, and private will cannot command the whole. We inherit a formed Heaven and Earth, a history, and a field of *ye*. We can act only within the conditions of this time and place.

Yet because the world is unfinished and the future remains unsealed, judgment and action matter.

Every truthful understanding allows the world to understand itself more adequately at this point. Every fitting good allows the world to continue generating here. Every creation of beauty opens a new sensible form of existence.

Human freedom is not freedom from consequence. It is awareness of one’s place in an interconnected world and the capacity to make the consequences that pass through this place open toward a wider becoming.

To become aware of this is to stop treating the self as master of the world, but also to stop treating the self as a meaningless particle.

I am finite and participate in what exceeds me. I receive the past and generate the future. I am one manifestation among the myriad and one place where the myriad become partially aware.

This is one meaning of attaining Dao.

To attain Dao is not to possess a final answer or to stand outside the world and see everything. It is to realize that one has never been separate from Heaven and Earth; that every act of perception, understanding, and conduct belongs to a field of mutual generation; that private obstruction can be loosened so one’s action becomes more fitting to the real grain of things; and that one’s own flourishing need not close the life of others.

Dao is not only distant and not only in classical texts.

It is present in the growth of plants, the metabolism of living bodies, human care and promise, the transmission and correction of history, and every action through which the world becomes more capable of continuing.

When human beings understand themselves as participants in the generation of Heaven and Earth, they no longer need to prove their worth by conquering the world. When they understand that the myriad things are connected, they become less willing to secure a local flourishing through wider extinction. When they understand that the future is being formed now, they treat each manifestation they produce with greater care.

Heaven and humanity no longer stand opposed.

Human awareness is Heaven and Earth becoming aware here. Human creation is the world continuing to unfold here. Human responsibility is to prevent this unfolding from consuming its own sources and to guide it toward more truth, more goodness, more beauty, and more possibilities of further becoming.

*Shengsheng buxi* is therefore not merely life reproducing somewhere outside us.

We ourselves are part of unceasing generation.

The world gives rise to us, and through our understanding and action we participate in the world’s renewal. We receive all that has already occurred and entrust unrealized possibility to the future.

> **We are a process within Heaven and Earth, within the universe’s continuing transformation and generation.**  
> **The world forms images of itself in us and continues changing itself through our action.**  
> **We are one among the myriad manifestations and a place where the myriad become aware; a consequence of the past and a cause of the future.**  
> **To realize our interconnection and our participation in the world’s becoming is to approach Dao.**  
> **To follow the real grain of things, not exhaust the source of generation, and leave the future open to further possibility is *shengsheng buxi*.**  
> **Humanity never leaves Heaven and Earth; Heaven and Earth continue becoming through humanity. This is what it means for Dao to follow *ziran*.**

# Appendix I — A Short Lexicon of Core Terms

## *Sheng* 生

To arise, grow, bring forth, form a capacity, or open a way. It is wider than biological birth.

## *Shengsheng* 生生

Generation that gives rise to further generation. What has formed becomes a condition for later arising. Its measure is not quantity but transmission, repair, and renewal.

## *Buxi* 不息

Not the immortality of one form, but the non-exhaustion of the source of further becoming and the non-closure of transformation.

## Dao 道

The course by which the myriad things proceed, meet, form, and transform. Dao is not an external ruler, a substance, or a law possessed by one party.

## *Ziran* 自然

“So of itself”: the way something unfolds from what it is and from its actual conditions. It should not be reduced to “Nature” as a separate domain.

## *Qi* 气

A way of attending to circulation, gathering, dispersal, resonance, and continuity through which beings participate in one another. It should not be equated simply with matter or energy.

## *Xiang* 象

A manifest configuration: the way something appears and becomes answerable within a concrete relation. It is not merely an internal representation.

## Names and Images

Human selections, descriptions, narratives, and models of manifest configurations. They make knowledge possible and can also obstruct reality when their limits are forgotten.

## Form

The boundary, organization, and mode of continuity through which becoming can be carried. Form sustains life and becomes rigid when it cannot transform.

## Instrument or Vessel 器

A concrete carrier of practice, institution, or technology. Instruments are necessary but must not elevate their local measure into Dao.

## *Li* 理

The grain, connectedness, causality, and fitting proportion discernible in concrete affairs. It is not a transcendent entity separate from things.

## *Zhong* 中

Situated centrality or fittingness through which forces find an appropriate measure. It is not simple compromise or arithmetic averaging.

## *De* 德

The embodied capacity or realized efficacy through which a person or institution carries Dao in practice. It is more than a list of moral virtues.

## *Ren* 仁

Humaneness: responsiveness that refuses to reduce another life to a mere means.

## *Yi* 义

Fitting rightness: situating local benefit within a larger relation and making responsibility proportionate to power.

## Ritual Form 礼

Repeatable practices through which respect, boundary, responsibility, and collective memory become visible and durable.

## Wisdom 智

The ability to see manifest configurations, discern their grain, judge the direction of consequence, and revise in changing circumstances.

## Trust / Trustworthiness 信

A reliable relation among speech, judgment, promise, and conduct that allows a common future to be carried. It depends upon truth and examination rather than blind loyalty.

## Good

That which enables life by preserving, restoring, and enlarging conditions of meaningful becoming.

## Evil

That which closes life by allowing one appetite, moment, or power to consume and seal the conditions shared by many forms of becoming.

## Harmony 和

A relation in which difference need not become mutual destruction and can become mutually corrective and fruitful. Harmony is neither sameness nor the absence of conflict.

## Rectification 正

The restoration of boundary, truthful feedback, and standing when a force has drifted into domination or obstruction.

## Government / Ordering 治

The regulation of feedback, preservation of cooperative memory, limitation of predatory force, and maintenance of collective corrigibility.

## *Tianxia* 天下

All-under-Heaven: the shared world in which peoples, communities, and living systems already affect one another. It is not the property of one state or a homogeneous empire.

## *Datong* 大同

A dynamic condition in which the common world is sufficiently shared, power sufficiently bounded, truth sufficiently accessible, and the future sufficiently open for different forms of life to flourish together. It is not a static endpoint.

# Appendix II — Nine Questions for Judging Whether an Action or Institution Is Life-Giving

1. **Truth** — Does it face reality, or depend upon concealment, manipulation, and falsehood?
2. **Renewal** — Does it replenish the living and social conditions it consumes?
3. **Humaneness** — Are affected people still treated as beings with feeling, dignity, and future?
4. **Rightness** — How are benefit and cost distributed? Is responsibility proportionate to power?
5. **Trust** — Does it increase verifiable cooperation, or force everyone into greater defense?
6. **Wisdom** — Does it consider scale, time, feedback, and the worth of the objective itself, or only optimize an immediate metric?
7. **Harmony** — Does it hold difference and make it fruitful, or consume difference through uniformity, exclusion, or struggle?
8. **Correction** — Can error be discovered, acknowledged, repaired, and appealed? Can affected people speak or exit?
9. **Posterity** — What will later people inherit? Do they retain paths of understanding, choice, and renewal?

These questions are not an algorithm. They are disciplines of attention. They ask an actor to step outside one interest, one time horizon, and one measure, and to see again the continuity of Heaven and Earth, self and other, past and future.

# Appendix IV — Classical Sources and the Limits of Interpretation

This book is a contemporary reconstruction grounded in Chinese intellectual language. It does not claim that early Chinese thinkers already assembled this entire argument in the present terminology or sequence. The following texts provide its principal classical bearings. The accounts of co-created reality, totalizing obstruction, digital environments, capital, and artificial intelligence are contemporary extensions rather than doctrines attributed directly to the ancients.

- **“Generation giving rise to further generation is what is meant by change”** appears in the first *Xici* commentary to the *Yijing*. The present book takes it as a basis for understanding change as continued generation, not merely altered properties of an otherwise static thing.
- **“The great potency of Heaven and Earth is life-giving”** appears in the second *Xici* commentary. It supports the connection developed here between the transformations of Heaven and Earth and *de* as an embodied life-giving capacity.
- **“One yin and one yang are called Dao”** appears in the first *Xici*. It informs the view that Dao does not abolish difference but appears through patterned alternation and mutual implication.
- **“Dao follows *ziran*”** appears in chapter 25 of the *Daodejing*. *Ziran* is interpreted here primarily as “so of itself,” not as a separate domain called Nature.
- **“Dao gives birth to one; one to two; two to three; three to the myriad things”** appears in chapter 42 of the *Daodejing*. The present work reads it as an unfolding of differentiation and fecundity, not as manufacture by an external creator.
- **“Harmony truly brings forth things; sameness does not continue”** appears in the “Discourses of Zheng” in the *Guoyu*. It is central to the distinction between harmony and homogeneity.
- **“The myriad things grow together without harming one another; ways proceed together without contradicting one another”** and **the passage on bringing centrality and harmony to fulfillment** appear in the *Zhongyong*. They guide the account of dynamic fittingness and common generation.
- **“The exemplary person harmonizes without becoming identical”** appears in the “Zilu” chapter of the *Analects*. It supports the claim that consensus need not mean identical conclusions and coordination need not mean total control.
- **“To govern is to rectify”** appears in the “Yan Yuan” chapter of the *Analects*. Rectification is interpreted here as reopening truth, boundary, and public responsibility when institutions drift.
- **The four beginnings of compassion, shame, deference, and right-and-wrong discernment** appear in “Gongsun Chou I” of the *Mencius*. They ground the account of moral responsiveness as an incipient capacity rather than a complete automatic answer.
- **“What one knows without deliberation is *liangzhi*”** appears in “Jinxin I” of the *Mencius*. This is the early basis for the use of *liangzhi* in the book, while the argument also stresses cultivation and correction.
- **Xunzi’s discussions of desire, ritual, human nature, and deliberate cultivation** are found especially in “Human Nature Is Bad” and “Discourse on Ritual.” Mencius and Xunzi are not reduced here to a simple choice between optimism and pessimism; together they disclose both moral beginnings and the formative power of practice and institutions.
- **Cook Ding’s following of “heavenly pattern” and “what is inherently so”** appears in the “Nourishing Life” chapter of the *Zhuangzi*. It supports the use of *li* as concrete grain, opening, and fittingness rather than an externally imposed law.
- **Free wandering and the distinction between dependence and imagined independence** are developed in the opening chapter of the *Zhuangzi*. Free wandering is interpreted here as release from total captivity by one measure, role, or reputation, not as abandonment of responsibility.
- **The sequence of investigating things, extending knowing, making intention sincere, rectifying the heart-mind, and cultivating the person** comes from the *Great Learning*. Investigation is interpreted here as entering an affair and examining its actual operation, including one’s own interests and assumptions.
- **“When the Great Way is practiced, all-under-Heaven is held in common”** appears in the “Liyun” chapter of the *Book of Rites*. “Held in common” is not interpreted as possession by one center, but as the refusal to let the shared world become private property.

These texts have always admitted multiple interpretations. The boundaries maintained by this work are therefore as important as its positive claims: Dao should not be turned into a substance; *ziran* should not be turned into social Darwinism; ritual should not be reduced to obedience; harmony should not be used to silence conflict; *tianxia* should not be converted into imperial ownership; and later rigid hierarchies should not be projected backward as the sole meaning of early Chinese thought.
