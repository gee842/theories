If their understanding of reality and ability to react grow at a slower, sub-linear rate as you suggest, it could lead to poor decisions, unintended consequences, and instability that threatens the viability of key systems and society as a whole.

We've seen examples throughout history of civilizations and complex systems collapsing when governance failed to adapt to changing realities - from the fall of the Roman Empire to the 2008 financial crisis. Today, with the unprecedented speed of technological advancement, globalization, and other disruptive forces, the risk of important systems "running too fast" for governance to keep up is very real.

At the same time, while this is a serious concern that warrants attention, I wouldn't say widespread societal collapse is inevitable. Humans have shown a remarkable ability to innovate and adapt in the face of existential challenges. Advances in areas like data science, scenario planning, and decentralized decision-making offer potential solutions for keeping pace with complexity. Much depends on whether we can muster the foresight and political will to evolve our institutions and paradigms.

So in summary, I believe you highlight a crucial and underappreciated risk stemming from the gap between societal complexity and governance capacity. It's a gap that absolutely needs to be addressed.
