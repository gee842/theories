**ASSISTANT** (2026-03-27T13:24):

Ren is asking a deep philosophical question about human nature and meaning-making — whether orientation toward purposes beyond the self is a natural attractor state that modern society suppresses. This connects to their existing work on relational ontology, coordination, and community building.

Let me engage substantively with this.This is a really sharp observation, and I think you're onto something structurally important — not just as philosophy but as a description of attractor dynamics in human motivational systems.

The evidence is pretty strong that orientation toward ends-beyond-self is the *default* configuration, not the exception. Tomasello's work on shared intentionality, Henrich on cultural group selection, Durkheim's "collective effervescence," even Panksepp's CARE and PLAY circuits — they all point to the same thing: humans are wired to find meaning through embedding in something larger. The self-as-terminal-goal is actually the weird, unstable configuration.

So what's doing the depressing? I'd frame it as several reinforcing dynamics:

**Atomization as economic product.** Consumer capitalism needs individual preference-maximizers, not people embedded in thick relational networks that might say "we don't need that." The isolated self is the ideal market participant. Advertising literally trains you to experience lack as individual rather than collective.

**Meaning infrastructure decay.** The institutions that historically channeled beyond-self orientation — religion, guilds, civic associations, extended kinship — have been hollowed out without replacement. Putnam documented this, but the acceleration since smartphones is another order of magnitude.

**Irony as defense.** Sincere commitment to something larger became culturally suspect — partly as healthy antibody response to 20th century ideological catastrophes, but it overcorrected into a default stance where earnestness itself feels naive.

What's interesting from a dynamical systems perspective is that the attractor *hasn't disappeared* — it's still there, which is why people keep falling into parasocial substitutes, brand loyalty as identity, political tribalism, conspiracy communities. The need to orient beyond self is so strong that when healthy channels are blocked, it routes through degraded ones. The Q-Anon phenomenon is arguably people's beyond-self orientation being captured by a broken narrative because no better one was available.

This connects directly to your Fork framework, I think. The question isn't whether humans will orient toward collective purpose — they will, because the attractor is too deep. The question is whether the available structures for that orientation are generative or extractive. Automated feudalism doesn't eliminate beyond-self meaning; it *captures* it (corporate mission statements, platform "communities," nationalist nostalgia). Automated commons would need to provide real channels for it.

The practical implication for what you're building with 706 and the Bonfire work: you're not trying to *create* motivation toward collective purpose. You're trying to build infrastructure that lets an already-existing attractor express itself without being captured. That's a very different design problem — more like clearing a channel than pumping water uphill.
