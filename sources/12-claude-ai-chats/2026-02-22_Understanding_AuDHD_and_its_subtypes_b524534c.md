The user is asking about AuDHD - the co-occurrence of Autism and ADHD. This is a knowledge question I can answer directly without searching.AuDHD refers to having both Autism (ASD) and ADHD simultaneously — which is more common than people used to think. Research now suggests 50-70% of autistic people also meet criteria for ADHD.

**How it differs from neurotypical experience**

The core difference is that you're running two neurological operating systems that often *contradict* each other. Autism tends to crave routine, predictability, and deep focus on specific interests, while ADHD craves novelty, stimulation, and resists structure. This internal tug-of-war creates a distinctive experience:

- **Paralysis loops**: Wanting to do something (ADHD novelty-seeking) but being unable to start because the plan isn't perfectly structured (autistic rigidity). Or having a perfect routine that ADHD executive dysfunction keeps blowing up.
- **Masking exhaustion on two fronts**: Suppressing stims *and* forcing yourself to sit still and focus, constantly managing both sets of "tells."
- **Interest intensity**: Autistic special interests collide with ADHD hyperfocus, creating incredibly deep but potentially short-lived obsessions — or long-lived ones you keep abandoning and returning to.
- **Sensory paradox**: Autism often means sensory sensitivity (overstimulation), while ADHD often means sensory seeking (understimulation). You can flip between "everything is too much" and "I need more input" within the same hour.
- **Social confusion**: Autistic difficulty reading social cues + ADHD impulsivity means you might blurt things out *and* not realize you've misread the room.

**Sub-categories / profiles**

There isn't an official clinical taxonomy yet, but the community and researchers generally recognize profiles based on which condition "leads":

1. **Autism-dominant AuDHD** — Externally looks more classically autistic. Stronger need for routine, more visible sensory issues, special interests are long-term and deep. The ADHD shows up more internally (racing thoughts, emotional dysregulation, executive dysfunction behind the scenes).

2. **ADHD-dominant AuDHD** — Externally looks more like "classic ADHD." More visibly impulsive, scattered, social. The autism shows up as hidden rigidity, meltdowns when plans change unexpectedly, or intense private interests that others don't see.

3. **Balanced / oscillating** — Neither clearly dominates. The person swings between autistic and ADHD states depending on stress, environment, sleep, etc. This is often the most confusing profile because the presentation is inconsistent.

Some people also distinguish by **internalizing vs. externalizing** presentation — whether the conflict plays out visibly (meltdowns, hyperactivity, blurting) or invisibly (shutdown, burnout, internal chaos behind a calm exterior). The internalizing version is especially common in women and AFAB people, which is part of why AuDHD is underdiagnosed in those groups.

The field is still catching up — AuDHD wasn't even diagnostically possible until 2013, when the DSM-5 dropped the rule against dual diagnosis. So a lot of the nuance is still being mapped out. What's driving the question — personal exploration, or more of a curiosity thing?

The user is asking whether autism and ADHD share the same root cause, since they seem like unrelated conditions. This is a great question with interesting emerging research.Great question — on the surface they look like opposites, but the genetic and neurological overlap is surprisingly deep.

**The genetic picture**

Twin studies and genome-wide association studies (GWAS) show substantial shared genetic architecture — roughly 50-70% genetic correlation between ASD and ADHD. Many of the same gene variants contribute to both conditions, particularly genes involved in synaptic development, dopamine and serotonin signaling, and neural connectivity. It's not that there's "an autism gene" and "an ADHD gene" — it's more like a shared pool of neurodevelopmental risk genes that can express differently depending on which combinations you get and how they interact with environment.

**The neurological overlap**

Both involve atypical development in executive function networks, particularly the prefrontal cortex and its connections. The differences show up in *how* those networks are atypical:

- In ADHD, the default mode network (daydreaming/internal thought) doesn't quiet down properly when you need to focus — it keeps intruding.
- In autism, connectivity patterns tend to be *hyper-local* (intense processing within regions) but *under-connected* between distant brain regions.
- In AuDHD, you often see both patterns simultaneously.

**Why they look so different**

Think of it like this: the shared root is **atypical neural wiring during development**. But the *downstream effects* diverge because they affect different balances:

- Autism skews toward **over-processing** — too much signal, too much pattern detection, too much detail. Hence sensory overload, rigid systematizing, deep focus.
- ADHD skews toward **under-regulation** — not enough top-down control over attention, impulse, and arousal. Hence distractibility, impulsivity, novelty-seeking.

Same foundation (neurodevelopmental wiring differences), different tuning knobs turned in different directions. When you get both, it's because enough of those shared genetic variants expressed in ways that pushed *both* knobs into atypical ranges.

**An analogy**

Imagine two buildings with the same faulty foundation. One develops cracks that make it too rigid (it can't flex in wind). The other develops cracks that make it too flexible (it sways too much). Different symptoms, same underlying structural vulnerability. AuDHD is the building that somehow does both.

The "they seem unrelated" intuition comes from looking at *behaviors*. Once you look at the neurobiology, they're more like cousins than strangers.

**Pattern recognition on overdrive.** The autistic brain tends to be a relentless systematizer — it pulls apart systems, finds patterns, builds models. When you layer ADHD's associative, non-linear thinking on top, you get someone who can see deep structure *and* make wild lateral connections between unrelated domains. That combination is basically the recipe for creative insight.

**Hyperfocus + special interests = accidental expertise.** A neurotypical person interested in a topic reads a few articles. An AuDHD person might consume everything available in 72 hours, build a mental model of the entire field, then connect it to three other interests. This produces depth of knowledge that reads as intelligence, but it's really an intensity difference.

**Compensatory intelligence from masking.** AuDHD people who function in neurotypical environments are essentially running a real-time social simulation engine in their heads — constantly modeling other people's expectations, monitoring their own behavior, translating between their internal logic and the social world. Doing this from childhood builds genuine cognitive muscle, particularly in abstract modeling and theory of mind. You had to *think your way through* what others do intuitively.

**Selection bias — the ones you notice.** This is the big caveat. There are plenty of AuDHD people who are average intelligence or who are intelligent but completely debilitated by executive dysfunction, sensory issues, or burnout. You don't see them because they're not producing visible output. The "hyper-intelligent AuDHD" archetype is survivorship bias — you're seeing the ones whose intelligence was high enough to compensate for the disability, making them visible.

The research is mixed. Autistic people show a wider *spread* of IQ scores than the general population — more people at both extremes. ADHD correlates with roughly average IQ but with a distinctive *profile*: often high verbal/fluid reasoning paired with lower processing speed and working memory. AuDHD specifically hasn't been studied enough to have clean data, but clinically, the "spiky profile" is the signature — huge gaps between what you're exceptional at and what you're terrible at, rather than uniformly high scores.

**The deeper point**

What AuDHD brains are genuinely better at, on average, isn't raw intelligence — it's **a different cognitive topology**. Neurotypical thinking tends to be more uniform and socially calibrated. AuDHD thinking tends to be more extreme, more idiosyncratic, more willing to follow a logical thread to uncomfortable or non-obvious conclusions. In environments that reward that (research, engineering, art, entrepreneurship), it looks like genius. In environments that reward consistency and social fluency, it looks like disability.

Same brain, different context, wildly different outcomes.

**ASSISTANT** (2026-02-22T19:50):

The user wants to understand what "atypical connections" means in the context of neurodevelopmental conditions like autism and ADHD.The brain is basically a network — 86 billion neurons wired together. "Atypical connections" means that network is organized differently from the statistical norm. Here's what that actually means concretely:

**How a typical brain is wired**

Imagine a city. You have local roads within neighborhoods (short-range connections) and highways between cities (long-range connections). A neurotypical brain has a fairly standard balance — good local processing within brain regions, and efficient highways connecting distant regions so they can coordinate.

The key infrastructure includes things like the corpus callosum (highway between left and right hemispheres), white matter tracts (bundles of long-range axons connecting distant areas), and synaptic density within local regions.

**What's different in autism**

The autistic brain tends to be **over-connected locally, under-connected long-range**. Using the city analogy: the neighborhoods have incredibly dense, detailed street grids — every alley and side street is built out — but the highways between cities are fewer, narrower, or noisier.

What this produces functionally: exceptional detail processing and pattern detection within a domain (because local circuits are rich), but difficulty integrating information *across* domains in real time. This is why an autistic person might notice a tiny visual detail everyone else misses, but struggle to simultaneously process that detail + someone's tone of voice + the social context of the room. It's not a processing *power* problem — it's a bandwidth-between-regions problem.

Specifically, the connectivity between the prefrontal cortex (planning, social reasoning) and areas like the amygdala (emotion), the fusiform gyrus (face processing), and the temporal lobes (language/social meaning) tends to be weaker or differently organized. That's a big part of why social cognition is effortful rather than automatic.

**What's different in ADHD**

ADHD connectivity issues are more about **network switching and regulation** than raw wiring topology. The brain has major functional networks that are supposed to take turns:

- **Default Mode Network (DMN)** — active when you're daydreaming, mind-wandering, doing internal reflection
- **Task Positive Network (TPN)** — active when you're focused on an external task
- **Salience Network** — the "switchboard" that decides which of the above should be active right now

In ADHD, the salience network is unreliable. The DMN doesn't fully deactivate when you need to focus, so internal thoughts keep leaking into task performance. And the TPN doesn't activate strongly enough unless the task provides high dopamine reward (novelty, urgency, personal interest). The connections between these networks are present but poorly *regulated* — the highway exists but the traffic lights are broken.

**What's different in AuDHD**

You get both simultaneously. Hyper-dense local processing with weak long-range integration *and* broken network switching. So the brain is doing incredibly intense local computation, struggling to coordinate across regions, *and* the system that's supposed to manage which network is active at any given moment isn't working reliably.

This is why AuDHD can feel like having a supercomputer with a terrible operating system — immense processing power that you can't reliably direct.

**What "atypical" actually looks like physically**

This isn't metaphor — it shows up on brain imaging:

- **Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI)** shows actual differences in white matter tract integrity (the physical "cables" between regions)
- **fMRI** shows different activation patterns — regions that should coordinate lighting up asynchronously, or regions that should take turns both firing simultaneously
- **Synaptic pruning differences** — during typical development, the brain aggressively prunes unused connections in adolescence, refining the network. In autism especially, this pruning process works differently, leaving more connections but less optimized ones

The word "atypical" just means statistically different from the median wiring pattern. It's not inherently worse — it's a different network topology with different performance characteristics. Some tasks it's better at, some it's worse at, and which tasks matter depends entirely on the environment you're operating in.

**ASSISTANT** (2026-02-22T19:55):

The user is asking whether there are people who have both high local connectivity AND high long-range/cross-region connectivity — essentially the "best of both worlds" brain wiring.Yes, and this is where it gets really interesting.

**The high-connectivity brain**

Some people show both dense local processing *and* strong long-range integration. This profile shows up in a few contexts:

**Giftedness research.** Studies on people with exceptionally high IQ or creative achievement consistently find greater *global efficiency* — both strong local clustering and strong long-range connections. Their brains essentially have dense neighborhoods *and* well-built highways. The technical term in network science is **high small-world index** — a network where any node can reach any other node in few steps, while still maintaining rich local structure.

This isn't just "more connections" in a brute force sense. It's better *organized* connectivity — the long-range connections are specifically linking the right hubs to each other, creating efficient information flow across the whole system.

**What this looks like functionally**

Someone with this profile can do what AuDHD brains struggle with: deep detail processing *and* real-time cross-domain integration simultaneously. They can zoom into the details of a problem while simultaneously holding the big picture, pulling in information from different sensory channels, and staying socially aware. It's the difference between having to consciously switch between local and global processing versus doing both in parallel.

**The openness-to-experience connection**

The personality trait "openness to experience" in Big Five psychology correlates with exactly this connectivity profile — higher integration between brain networks that don't usually talk to each other much. People high in openness tend to have more cross-talk between sensory regions, the default mode network, and executive areas. This is thought to be the neurological basis of creativity in neurotypical people — not the autistic route of hypersystematizing, but fluid association across domains.

**So why doesn't everyone have this?**

Cost. The brain is roughly 2% of body weight but uses about 20% of metabolic energy. Long-range connections are *expensive* — they require more myelin, more metabolic support, more oxygen. Dense local connections are also expensive. Having both maxed out is metabolically demanding and also introduces a risk: **noise**.

More connections means more potential for crosstalk and interference. The brain has to not only build the connections but *regulate* them — knowing when to let signals through and when to suppress them. This is why the high-connectivity profile, when it works well, produces exceptional cognition, but when the regulation isn't keeping up, it can shade into things like:

- **Sensory overload** — too much information flowing too freely
- **Anxiety and rumination** — the default mode network too efficiently connected to threat-detection systems
- **Psychosis spectrum experiences** — in extreme cases, too much crosstalk between regions that should be somewhat independent (e.g., internal speech circuits leaking into auditory processing)

There's actually a well-documented finding that the genetic variants associated with high intelligence overlap partially with those associated with mood disorders and psychotic spectrum conditions. The same wiring that enables exceptional integration can, if regulation falters, produce overwhelming inner experience.

**The spectrum of connectivity profiles**

Think of it as two independent axes:

- **Local connectivity**: low → high
- **Long-range connectivity**: low → high

| | Low long-range | High long-range |
|---|---|---|
| **High local** | Classic autistic profile — deep but siloed | The "gifted" profile — deep and integrated |
| **Low local** | General cognitive difficulty | Possibly the high-openness "creative generalist" — broad but shallow |

Reality is obviously more nuanced than a 2x2, and different brain regions can have different profiles. But this framing captures the basic landscape.

**The honest answer to your question**

Yes, these people exist, and they're disproportionately the ones who end up producing paradigm-shifting work — because they can do the deep systematic analysis *and* the cross-domain synthesis. But it's a costly configuration that runs hot, and the line between "exceptionally functional" and "overwhelmed" can be thinner than it looks from the outside.

**ASSISTANT** (2026-02-22T20:00):

The user is asking how to distinguish between these different connectivity profiles — how can you tell if someone has high local + high long-range vs high local + low long-range (autistic) vs other profiles. This could mean clinical assessment, neuroimaging, or behavioral indicators.This is the hard part. There's no simple test you can walk into a clinic and get. Here's what actually exists and what's practical:

**Neuroimaging (the direct route)**

This is the most literal way to see connectivity, but it's mostly a research tool, not a clinical one.

- **fMRI functional connectivity** — measures which regions activate together over time. Can map your actual network topology. But results vary depending on what you were doing in the scanner, your sleep the night before, caffeine intake, anxiety level. Test-retest reliability is mediocre for individual-level claims.
- **DTI/diffusion MRI** — maps the physical white matter tracts. More stable than fMRI but tells you about structural *potential* for connectivity, not how it's actually being used dynamically.
- **EEG coherence** — measures synchronization between brain regions in real time. Cheaper and more accessible than MRI. Some research groups use it to distinguish autistic connectivity patterns. But interpretation is still more art than science at the individual level.

The fundamental problem: neuroimaging can reliably distinguish *group averages* (autistic brains vs neurotypical brains as populations) but is much weaker at classifying *individuals*. Your brain on a bad day might look like someone else's brain on a good day.

**Neuropsychological testing (the practical route)**

This is what's actually available and more informative than most people realize. A full neuropsych battery doesn't image your brain but maps your *functional* connectivity profile through performance patterns.

The key is the **cognitive profile shape**, not the overall score:

**High local / low long-range (autistic pattern):**
- Large gap between perceptual reasoning (high) and processing speed (lower)
- Strong performance on detail-oriented tasks — block design, matrix reasoning, embedded figures
- Weaker on tasks requiring rapid integration across domains — comprehension subtests, absurdity detection under time pressure
- Strong single-channel performance that degrades sharply when you have to combine channels (e.g., listening while watching while planning)

**High local / high long-range (gifted/integrated pattern):**
- Uniformly high or at least consistently elevated across subtests
- Or, critically: high scores on *both* detail tasks and integration tasks
- Maintains performance under cross-domain load — can do the detail work while also tracking the big picture
- Fast processing speed that doesn't come at the cost of accuracy

**ADHD pattern (dysregulated switching):**
- Processing speed and working memory notably lower than verbal and perceptual reasoning
- High variability *within* a single test — brilliant answer, careless error, brilliant answer, random mistake
- Performance that's wildly inconsistent between sessions or even within the same hour
- Scores that improve dramatically under novel or high-stakes conditions (because dopamine)

**AuDHD pattern:**
- The spikiest profile of all — huge peaks and valleys across subtests
- Detail tasks: high. Integration tasks: inconsistent. Processing speed: often surprisingly low relative to reasoning ability. Working memory: could go either way.
- The signature tell: performance that seems to *contradict itself* — clearly brilliant in some areas, clearly struggling in others, in ways that don't make sense as a coherent single profile

**Behavioral observation (the everyday route)**

If you're trying to distinguish these profiles without any formal testing, here are the functional signatures:

**Can they do deep work AND pivot quickly?** High local + high long-range people can go deep on a problem and then smoothly shift to a completely different domain without much ramp-up time. Autistic-profile people go deep but transitioning is costly and slow. ADHD-profile people pivot easily but struggle to go deep unless interest-driven.

**How do they handle complexity vs. chaos?** Complexity is structured multi-dimensionality. Chaos is unstructured multi-dimensionality. The integrated high-connectivity person *thrives* on complexity and handles chaos reasonably. The autistic profile handles complexity well but chaos is overwhelming. The ADHD profile handles chaos better than you'd expect but may struggle with sustained complexity.

**What does their conversation pattern look like?** Integrated high-connectivity people make connections between disparate topics fluidly and those connections are *coherent* — you can follow the logic even if the jump is big. Autistic-profile people go extremely deep on one thread. ADHD-profile people make associative jumps that are sometimes brilliant and sometimes loose. AuDHD people alternate between all of these unpredictably.

**What breaks them?** This might be the most diagnostic question of all. The integrated brain breaks under sustained metabolic/emotional load — burnout, anxiety, overstimulation after prolonged periods of running hot. The autistic brain breaks when the environment becomes unpredictable and multi-channel. The ADHD brain breaks under monotony and low stimulation. The AuDHD brain can break under almost anything depending on the day, which is itself the tell.

**The honest limitation**

None of this is clean. These are statistical tendencies, not binary categories. Every individual is a unique mix, and the same person can present differently depending on stress, sleep, medication, life phase, and what they had for breakfast. The field doesn't yet have a reliable way to hand someone a precise connectivity profile the way you'd get a blood panel. What exists is pattern recognition across multiple data points — testing, observation, self-report, history — triangulated to build a picture.

If you're trying to figure out where *you* sit on this map, a comprehensive neuropsych evaluation with someone who understands giftedness, autism, and ADHD as overlapping rather than mutually exclusive categories is the best currently available tool. That expertise is rarer than it should be, but it exists.

The user wants to know what an experienced clinician actually looks for in observation — the behavioral markers that distinguish these connectivity profiles in practice.Good question. An experienced clinician who understands all these profiles isn't primarily listening to *what* you say. They're watching *how your mind moves*. Here's what they're actually tracking:

**The conversation itself is the test**

A skilled clinician uses open-ended conversation strategically. They're not just gathering information — they're watching your cognitive architecture in real time.

**Transition behavior.** They'll shift topics unexpectedly. Not to be rude, but to watch what happens. Does your mind follow smoothly? Do you resist the shift and try to finish your previous thought? Do you follow too eagerly and lose track of where you were? Do you somehow hold both threads? Each response pattern maps to a different profile.

The integrated high-connectivity person handles the shift gracefully — they can park the previous thread, engage with the new one, and often spontaneously connect the two. The autistic profile shows visible friction — a pause, slight irritation, or a need to explicitly close out the previous thread before moving on. The ADHD profile jumps willingly but may never return to the original point. AuDHD does something recognizably different each time, which is itself informative.

**Abstraction level tracking.** A really good clinician will move between concrete and abstract levels within the same topic. "Tell me about your morning routine" then "Why do you think routines matter to you" then "What does structure mean in your life more broadly." They're watching whether you can move fluidly between levels.

High local + high long-range people navigate these shifts naturally and often *anticipate* where the clinician is going. High local / low long-range people are more comfortable at either the concrete detail level or within a specific abstract system they've already built, but the *transition between levels* is where you see effort. They might give a very concrete answer to an abstract question, or an overly systematized answer that feels rehearsed rather than fluid.

**Error and repair patterns**

This is a big one. The clinician says something slightly ambiguous or makes a small mistake on purpose. How do you respond?

The integrated brain catches the ambiguity, holds multiple interpretations, and often asks a clarifying question that reveals they saw several possible meanings. The autistic profile may lock onto one literal interpretation and run with it, or catch the ambiguity but become uncomfortable with the unresolved uncertainty. The ADHD profile might not notice the ambiguity at all because they're already three steps ahead on their own thread. AuDHD might catch it *and* run ahead simultaneously, producing a response that's both oddly precise and oddly tangential.

**Emotional-cognitive integration**

The clinician brings up something emotionally loaded and watches whether the person can *think and feel at the same time*.

The high-connectivity integrated person can discuss something emotional while simultaneously analyzing it — they cry and explain why they're crying and connect it to a broader pattern in their life, all in the same breath. It's almost unsettling how fluidly they do this.

The autistic profile tends to separate emotion and cognition more — they either feel it and can't articulate it in the moment, or they analyze it from a distance with impressive clarity but the emotional access comes later, or not at all in the room. The processing happens, but more sequentially than in parallel.

ADHD shows emotional intensity that's immediate but sometimes disconnected from the narrative structure — they feel strongly but the *story* around the feeling may be scattered.

AuDHD can do something really distinctive: deliver an incredibly articulate analytical framework about their emotions while being visibly dysregulated, or go completely flat while describing something that clearly matters. The mismatch between the intellectual and emotional channels is the tell.

**The "too many models" sign**

This is something experienced clinicians develop intuition for. The high-connectivity integrated person has models of themselves that are *flexible* — they describe themselves differently depending on context, and the descriptions are coherent and complementary. "At work I'm like this, with friends I'm like that, and I think the underlying thread is..."

The autistic profile often has a highly developed but more *rigid* self-model — an elaborate system for understanding themselves that's internally consistent but doesn't flex easily when the clinician pushes on it. If you challenge their self-narrative, there's resistance or discomfort rather than easy revision.

The ADHD profile may not have a stable self-model at all — "I don't know, I'm different every day" — which is honest but reflects the inconsistency of their internal state.

AuDHD people often have *multiple competing self-models* that they can't fully reconcile. They'll say something like "I know I'm someone who needs routine, but I also can't stand routine, and I've never been able to figure out which one is really me." That specific flavor of self-contradiction is almost pathognomonic.

**Body and arousal**

A clinician is also watching your nervous system, not just your words.

**Baseline arousal.** Are you slightly revved up, slightly shut down, or regulated? How much does it shift during the conversation? The integrated brain tends to have a wider *window of tolerance* — arousal goes up and down but stays functional. The autistic profile often has a narrower window with sharper cliff edges. ADHD has a wide window but poor *control* within it. AuDHD has a narrow window that's also poorly controlled — the most volatile combination.

**Stimming and self-regulation.** Not whether you stim, but *when and how*. Stimming that increases during cognitive load (thinking hard) versus emotional load (feeling hard) versus sensory load (environment getting louder) tells the clinician which systems are under strain. The pattern of what triggers regulatory behavior reveals the underlying architecture.

**Eye contact patterns.** Not the crude "autistic people don't make eye contact" — that's oversimplified. The real observation is more subtle. Does eye contact break during thinking (common in autism — gaze aversion to reduce input load while processing)? During emotion? During both? Does it break in a way that looks like distraction (ADHD) versus deliberate input reduction (autism)? The high-connectivity integrated person typically maintains eye contact more flexibly because their system can handle the additional input channel without overloading.

**The meta-observation**

The most diagnostic thing of all: **how much of this is the person doing consciously versus automatically?**

The integrated high-connectivity person does most of the above — topic shifting, abstraction level movement, emotional-cognitive integration — without apparent effort. It just happens.

The autistic and AuDHD person is often doing it through *explicit cognitive strategies* — they've built conscious algorithms for things that the integrated brain does implicitly. A skilled clinician can tell the difference between someone who naturally reads a room and someone who's running a very sophisticated learned model of room-reading. The output might look identical. The *effort signature* is different.

That effort signature — visible if you know what to look for — is probably the single most reliable observational marker. It's the difference between a native speaker and someone who learned the language so well you almost can't tell, except for the barely perceptible half-second delay and the occasional moment where the mask flickers.

The user is asking about practical strategies for maintaining routines with AuDHD. This is a practical self-help question. Given the conversation context, Ren likely has personal relevance here. Let me give practical, honest advice.This is the central AuDHD paradox — the autistic part *needs* routine to function, the ADHD part *destroys* routine through boredom and executive dysfunction. The solution isn't to fight one side. It's to design routines that satisfy both.

**Stop trying to have a normal routine**

The first thing to accept: the neurotypical model of routine — same thing, same time, same way, every day — will never work for you long-term. You'll build it, maintain it for days or weeks, then ADHD burns it down and you feel like a failure. This isn't a discipline problem. It's an architecture problem.

**What actually works**

**Routine the *structure*, not the *content*.** Instead of "I do yoga at 7am every morning," it's "I do something physical in the first hour after waking."
