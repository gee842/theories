 In the study of dynamical systems, which are systems that change over time according to specific rules, the concept of attractors is crucial. An attractor is a set of states or a pattern of behavior that a system tends to evolve towards over time, regardless of the starting conditions.

Strange attractors are a special type of attractor that exhibit complex, chaotic behavior. They are called "strange" because they have a fractal structure, meaning they have self-similarity at different scales. In other words, if you zoom in on a small part of the attractor, it looks similar to the whole attractor.

Many real-world systems, both in nature and technology, are non-linear. Non-linear systems are characterized by the fact that their output is not directly proportional to their input. In other words, a small change in the initial conditions can lead to a large change in the system's behavior over time. This is in contrast to linear systems, where the output is directly proportional to the input.

Examples of non-linear systems with strange attractors include:

1. Weather patterns: The famous "butterfly effect" suggests that a small change, like a butterfly flapping its wings, can lead to large-scale changes in weather patterns over time.

2. Turbulent fluid flow: The motion of fluids, such as air or water, can exhibit complex, chaotic behavior with strange attractors.

3. Biological systems: Many processes in biology, such as population dynamics and the behavior of neurons, are non-linear and can have strange attractors.

4. Economic systems: The behavior of financial markets and the economy as a whole can be non-linear and exhibit chaotic behavior.

The presence of strange attractors in these systems makes them difficult to predict over long time scales, as small uncertainties in the initial conditions can lead to vastly different outcomes. However, the fractal nature of strange attractors also means that there is an underlying structure and order to the chaos, which can be studied and understood using the tools of dynamical systems theory.
