## Part One: The 12 Core Values

These 12 values were formally enshrined by the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) in 2012 under Xi Jinping's leadership and are organized into three tiers, each addressing a different scale of social life.

---

### National Level (国家层面)

**1. Prosperity (富强 — Fùqiáng)**
This refers to national wealth and military strength. It draws on a deeply rooted historical narrative — the "Century of Humiliation" (1839–1949) — during which China suffered colonialism, invasion, and internal collapse. The value signals that China's primary national mission is to never be weak again. It justifies state-led economic development, technological self-reliance, and military modernization as existential priorities rather than mere policy choices.

**2. Democracy (民主 — Mínzhǔ)**
This is not democracy in the Western liberal sense of multiparty elections and individual rights. The CCP defines it as "whole-process people's democracy" (全过程人民民主), meaning the Party claims to represent the people's interests through consultation, feedback mechanisms, and centralized decision-making. The inclusion of this term serves a dual purpose: it legitimizes the CCP's governance model domestically while countering Western criticism that China lacks democratic institutions.

**3. Civility (文明 — Wénmíng)**
This term carries more weight in Chinese than its English translation suggests. It encompasses cultural sophistication, moral advancement, and the idea that China is an inherently civilized society with thousands of years of continuous history. It implicitly positions China as an equal or superior civilization to the West, and is tied to campaigns promoting public manners, cultural refinement, and "spiritual civilization" (精神文明).

**4. Harmony (和谐 — Héxié)**
Rooted in Confucian philosophy, harmony emphasizes social stability, balance, and the avoidance of conflict. Politically, it became a signature concept under Hu Jintao's "Harmonious Society" (和谐社会) initiative. In practice, it has been used to justify the suppression of dissent, protest, and social movements under the logic that disruption threatens collective well-being. The word itself has become so politically loaded that Chinese internet users turned it into a verb — "to be harmonized" (被和谐) — meaning to be censored.

---

### Societal Level (社会层面)

**5. Freedom (自由 — Zìyóu)**
Freedom here is understood collectively rather than individually. It does not imply freedom of speech, press, or assembly in the liberal tradition. Instead, it refers to national sovereignty and the freedom of the Chinese people as a collective from foreign domination. Individual freedoms exist within boundaries set by the state and are subordinated to social stability and national interest.

**6. Equality (平等 — Píngděng)**
Equality is framed primarily as economic and social equity — reducing the gap between rich and poor, between urban and rural areas, and between regions. It draws on Marxist ideals but is not interpreted as political equality in the sense of equal political rights or one-person-one-vote. The concept supports policies like poverty alleviation campaigns while coexisting with significant hierarchies in Party, state, and social structures.

**7. Justice (公正 — Gōngzhèng)**
Justice refers to fairness in governance and social systems. It supports anti-corruption campaigns (such as Xi Jinping's sweeping purges since 2012) and the idea that officials should serve the people rather than enrich themselves. However, it operates within a legal system where the Party sits above the law, and judicial independence is explicitly rejected as a Western concept. Justice is therefore Party-mediated justice.

**8. Rule of Law (法治 — Fǎzhì)**
This is one of the most contested values. In Western jurisprudence, rule of law means that laws constrain even the government. In the Chinese context, rule of law means governance through law — the state uses legal systems as a tool of administration, but the Party remains the ultimate authority above the legal framework. Laws serve to regulate society and punish wrongdoing, but they do not function as a check on Party power.

---

### Individual Level (个人层面)

**9. Patriotism (爱国 — Àiguó)**
Patriotism in this context is inseparable from loyalty to the CCP. The Party narrative equates love of country with support for the Party, such that criticism of the government can be framed as unpatriotic. This value is reinforced through education, media, national holidays, military parades, and increasingly through nationalist internet culture. It provides emotional fuel for foreign policy positions on Taiwan, Hong Kong, the South China Sea, and responses to Western criticism.

**10. Dedication (敬业 — Jìngyè)**
This value promotes diligence, professionalism, and devotion to one's work. It carries echoes of both traditional Confucian duty and socialist labor ethics. It supports the idea that individuals contribute to national development through hard work and self-sacrifice. It has been invoked in discussions around China's intense work culture, including the controversial "996" schedule (9am–9pm, 6 days a week), framing overwork as a form of patriotic contribution.

**11. Integrity (诚信 — Chéngxìn)**
Integrity encompasses honesty, trustworthiness, and good faith in personal and commercial dealings. It has been elevated partly in response to widespread social anxieties about fraud, food safety scandals, academic dishonesty, and corruption. It also provides the moral foundation for the Social Credit System, which aims to quantify and incentivize trustworthy behavior through rewards and punishments.

**12. Friendliness (友善 — Yǒushàn)**
Friendliness promotes interpersonal kindness, neighborliness, and social cooperation. It is the most seemingly apolitical of the values, yet it reinforces the broader theme of social harmony — the idea that good citizens are those who get along, avoid conflict, and contribute to a stable community. It subtly discourages confrontation, complaint, and public expressions of grievance.

---

## Part Two: The Related Political Frameworks

Beyond the 12 values, several doctrinal frameworks reinforce them:

**The Four Principles (四项基本原则):** Adherence to the socialist path, the people's democratic dictatorship, CCP leadership, and Marxism-Leninism-Mao Zedong Thought. These are non-negotiable red lines that define the boundaries of permissible political discourse.

**The Four Confidences (四个自信):** Confidence in China's path, theory, system, and culture. This framework asserts that China's development model is not a temporary expedient but a legitimate civilizational alternative to the Western liberal order.

**The Four Comprehensives (四个全面):** Building a moderately prosperous society, deepening reform, governing by law, and strict Party discipline. These are more operational goals that translate ideology into policy.

---

## Part Three: How These Values Discipline Contemporary Life

### 1. The Architecture of Omnipresence

The values are not abstract principles stored in government archives. They are physically and digitally ubiquitous. You encounter them on subway advertisements, highway billboards, apartment complex walls, restaurant menus, children's textbooks, mobile apps, and even in popular entertainment. The state-funded "Learning Power" (学习强国) app gamifies political study, awarding points for reading Party documents and watching propaganda videos — points that in some workplaces affect performance evaluations.

This omnipresence creates what might be called an **ambient ideology**. Most people don't actively engage with the content, but it establishes the default vocabulary through which public life is narrated. When a local government explains a policy, when a school principal gives a speech, when a company writes its mission statement — the 12 values provide the ready-made language.

### 2. Education as the Primary Disciplinary Institution

The Chinese education system is the most powerful vehicle for internalizing these values. From primary school through university, students take mandatory "thought and political" (思政) courses. The gaokao (college entrance exam) and postgraduate exams include political theory sections that require memorization and correct reproduction of Party doctrines.

This creates a specific form of discipline: students may not personally believe in these frameworks, but they must demonstrate mastery of them to advance in life. The result is what scholars sometimes call **performative compliance** — people become fluent in the official language not out of conviction but out of necessity. Over time, however, the boundary between performance and belief can blur. When you have spent 16 years learning to articulate the world in a specific vocabulary, that vocabulary inevitably shapes how you think, even if you consciously resist it.

### 3. The Internalization of Self-Censorship

Perhaps the most profound disciplinary effect is not external censorship but **internalized self-censorship**. The values and their surrounding political frameworks define what is sayable, thinkable, and publishable. People learn — through observation of others being punished, through subtle social cues, through platform algorithms that suppress certain content — where the boundaries are.

This produces a phenomenon where millions of people simultaneously know the boundaries and pretend they don't exist. A journalist knows which stories won't be approved. An academic knows which research topics invite trouble. A social media user knows which words trigger automated deletion. This knowledge doesn't need to be explicitly taught — it is absorbed through repeated experience, creating what the Chinese internet calls the "invisible wall" (隐形的墙).

### 4. Nationalism as Emotional Infrastructure

Patriotism, combined with the Four Confidences and the narrative of national rejuvenation, has created a powerful emotional infrastructure. The "Century of Humiliation" narrative is taught from childhood and provides a framework through which Chinese citizens interpret international relations: the West humiliated China, China is rising again, and any foreign criticism is a continuation of historical aggression.

This emotional framework disciplines behavior in specific ways. Criticizing China's foreign policy can provoke intense social backlash. Consuming Japanese products during territorial disputes becomes a moral question. Supporting Hong Kong protesters or questioning the official position on Taiwan can result in social ostracism, job loss, or legal consequences. The discipline here operates not only through state mechanisms but through **peer enforcement** — ordinary citizens police each other's patriotism.

### 5. The Social Credit System as Quantified Morality

The values of integrity and rule of law find their most concrete expression in the emerging Social Credit System. While the system is more fragmented and less dystopian than Western media often portrays, its conceptual ambition is remarkable: to create a comprehensive behavioral scoring mechanism that rewards "trustworthy" behavior and punishes "untrustworthy" behavior.

This represents a shift from moral exhortation to **algorithmic governance**. The 12 values are no longer just slogans — they become measurable, enforceable standards. Pay your debts on time, follow traffic rules, maintain good social relationships, and your score rises. Default on loans, jaywalk, or spread "rumors" online, and your score drops, potentially affecting your ability to buy plane tickets, secure loans, or enroll your children in good schools.

### 6. The Paradox of "Harmony" and Suppressed Grievance

The emphasis on harmony and friendliness creates a cultural expectation that conflicts should be resolved quietly, complaints should be restrained, and public displays of dissatisfaction are socially unacceptable. This produces a paradox: a society with genuine grievances (inequality, pollution, corruption, labor exploitation) that lacks legitimate channels for expressing them.

The result is a pattern of **punctuated eruptions** — long periods of apparent stability interrupted by sudden outbursts. The 2022 "White Paper" protests against COVID lockdowns were a dramatic example: after years of compliance, people took to the streets holding blank sheets of paper — a powerful symbol of everything they were not allowed to say. The very emptiness of the paper was a commentary on the disciplinary system itself.

### 7. Generational Fractures

The disciplinary effects are experienced very differently across generations. Older generations who lived through the Cultural Revolution and Reform era may genuinely believe in the values as a stabilizing force. The middle generation, who benefited from economic growth, often practice pragmatic acceptance — they don't deeply believe but see stability as worthwhile.

The youngest generation, however, shows signs of deep ambivalence. Movements like "lying flat" (躺平 — rejecting the pressure to work, marry, and consume) and "let it rot" (摆烂 — giving up on upward mobility) represent a passive rejection of the values of dedication and prosperity. The "run philosophy" (润学 — strategizing about emigration) suggests that some young people have concluded that the system cannot be reformed from within. These are not organized political movements — they are diffuse, individual acts of withdrawal that collectively signal a crisis of the disciplinary model's legitimacy among those it most needs to convince.

### 8. The Digital Panopticon

Finally, all of the above operates within an unprecedented infrastructure of digital surveillance. The Great Firewall controls what information enters the country. Platform algorithms on WeChat, Weibo, Douyin, and others filter and suppress content in real time. Facial recognition cameras blanket public spaces. Phone apps track location data.

This creates what Foucault would recognize as a modern **panopticon** — a system where the possibility of being watched is sufficient to alter behavior, regardless of whether actual watching occurs at any given moment. The discipline is not primarily about punishment (though punishment exists) but about the **anticipation** of punishment, which transforms external control into internal habit.

---

## A Final Complexity

It would be a mistake to conclude that all of this produces a uniformly obedient population. Human beings are remarkably creative at finding spaces of autonomy within even the most disciplinary systems. Chinese citizens engage in subtle humor, coded language, commercial consumption as identity expression, private dissent, and selective compliance. The system disciplines, but it does not totally determine.

What it does do is set the **terms of engagement** — the vocabulary, the boundaries, the risks, and the rewards within which 1.4 billion people navigate their daily lives. Understanding these values is therefore not just an exercise in political science but a key to understanding how contemporary Chinese society actually functions at the level of lived experience.
