Déjacque viewed the state, religion, and patriarchy not as separate entities, but as interconnected systems that worked together to maintain oppression and inequality. His analysis was holistic, recognizing that these institutions reinforced each other and needed to be addressed collectively rather than in isolation.

1. Critique of the State:
Déjacque, like many anarchists, saw the state as an inherently oppressive institution. He argued that the very existence of a state, regardless of its form, inevitably led to the concentration of power in the hands of a few, resulting in the exploitation and domination of the many. 

He criticized not only overtly authoritarian governments but also more liberal forms of democracy, arguing that even representative democracy was a form of tyranny. In his view, the act of delegating decision-making power to representatives was itself a surrender of individual liberty.

Déjacque believed that the state's primary function was to protect the interests of the wealthy and powerful, maintaining social hierarchies and economic inequality. He saw laws and legal systems not as protectors of justice, but as tools to legitimize and enforce oppression.

2. Critique of Religion:
Déjacque was deeply critical of organized religion, which he saw as a tool for mental and social control. He argued that religious institutions worked hand in hand with the state to keep people subservient and accepting of their oppression.

In his view, religion promoted a worldview that justified hierarchies and inequalities as natural or divinely ordained. He saw religious doctrines as promoting passivity and acceptance of suffering in this life, with the promise of rewards in an afterlife.

Moreover, Déjacque criticized the patriarchal structures within most religious institutions, seeing them as reinforcing gender inequalities in society at large.

3. Critique of Patriarchy:
Déjacque's critique of patriarchy was particularly advanced for his time. He recognized patriarchy as a system of oppression that went beyond just the subjugation of women, seeing it as a hierarchical principle that permeated all aspects of society.

He criticized the institution of marriage as a form of ownership of women by men, and advocated for complete equality between genders. Déjacque even criticized fellow anarchist thinkers like Proudhon for not extending their principles of liberty and equality to gender relations.

In Déjacque's utopian vision, traditional family structures would be abolished, replaced by free associations between individuals. Child-rearing would be a communal responsibility, eliminating one of the primary justifications for patriarchal family structures.

4. Interconnection of These Systems:
What sets Déjacque's analysis apart is his understanding of how these systems work together. He saw that the state often used religious justifications for its authority, that religious institutions often supported state power, and that both reinforced patriarchal norms.

For example, he recognized how patriarchal family structures served the interests of both the state and religious institutions by creating easily controllable social units. He saw how religious doctrines often justified state authority and patriarchal power structures.

Déjacque argued that attempts to reform any one of these systems in isolation would ultimately fail. In his view, true liberation required the complete dismantling of all these interconnected systems of oppression.

5. Vision for Dismantling:
Déjacque didn't just critique these systems; he also proposed their complete dismantling. He envisioned a society without any form of hierarchical authority, where individuals would freely associate based on mutual interest and need.

In place of the state, he proposed a system of voluntary associations and direct democracy. Instead of organized religion, he advocated for free inquiry and individual spiritual exploration. And in place of patriarchy, he envisioned complete gender equality and the abolition of traditional family structures.

Déjacque's radical vision called for a complete reimagining of social relations. He believed that only by entirely dismantling these interconnected systems of oppression could humanity achieve true freedom and equality.

In conclusion, Déjacque's critique of the existing social order was comprehensive and radical. He saw the state, religion, and patriarchy not as isolated problems, but as interconnected systems that needed to be addressed holistically.  Déjacque's argument against the state was rooted in his anarchist philosophy and his understanding of power dynamics. While I don't have access to the full text of his writings, I can explain the general anarchist argument that Déjacque and others have made against the state. Here's a breakdown of the key points:

1. Inherent Power Imbalance:
Déjacque argued that the very structure of a state creates an inherent power imbalance. By its nature, a state concentrates decision-making power and the legitimate use of force in the hands of a select few individuals or institutions.

2. Corruption of Power:
He believed in the old adage that "power corrupts, and absolute power corrupts absolutely." Even if those who initially gain power have good intentions, the very possession of that power tends to corrupt them over time.

3. Self-Perpetuation:
Déjacque likely argued that once established, states tend to self-perpetuate and expand their power. Those in power have a vested interest in maintaining and increasing their authority, leading to a gradual erosion of individual liberties.

4. False Representation:
Even in democratic states, Déjacque would argue that the idea of representation is flawed. No elected official can truly represent the diverse interests and desires of all their constituents, leading to decisions that benefit some at the expense of others.

5. Monopoly on Violence:
The state's monopoly on the legitimate use of force was seen as a fundamental problem. This monopoly allows the state to enforce its will on the population, even when that will goes against the interests or desires of many individuals.

6. Protection of Elite Interests:
Déjacque likely argued that the state, regardless of its form, tends to protect the interests of economic and social elites. Even ostensibly democratic states can be captured by wealthy interests through lobbying, campaign financing, and other forms of influence.

7. Suppression of Individual Liberty:
The very existence of a state, with its laws and enforcement mechanisms, was seen as an infringement on individual liberty. Every law, no matter how well-intentioned, is ultimately a restriction on individual freedom.

8. Creation of Artificial Divisions:
States create artificial boundaries and divisions among people, fostering nationalism and conflict between different state entities.

9. Inefficiency and Bureaucracy:
The centralization of power in a state leads to inefficiencies, with decisions being made far from the people they affect and bureaucracies growing ever larger and more cumbersome.

10. Stifling of Natural Social Organization:
Déjacque likely argued that without the state, people would naturally organize themselves in more equitable and efficient ways based on mutual aid and voluntary association.

In Déjacque's view, these factors combined to make any form of state inherently oppressive and exploitative. He saw the concentration of power as inevitably leading to the domination of the many by the few, regardless of the specific form that state power took.

It's important to note that while these arguments were radical in Déjacque's time, they continue to be debated in political philosophy today. Many would argue that states can be structured to mitigate these issues, while others continue to advocate for anarchist alternatives.
