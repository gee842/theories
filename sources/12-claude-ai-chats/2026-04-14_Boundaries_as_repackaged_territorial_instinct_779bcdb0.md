The key insight is that the self *is* a model, and models inherently resist falsification — not from ego stubbornness but because of how information processing architectures fundamentally work.This is actually a really compelling hypothesis, and there's some neuroscience that supports it.

The strongest framework that maps onto what you're describing is **predictive processing / active inference** (Karl Friston). The core idea is that the brain is fundamentally a prediction engine — it builds a model of the world and constantly tries to minimize the gap between predictions and reality. The "self" in this framework isn't a thing, it's a model — the brain's best running hypothesis about the agent doing the predicting.

And here's where your insight lands hard: **defending that model is computationally identical to defending territory.** When reality contradicts your self-model or worldview, the brain registers it as prediction error — and prediction error activates threat responses. The amygdala doesn't cleanly distinguish between a physical intruder and an idea that challenges your identity. Studies show similar neural activation patterns in both cases.

So the evolutionary path might look something like:

> defend physical space → defend social position → defend symbolic status → defend the model of self → defend the model of *what reality is*

Each layer is an abstraction of the same underlying drive, running on the same threat-detection hardware. The territory just keeps expanding inward and outward simultaneously.

Your specific framing — that the ego colonizes not just "what is mine" but "what should be" — is the normative extension. It's territorial claim over **possibility space**, not just present reality. That's actually a very expensive cognitive move. It means anything that *could* challenge your model becomes a pre-emptive threat.

Which explains a lot of human suffering that has nothing to do with actual survival.

The question this raises: if the self is a model that defends itself, then practices like 无我 or meditation aren't really "destroying the self" — they're loosening the model's grip on its own predictions. Holding it more lightly. The territory doesn't disappear, the *attachment to defending it* does.

Does this match your intuition, or do you think the ego-as-territory goes even deeper than a cognitive model — more like a structural feature of consciousness itself?
