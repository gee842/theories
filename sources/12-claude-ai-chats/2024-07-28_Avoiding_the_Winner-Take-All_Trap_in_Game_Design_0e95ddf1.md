Let's break this down:

1. The problem: When game winners receive rewards that give them further advantages within the game itself, it can create a "rich get richer" effect.

2. The consequence: Over time, this can lead to a situation where the early winners accumulate so many advantages that it becomes nearly impossible for others to catch up or compete effectively.

3. The end result: As you noted, this can cause the game to "converge," with one player or a small group of players dominating completely - the "winner takes all" scenario.

This principle applies to many contexts beyond just games:

- Economic systems: Wealth concentration can lead to similar effects in real-world economies.
- Competitive markets: Companies that gain early advantages can sometimes create near-monopolies.
- Social networks: Popular users gaining more visibility can lead to outsized influence.

To address this issue in game design, some strategies include:

- Offering non-gameplay rewards (e.g., cosmetic items, titles, or out-of-game perks)
- Implementing "catchup" mechanics for players who are behind
- Resetting certain aspects of the game periodically
- Designing reward systems that don't directly impact gameplay power

Your observation highlights the importance of careful balancing in game design to maintain long-term engagement and fairness. It's a principle that game designers, economists, and policymakers often grapple with in their respective fields.
