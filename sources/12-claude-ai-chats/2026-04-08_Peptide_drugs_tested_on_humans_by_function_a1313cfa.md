The honest answer is: we understand them unevenly, and our understanding is far from systematic in a unified sense.

**What we understand well:**

The ~100 approved peptide drugs mostly have well-characterized mechanisms — we know their receptor targets, binding affinities, and pharmacokinetics. GLP-1 agonists like semaglutide are a good example: the signaling cascade from receptor binding through insulin secretion and appetite suppression is mapped in detail.

For peptides that mimic endogenous hormones (insulin, oxytocin, vasopressin, GLP-1/2), we understand them best because we're copying nature's own signaling molecules.

**What we don't understand well:**

- **Off-target effects and pleiotropy.** Semaglutide is the poster child — it was designed for diabetes, then found to affect obesity, cardiovascular risk, addiction, possibly neurodegeneration. We're discovering effects empirically faster than we can explain them mechanistically.

- **Antimicrobial peptides.** We know many work by disrupting membranes, but the selectivity mechanisms (why they kill bacteria but not host cells) are still debated. There's no unified model.

- **Structure-activity relationships at scale.** We can optimize individual peptides, but we lack a general predictive framework — "given this sequence, predict all biological effects." Peptide space is astronomically large and we've barely sampled it.

- **In vivo behavior.** Absorption, distribution, degradation by proteases, tissue-specific accumulation — these are still largely characterized empirically, drug by drug.

**The systematic gap:**

We have deep vertical knowledge (individual drugs, individual pathways) but weak horizontal integration. There's no unified "peptide pharmacology" the way we have, say, systematic organic chemistry. Each therapeutic area — oncology, metabolic, antimicrobial — has its own frameworks that don't talk to each other well.

AI/ML is starting to change this (AlphaFold for structure prediction, large-scale screening), but we're still in early innings. The field is closer to "curated catalog of things that work" than "systematic science that predicts what will work."

## The Actual Discovery Path: Not Design, But Chopping and Testing

The story starts **not in Russia, but in the Netherlands.** In the 1960s, David de Wied and colleagues at Utrecht University discovered that removing the pituitary gland in rats impaired their ability to learn avoidance tasks, and that ACTH could restore this ability. The key insight: ACTH fragments that had zero hormonal activity still affected learning and attention.

This was a chopping exercise, not a design exercise. Researchers took the full 39-amino-acid ACTH hormone and started cutting it into smaller pieces to see which fragment did what. The discovery of cognitive effects from these fragments was partly serendipitous — and led to the coining of the term "neuropeptide" at a time when nobody had even proven neurons could produce peptides.

By the 1970s, de Wied's group had mapped out that ACTH 4–10 (a 7-amino-acid fragment) retained cognitive effects while being completely stripped of hormonal activity. They even developed their own commercial analog, **ORG 2766**, which was 1,000 times more potent than ACTH 4–10 in animal tests.

**Then the Dutch work mostly stalled.** Clinical trials in cognitively impaired elderly showed mixed results — improvements that were dose-dependent and baseline-dependent, but not the blockbuster outcomes pharma needed. One study in healthy young adults found ACTH 4–10 actually *impaired* performance on complex attention tasks, suggesting it "may act on arousal processes which energize behavior unselectively rather than result in focusing of attention." Organon (the Dutch pharma company) eventually abandoned the program.

## What the Soviets Did Differently

In the late 1970s, the Soviet Ministry of Defense and Academy of Medical Sciences tasked researchers with developing nootropics for extreme conditions. The work was based directly on de Wied's Dutch research from the 1970s.

The Russian contribution was a specific engineering fix: the natural ACTH 4–10 fragment had a half-life of only 30–60 minutes, and increasing the dose didn't help. The Russians added a **Pro-Gly-Pro tripeptide** to the C-terminus, which dramatically increased metabolic stability. That's Semax.

So the design was: take a known active fragment (discovered by others) → solve the stability problem (add PGP tail) → test it. The prolonged activity comes partly from the fact that Semax's degradation products themselves retain neurotropic activity — so it's not one drug but a cascade of active fragments.

For Selank, same pattern: take a known active molecule (tuftsin, an immunomodulatory peptide) → add the same PGP tail for stability → discover it has anxiolytic properties.

The mechanism is "unknown" in the way that **aspirin's mechanism was unknown for 70 years** after it was first used. The empirical observation came first. The molecular explanation came later (and is still incomplete).

- The Dutch showed ACTH fragments affect cognition → **empirical observation, not mechanistic understanding**
- The Russians stabilized the fragment → **engineering, not mechanistic understanding**
- BDNF upregulation was discovered later → **partial mechanism found after the fact**
- Melanocortin receptor interactions were identified even later → **still being mapped**
