  The user is asking about the noradrenergic system's connection to ADHD, and given their background, they'll want a substantive answer grounded in evidence. The locus coeruleus-norepinephrine pathway is indeed directly implicated in ADHD, with pharmacological evidence being particularly compelling—I should focus on that mechanism first.

The strongest evidence comes from ADHD medications: atomoxetine selectively blocks norepinephrine reuptake, while stimulants increase both dopamine and norepinephrine in the prefrontal cortex, and alpha-2A agonists like guanfacine and clonidine work by enhancing noradrenergic signaling there. Amy Arnsten's research shows that the prefrontal cortex requires an optimal level of norepinephrine for working memory and top-down attention—too little or too much impairs function. The dual-mode locus coeruleus theory from Aston-Jones and Cohen suggests ADHD involves dysregulated firing patterns, with elevated tonic activity driving distractibility and blunted phasic responses to relevant stimuli. This connects to the Yerkes-Dodson inverted-U curve the user mentioned, which is fundamentally a catecholamine-dependent phenomenon in prefrontal function.

That said, dopamine is equally important—ADHD involves both systems, especially dopamine's role in motivation and reward through the ventral tegmental area and striatum. But norepinephrine appears more central to the attention and executive control deficits than typically emphasized, which is why the user's framing about tonic versus phasic dynamics is spot-on.Yes, very directly. The LC-NE system is one of the most clearly implicated circuits in ADHD, arguably as central as dopamine.

The strongest evidence is pharmacological. Atomoxetine is a selective norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor and is FDA-approved for ADHD. Guanfacine and clonidine are alpha-2A adrenergic agonists that work postsynaptically in the PFC and are also approved. Stimulants like methylphenidate and amphetamines raise both DA and NE in prefrontal cortex — the NE component isn't a side effect, it's part of the therapeutic mechanism. So three of the main medication classes for ADHD are essentially titrating the LC-NE system.

Mechanistically, Amy Arnsten's work shows that PFC network connectivity depends on alpha-2A receptor stimulation by NE. Too little NE and pyramidal cells can't sustain persistent firing for working memory and top-down control; too much (stress-level NE acting on alpha-1 and beta receptors) and PFC goes offline, ceding control to more reflexive striatal/amygdalar circuits. This is the inverted-U you mentioned, and ADHD looks like a state shifted leftward on that curve — which is why stimulants are calming rather than activating in ADHD: they're moving people toward the optimum, not past it.

The Aston-Jones/Cohen tonic-vs-phasic framing maps onto ADHD phenomenology well too: dysregulated tonic LC tone (restless, scanning, distractible) plus impaired phasic responses to actually-relevant stimuli (failure to register the salient signal in the noise). 

ADHD isn't *only* an NE disorder — DA in striatal/reward circuits is equally important, especially for motivation and delay aversion — but the NE system is genuinely a primary axis, not a side player.
