# Moloch

Allen Ginsberg saw him first, in 1955, through benzedrine haze: *Moloch whose mind is pure machinery! Moloch whose blood is running money!* The Canaanite god of child sacrifice had been repurposed as the presiding deity of industrial modernity — not a devil you could name and cast out, but the logic of the system itself, eating its children by running correctly.

Scott Alexander formalized the intuition in 2014. Moloch is what you get when rational individual action produces collectively insane outcomes. The arms race where both sides would prefer to stop but neither can. The fishery where every fisherman knows overfishing will collapse the stock, and catches as much as they can anyway. The social platform that cannot stop optimizing for outrage because the one that tries will lose to the one that does not. Moloch is not malice. Moloch is the shape of multipolar traps, the god whose sacraments are defection and race-to-the-bottom, and whose only demand is that you keep competing.

The old consolation about Moloch was that he was local. A given tragedy of the commons could be bounded by a regulator, a treaty, a cultural norm. The cod bank could be closed. The CFCs could be banned. Moloch won a lot of hands but not all of them, because human coordination — slow, imperfect, incomplete — sometimes got there in time. The twentieth century was a running tally of Molochs contained, Molochs escaped, Molochs half-fought-to-a-draw.

What is qualitatively new in this decade is that the Molochs have begun to compound.

Consider the loop. An attention economy optimized for engagement produces populations with degraded epistemic capacity and shortened time horizons. A population with degraded epistemic capacity cannot sustain the deliberative institutions required to regulate an attention economy. So the attention economy strengthens. Now layer AI on top: generative systems lower the cost of producing engagement bait by orders of magnitude, which starves human-produced signal of oxygen, which further degrades the epistemic commons. At the same time, the AI race itself is a pure Moloch — any lab that unilaterally slows for safety is outcompeted by one that does not, and the coordination mechanism that might bind them (national regulation, international treaty) is made impotent by the very attention-collapse the previous Molochs produced. The loop closes. Each Moloch makes the next one harder to escape.

This is what winner-takes-all dynamics do at scale. In a market with strong network effects — compute, capital, attention, talent — the payoff curve is not a gentle slope but a cliff. First place takes eighty percent, second place takes fifteen, and everyone else divides the remainder. When the payoff curve is that steep, defection from any coordination attempt becomes overwhelming. You cannot ask a lab to surrender a hundred-billion-dollar position voluntarily. You cannot ask a platform to give up its engagement moat. You cannot ask a nation to decline the decisive military technology. Each actor knows, with perfect clarity, that their restraint buys them nothing except the loss.

And so the machine accelerates. The doom in question is not necessarily extinction, though extinction is on the table. It is closer to what Bostrom called a Disneyland without children: a universe running at full efficiency toward no one's actual values, optimizing a proxy past the point where the proxy still tracks the thing it was meant to proxy. A world where every quarter's metrics go up and every decade's measure of human flourishing goes down, and no one chose this, and no one can stop it, because to stop would be to lose, and to lose would be to be replaced by something that did not stop. Moloch does not require you to believe in him. He requires only that the dominant strategy exist and that someone, somewhere, be willing to play it.

The honest question is: what kills Moloch?

Alexander's answer, and it is the right one, is: a stronger coordinator. Something with enough scope and enough enforcement power to make cooperation the dominant strategy rather than the sucker's move. Historically this has been the state, occasionally the supranational treaty, sometimes — rarely — a cultural consensus strong enough to price defection out of the market. None of these look sufficient to the current Molochs. The state is hollowed. Treaties move in years while the frontier moves in months. Cultural consensus is precisely the commons that the attention economy has strip-mined.

Which leaves a narrower, more difficult answer. Moloch's power is conditional on the belief that defection pays. Whenever a critical mass of players becomes convinced, even briefly, that cooperation is the winning move — because the cost of continued defection has become visible and unbearable, because a credible enforcement mechanism has appeared, because the game itself has been reframed — Moloch loses that round. The Montreal Protocol on CFCs happened. Nuclear weapons have not been used in war since 1945. These are small victories against a god who takes every hand he is allowed to take, but they are victories, and they demonstrate that the outcome is not written.

What this means, for a person living inside the compound, is unglamorous. You do not defeat Moloch with a manifesto or a single institution. You defeat him, to the extent that he is defeated, by building the preconditions for coordination wherever they can still be built: institutions that can hold a rule, communities that can hold a norm, information environments in which truth still has a friction advantage over its cheap imitations, and — most importantly — enough slack in your own life and attention that you remain capable of acting on something other than the immediate competitive gradient. Moloch feeds on the removal of slack. Every minute of compressed attention, every quarter of maximized extraction, every unit of redundancy surrendered to efficiency, is tribute paid to him.

The end is not foreordained, but the current trajectory is not survivable either. The essay one can honestly write in 2026 is not a hopeful one. It is this: the dynamics are real, the doom logic is coherent, and the only available move is to refuse, in the specific places where refusal is still possible, to feed the god.
