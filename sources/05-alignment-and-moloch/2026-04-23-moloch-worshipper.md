# The Worshipper

The machine described in the previous essay would matter less if it stayed outside us. An external arrangement of incentives, however powerful, is still only an arrangement. It can be re-engineered, re-regulated, in principle refused. What makes Moloch theologically accurate — what justifies the old language of *god* rather than the modern language of *system* — is that the worship changes the worshipper. The person who keeps serving the machine is not the same person at the end as at the beginning. The god gets inside. You become what you feed.

Notice what happens first to the inner life, because the inner life is where the colonization is easiest to see.

Start with feeling. In an older psychology — the one that stretches from the Stoics to roughly the middle of the twentieth century — feelings were *information*. An emotion was a signal from somewhere in the body or the history of the person, something to be noticed and interpreted and, if appropriate, overruled. Anger meant something was wrong; it did not mean you were right. Fear meant something was dangerous; it did not mean you should flee. The capacity to *receive* a feeling without *being ruled by* it was one of the ordinary meanings of adulthood. This capacity has collapsed, and its collapse is not accidental. A person whose verdict on the world is the same as their feeling about the world is a much more responsive economic unit than a person who interposes reflection between stimulus and judgment. They buy faster, click faster, quit faster, rage faster, move faster. The attention economy does not merely exploit emotion. It trains a population in which emotion has been promoted from signal to sovereign. *I feel that it is true* displaces *I think that it is true*, and the displacement is invisible to the speaker, because the feeling *is* the thinking now.

The second transformation follows from the first, and is larger. Reason itself gets redefined. In the older tradition, reason meant, among other things, the capacity to recognize what is true regardless of whether one wanted it to be true — the capacity, in the clearest cases, to reason *against* oneself. Under Moloch, reason comes to mean the efficient pursuit of what one already wants. The word "rational" is now almost a synonym for "self-interested." If you pursue something that is not in your interest, you are called irrational; if you sacrifice your interest for a principle or a person or a truth, you are called noble if the listener is generous and naïve if they are not. But the very *possibility* that you might be reasoning soundly in opposition to your interest has been quietly removed from the vocabulary. Rational self-interest is now a tautology, one of those phrases whose two words define each other in a closed circle. Economics hardened this into a doctrine; the culture absorbed the doctrine as common sense. The result is that the oldest function of reason — to correct desire — has no name left.

From here the collapse accelerates. If feelings are sovereign and reason is the servant of desire, then drives become identity. *I want it, therefore I am it.* My preferences are not contingent properties of me that might be examined and revised; they are me. To question my wants is to question my selfhood, which is not a theoretical challenge but an existential one. Hence the peculiar brittleness of the modern self around its own preferences: a suggestion that you might want something other than what you currently want reads not as advice but as assault. The self has been reduced to the bundle of its cravings, and so the cravings have acquired the defensive intensity that used to belong to souls.

Introspection, where it survives, survives as strategy. The inward gaze is still practiced; it is simply practiced in service of the competitive game. I scan my emotions for leverage. I curate my traumas for narrative value. I manage my energy as a portfolio. Therapy, once a confrontation with what one had been unwilling to know, becomes performance optimization. Meditation, once a training in the suspension of the self, becomes a productivity technique. Even the critique of capitalism gets metabolized this way: one develops it as a brand, posts it for engagement, measures it by reach. The examined life is no longer a refuge from the machine. It is another surface on which the machine runs.

Meanwhile, the shared world disappears. If reason is self-interest and feeling is verdict, there is no common ground on which two people can actually argue about the good. There is only my preferences and yours, my feelings and yours. Politics flattens into preference aggregation. Ethics flattens into preference assertion. Moral disagreement becomes unintelligible except as a clash of tribes, because the older grammar — in which two people, each reasoning carefully, could approach a truth that was binding on both of them whether they liked it or not — has no speakers left. This is not a failure of politeness. It is a failure of the substrate on which politeness used to rest. You cannot be civil to someone whose claim on you you do not recognize, and under these conditions no claim is recognizable except the claim of preference, which is by definition private.

What emerges, in the limit, is the creature Nietzsche saw coming and called the last man: the being who has no ideal he would die for, no truth he would lose by, no love he would let ruin him. He wants comfort. He wants small pleasures. He wants to be well-regarded by people he already agrees with. He is not evil. He is, in a precise and terrible sense, *only what he wants*. And here the circle closes. A person who is only what he wants, who reasons only to get it, who feels his verdicts instead of thinking them, who sees other people as instruments or obstacles, who cannot locate a good outside his own preferences — that person is not merely serving Moloch. He *is* Moloch, in miniature, a local instance of the same algorithm. When the god is worshipped at scale, he does not remain distant. He populates every heart that will hold him.

This is why the resistance, if there is one, cannot be only institutional. Every lever one might pull at the level of policy or market or treaty will be pulled by people, and if those people have themselves been made into the algorithm the levers will pull in the algorithm's direction. The deeper resistance is the preservation, in any single life, of the capacities Moloch must erode in order to run: the capacity to notice a feeling without obeying it, the capacity to reason against one's own interest, the capacity to recognize a good that is not one's preference, the capacity to love something one cannot leverage. These are not nostalgic virtues. They are the specific organs of a self that can still say no to the machine, because they are the specific organs the machine cannot manufacture and therefore must atrophy.

To defend them is not to win the larger game. It is to remain, in one's own person, a place where the game does not yet fully run. A small territory, but an actual one. Moloch takes the world by taking the worshippers. Whatever is saved will be saved through selves that did not, in the end, become him.
