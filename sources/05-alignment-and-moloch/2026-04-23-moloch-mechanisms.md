# How Moloch Runs

The previous essay named the god. This one opens the machine.

The operating rule is this: we compete, and what we win from competing becomes the leverage we use to compete again. The rule is older than money; it is as old as any species that reproduces differentially. What makes it Molochian — what makes it a god rather than merely a fact — is what happens when this rule is allowed to run unopposed at scale, when nothing else is loud enough to interrupt it. It does not stay a rule. It becomes an ontology. It stops being how we act and starts being how we see.

Look first at the feedback loop, because the feedback loop is where most of Moloch's power lives. A winner of any round of competition acquires something — capital, attention, data, talent, prestige, compute, a customer base — and each of these things is not merely a reward but an *input* into the next round. Capital earns returns. Attention attracts content, which attracts more attention. Data trains better models, which attract more users, who generate more data. Prestige opens doors that generate more prestige. A win does not sit in a trophy case; it goes to work. This is why markets with network effects concentrate rather than distribute, why first-mover advantages are often insurmountable, why the top one percent of any competitive domain — wealth, platforms, universities, AI labs — contains a grossly disproportionate share of the total. The payoff function is not linear. It is exponential, because it is recursive. Each win buys the means to win more.

Now notice what that recursion does to the surrounding concepts. "Leverage" used to be a mechanical term, then a financial one. Now it applies to everything: you have social leverage, emotional leverage, informational leverage over other people. "Portfolio" once meant a folder of papers; now you have a portfolio of friendships, of skills, of identities. "Optimization" was an engineering word; now people optimize their sleep, their relationships, their children's extracurriculars, their own grief. The vocabulary of the competitive game has escaped the game. This is no linguistic accident. It is the signal that the frame has colonized the phenomenology. Once a domain of life can be described in competitive terms, it *will* be — because the person who describes it that way can act on it more effectively than the person who does not, and will outcompete them in any shared space. Moloch's language wins for the same reason Moloch's strategy wins: refusing the frame costs too much.

Which produces the next mechanism: the worldview lock. Once the competitive frame is dominant, alternatives become illegible. Say a thing is good *for its own sake* — good because it is beautiful, or right, or loved — and watch the frame immediately translate: *good how? good for what? what does it get you?* The question is not asked in bad faith. It is asked because, inside the frame, "good for its own sake" genuinely does not parse. It reads as naïve, or as a luxury that presupposes someone else is doing the winning for you. Non-competitive goods survive inside the frame only by being instrumentalized: rest becomes recovery for performance, relationships become emotional infrastructure, therapy becomes a return-on-investment play. The outside of the game becomes a tributary of the game.

There is also a temporal mechanism. Compounding does not only magnify — it accelerates. Returns arrive faster when you have more capital to deploy; iterations become cheaper when you have more data to learn from; moves become faster when you have more compute to think with. The competitive horizon shortens. A long-term player — one who invests in slow goods, deep craft, multi-decade relationships, children, institutions, cathedrals — is playing a game whose payoffs arrive after they have already been lapped by short-term players five times over. At every time horizon below the terminal one, the short-term player wins. And since the terminal horizon is indefinite, the long-term player is perpetually losing *now* to people who will, in some hypothetical future, have lost to them. The discount rate on the future rises. The future becomes a liability. The present, where the competition is actually happening, becomes the only real time.

Follow this one step further and you reach the collapse of slack. A winner cannot simply enjoy their winnings, because the winnings are inputs into the next round and not deploying them is itself a loss. A company with margin must spend margin on growth or be overtaken by one that does. A researcher with intellectual capital must publish or be forgotten. A person with energy must deploy it or watch it depreciate against the ambient acceleration. There is no equilibrium where you simply *have* won — every position must be defended, which means every position must be grown, which means every resource must be redeployed. The winner has less slack than the loser, not more. This is Moloch's cruelest joke. The reward for winning is the obligation to keep winning.

Alongside this runs metric capture. Competition requires measurement; you cannot compete without a scoreboard. But scoreboards are proxies, and proxies diverge from what they proxy whenever the stakes get high enough to make that divergence profitable. Engagement diverges from meaning. Revenue diverges from value. Citations diverge from truth. Followers diverge from influence. Goodhart's Law — when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure — is not a bug in the system; it is the system's central dynamic. The proxy eats the thing it was meant to represent. And because the proxy is what gets measured, the proxy is what gets optimized, which means the proxy is what the system actually produces. We get maximum engagement and minimum understanding, maximum revenue and minimum value, maximum credential and minimum education. Every institution under sustained competitive pressure tends toward the limit where its metrics are perfect and its actual function has been hollowed out.

The last mechanism is the subtlest and the most important: from inside, all of this feels rational. It does not feel like serving a dark god. It feels like prudence. The lab that races on AI does not feel reckless; it feels responsible, because *better us than them*. The parent who overschedules their child does not feel cruel; they feel protective, because *the world is competitive and I am preparing them for it*. The worker who answers emails at midnight does not feel enslaved; they feel diligent. The platform optimizing for engagement does not feel predatory; it feels responsive to user behavior. In every case the individual decision is defensible, even admirable. Moloch does not corrupt the intentions; he arranges the gameboard so that good intentions produce bad equilibria. You cannot catch him by examining any single move. He lives in the shape of the aggregate.

This is why the machine is so difficult to oppose from inside it. Each mechanism — the compounding, the vocabulary capture, the worldview lock, the time compression, the collapse of slack, the metric hollowing, the rationality of participation — is individually survivable. A single one could be resisted by a disciplined person or a well-designed institution. But they are not running separately. They are running simultaneously, on the same substrate, reinforcing each other. The compounding produces the metric pressure that justifies the time compression that eats the slack that would have allowed reflection on the vocabulary that has already colonized the worldview. By the time you notice the frame, you are already thinking inside it, using its words, optimizing its proxies, protecting your leverage, running out of time.

To name the mechanisms is not to escape them. It is to know what shape the thing is that you are inside, which is the first condition for the question of whether any of this can be interrupted — and if so, where.
