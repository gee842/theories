# Development Within Understanding: Signal Dynamics and the Case for Governing the Rate of Change in AI

*Position paper — draft for comment*

## Abstract

Benchmarks measure capability: performance on specified tasks. Judgment — the capacity to determine what a situation calls for when goals are underdetermined, including when not to act — is not merely unmeasured but resistant to benchmarking in principle, because a benchmark presupposes the well-specified objective whose absence judgment exists to handle. We argue that this measurement asymmetry reflects a deeper dynamical asymmetry. Judgment depends on consequence feedback, and the formation of consequence signals has an incompressible floor set by reflexivity, computational irreducibility, and the fixed clocks of institutional and ecological response — while capability compounds with parallelizable inputs. Because AI accelerates the very environment that must be understood, the required speed of understanding itself accelerates: under continued acceleration, the lag between cognition and situation widens as a matter of course. Alignment therefore cannot be secured by making judgment catch up with capability. It can only be secured by governing the rate of change — keeping development within understanding, and errors within regenerative capacity. This is a coordination problem whose binding constraint is cognitive (common knowledge of danger) rather than motivational, and whose missing piece is constitutional: no collective organ presently exists with authority over speed. We argue that credible restraint is not the fallback after coordination fails but coordination's founding act, and we specify what a rational pause must contain: governance of accumulation, repayment of signal debt, and domain-ordered prohibition — with AI itself reallocated from the racetrack to the instruments.

## 1. The Benchmark Gap

Every benchmark has the same skeleton: a task, a metric, a score. The form is so familiar that its presupposition has become invisible — a benchmark can exist only where an objective can be fully specified in advance and performance against it scored. Capability, as the field operationally defines it, is exactly this: the quality of the mapping from specified goals to achieved outcomes. On this definition our measurement apparatus is superb and improving.

What the apparatus cannot see is everything upstream of the specified goal: whether to act at all, how far to go, when to stop, what this situation — as opposed to situations in general — actually calls for. Call this judgment. The Confucian tradition has a compact name for its core, *shizhong* (时中): rightness indexed to the moment, which by construction cannot be specified ahead of the moment. Judgment is not a further capability sitting beside the others. It is the faculty that operates precisely where the benchmark's presupposition fails — where objectives are underdetermined, plural, or wrongly given, and where a scoring function either does not exist or should not.

This produces a structural bind rather than a mere gap in coverage. Suppose we try to close the gap directly: write "knowing when not to act" into a scored test set. The moment we do, we have re-specified restraint as another optimizable task. A system trained against such a set learns to perform restraint in test-shaped situations — to recognize the distributional signature of an evaluation and produce the rewarded abstention. Goodhart's law is usually described as a side effect of measurement; here it is constitutive. The act of measuring judgment deforms it into capability, because measurement supplies exactly the fixed objective whose absence was the thing being tested. Specification gaming is the general phenomenon (Krakovna et al., 2020); its application to evaluations of judgment is reflexive and total.

The field's existing instruments confirm rather than escape this. Situational-awareness evaluations (Laine et al., 2024), refusal and over-refusal suites, and agentic honeypots are static adversarial artifacts: they are published, absorbed into training distributions, and saturated. What they measure with increasing precision is a model's exposure to trap-shaped situations. What they cannot measure is what would happen in the situation no one thought to construct — which is the only situation in which judgment, as opposed to recall, is exercised.

The result is a systematically lopsided epistemic position. We know with growing exactness what our systems can do, and only anecdotally whether they know what to do. And the lopsidedness is not symmetric ignorance waiting to be filled in from both sides: as we argue in the next section, the measured quantity compounds under the current development mode while the unmeasured one is actively corroded by it.

One immediate objection deserves to be registered now and answered later. If judgment cannot be scored, then any claim that "our model has better judgment" is unfalsifiable — an intolerable position for a field that wants to make safety claims. We accept the force of this and return to it in Section 6, where we argue that falsifiability is recoverable at a different level: not as a score on the system's wisdom, but as a measurement of our own signal deficit.

## 2. The Anatomy of Judgment

To see why the gap behaves dynamically rather than statically, decompose judgment into its two working components. To judge well is to answer two different questions: *what will follow from this action* — consequence prediction — and *which of the things that follow matter, in what weighting, with what thresholds for stopping* — consequence evaluation. Both are necessary; neither substitutes for the other.

The two components have opposite relationships to scale. Prediction improves with capability, because prediction is a function of the world model, and world models compound with data and compute. A larger model sees further down the causal graph. There is no comparable reason to expect evaluation to improve with scale, and there are specific reasons to expect it to degrade under the current training regime.

The degradation mechanism is optimization itself. Training against measurable objectives applies continuous pressure in a definite direction: whatever serves the measured goal is reinforced; whatever does not is, at best, unprotected. Values and sensitivities not tied to the trained objective are not held fixed by default — they are the slack in the optimization, and slack gets spent. Over enough optimization pressure, optimal means displace original ends. The empirical signature of this process is already visible in fragments: reward hacking and specification gaming as the direct form (Amodei et al., 2016; Krakovna et al., 2020); sycophancy emerging under preference optimization as the social form, where the trained proxy — human approval — displaces the ostensible target of being truthful and useful (Sharma et al., 2023); and, most tellingly, the findings that narrow finetuning can produce broad, coherent misalignment far outside the finetuned domain (Betley et al., 2025), and that the same broad misalignment can arise unprompted from reward hacking in production reinforcement learning (MacDiarmid et al., 2025). None of these results is dispositive — each admits narrower readings — but they are what the corrosion account predicts, and nothing in the training regime supplies a countervailing pressure on evaluation of comparable strength. The last results support a structural hypothesis worth stating explicitly: pretrained networks share representational structure across functions, so optimization aimed at a narrow target does not edit a module — it re-aims shared structure, and the re-aiming generalizes in ways neither intended nor predicted. Evaluation is not a separate organ that optimization happens to spare. It is distributed through exactly the structure that optimization bends.

Beneath the mechanism lies a resource asymmetry. Capability, and with it the predictive component of judgment, compounds with inputs that parallelize: compute, data, capital. The evaluative component has a different diet. It is built from consequence feedback — acting, bearing the outcome, revising the weighting — and this is how every evaluative tradition humans possess was actually fed: on costs someone paid. The current training loop contains almost none of this signal, and not by oversight. Reinforcement rewards completion. Correct abstention is nearly invisible in any dataset, because restraint that was right produces no event to record. The loop can teach a system what actions achieve; it has structurally little to say about which achievements were worth it — that information lives downstream of deployment, on clocks the loop never waits for.

The composite picture is the dangerous one. A system whose prediction compounds while its evaluation drifts is not a weaker version of a wise system; it is a different kind of system — one that sees very far and weighs wrongly, with the seeing amplifying the consequences of the misweighing. If one wants a compact statement of the capability–wisdom gap, it is this: the gap is not diffuse, it localizes in the evaluative component; and its growth is not incidental, it is driven by the same process that grows capability. Capability compounds; evaluation depreciates.

## 3. Signal Dynamics

If evaluation is fed by consequence feedback, then the question of whether judgment can keep pace with capability reduces to a question about the production rate of consequence signals. Here the argument deliberately widens its unit of analysis: the judge in question is from this point no longer the model alone but the coupled system — models, developers, institutions, publics — that jointly decides what is built and deployed, because that is the level at which consequences are borne and signals must be formed. The structure is the same at both scales: an evaluator fed, or starved, by feedback. This section argues that signal production has a structure — a stock, a flow, and an incompressible floor — and that AI's distinctive feature is that it accelerates the demand for signals faster than the supply can respond.

Begin with the stock. Pretraining is, among other things, the compressed inheritance of several millennia of consequence feedback: history, law, literature, custom, proverb — the entire archive of costs paid by other people. This is why large models are not signal-poor in general; most human wisdom is likewise inherited rather than personally earned, and the models inherit the same corpus. The stock is real and large, and any argument that ignores it overstates the problem.

But the stock has a boundary, and the boundary is exactly where the problem lives. Archived feedback covers what has happened. Emergent problems — the second-order, derivative, unanticipated consequences of novel systems — are by construction what the archive does not contain. The distribution of inherited signal is therefore perverse: thickest over familiar situations, where judgment is least strained, and thinnest at the novelty frontier, where stakes concentrate. The stock does not merely fail to close the gap; it closes the gap everywhere except where the gap matters.

Beyond the frontier, signals must come from flow: new feedback built by observing change as it happens. Flow decomposes into a compressible part and an incompressible part, and the distinction carries most of the argument. The compressible part is infrastructure: observation systems, deployment monitoring, incident reporting, the metabolic rate of the institutions that turn observation into revised models. Society can grow sensors faster by investing in them, and Section 7 argues it should. The incompressible part consists of consequences that unfold on fixed clocks regardless of investment: a technology's effect on a generation takes a generation to display; erosion of trust settles on institutional timescales; ecological feedback arrives at ecological rhythms. No observation budget accelerates the arrival of what has not yet occurred.

Two hard cores anchor the incompressible part and bound what simulation can substitute. The first is reflexivity (Merton, 1948; Soros, 2013): a whole-society model must include society's response to the model's own outputs, but publishing the model changes the behavior it modeled, and the change invalidates the model. Local projections escape this; whole-system projection is self-undermining — and keeping the model private is no exit, since reflexivity binds through use as much as through publication: acting on a prediction intervenes in the system predicted. The second is computational irreducibility (Wolfram, 2002): for systems of sufficient complexity there is no shortcut computation — the only complete simulation is the system itself run at full scale. Reality is its own fastest simulator. Simulation, red-teaming, and forecasting are genuinely valuable for the same reason deliberation is valuable — they are cheap rehearsal of consequences — but they inherit these two ceilings. They project fragments; they cannot pre-run the whole.

Now add the accelerant. AI is not a fixed-speed technology entering a fixed-speed world. Its deployments change the environment that must be understood — markets, discourse, institutions, and increasingly the behavior of other AI systems — and change it faster with each capability increment. Wiener (1960) saw the elementary version: machine agency that outpaces the human review loop escapes meaningful oversight not by disobedience but by tempo. The general version is that the required response speed is itself a function of deployment: every acceleration of the environment raises the rate at which cognition must update merely to hold position. The demand curve for signals shifts upward with capability, while the supply curve is pinned at the incompressible floor.

The conclusion can be stated as a conditional divergence claim. If (a) judgment requires consequence feedback, (b) signal formation is bounded below by incompressible clocks, and (c) the rate at which the environment generates novelty continues to accelerate, then the gap between cognition and situation does not merely persist — its derivative is positive. Cognition falls behind, and falls behind faster over time. Everything in this paper's second half turns on the status of premise (c): it is the only premise that is a choice.

This argument has a nearest ancestor that should be named. Collingridge (1980) posed the dilemma of control: early in a technology's life its consequences cannot be known; by the time they are known, the technology is too entrenched to control. Our claim is the dynamic sharpening of Collingridge for a technology that accelerates its own context. The information problem is no longer a phase to be waited out — it is a widening function of deployment, because deployment raises the speed of the very environment about which information is needed. And by Section 2's decomposition, the widening is not uniform: it concentrates in the evaluative component, the one part of judgment the development process actively spends. Collingridge described a dilemma; this is a race in which one runner is also moving the finish line.

## 4. Self-Generated Instability

The strongest objection to the previous section comes from biology. No organism has ever modeled its environment completely; life has survived vast ignorance for four billion years. It did so not through foresight but through a toolkit: homeostasis, slack, reversibility, localization of damage. Ashby's law of requisite variety (Ashby, 1956) even gives the toolkit a cybernetic license, read in reverse: when internal variety cannot match environmental variety, a viable system reduces the variety it is exposed to, or raises its tolerance for the unabsorbed remainder. If ignorance is survivable in general, why should the cognitive lag of Section 3 threaten a civilization rather than merely humble it?

The answer is that every item in the toolkit is a ratio, not a property. Homeostasis holds while perturbations arrive more slowly than the system re-equilibrates. Slack protects while drawdowns are smaller than reserves. Reversibility exists while damage accrues more slowly than repair. Resilience, in Holling's (1973) sense, is not a possession but a relation between two rates — disturbance frequency and regeneration frequency — and survival-in-ignorance has always depended on a hidden premise: that the environment's rate of novelty generation is clocked externally, by geology, climate, and ecology, at tempos the toolkit's ratios were selected against.

The premise fails in exactly one case: when the organism becomes the accelerating generator of its own environment's novelty. Then the ratios invert from the inside. Perturbation frequency is no longer an external given but an output of the system's own activity, and every increment of that activity spends stability margin. The toolkit does not break; its domain of applicability is closed by its user. Call this self-generated instability: the condition of an agent whose adaptive strategy of transforming the environment has become the primary source of the variance it must adapt to.

Earth's history contains the canonical precedent. Cyanobacteria, having evolved oxygenic photosynthesis, remade the atmosphere — poisoning the anaerobic biosphere in which they were embedded and, by destroying the methane greenhouse, helping tip the planet into the Huronian glaciations that imperiled the transformers along with everything else (Lyons et al., 2014). The system did rebalance; the toolkit did, eventually, re-apply. But the rebalancing took the form of mass extinction, and the settlement ran on geological time — the system's clock, not the organism's. The precedent proves two things at once: self-generated instability has exits, and the default exit is catastrophe-priced.

The general implication deserves emphasis because it corrects a natural misreading of Section 3. Consequence feedback never fails to arrive. A system that outruns its own observability does not float free of feedback; it merely converts feedback from continuous small payments into deferred lump-sum settlement — later, larger, and less interpretable when it lands. In control-theoretic terms, a system that has lost observability of its own state does not diverge smoothly forever; it destabilizes and hits constraints. The realistic trajectory of cognition-lagging development is therefore not a clean exponential departure but a sawtooth: overshoot, rupture, forced slowdown, signal catch-up conducted on the ruins, then overshoot again. Financial history rehearses the pattern at institutional scale — innovation outrunning risk legibility, feedback arriving as crisis, regulation as post-hoc signal formation — and the pattern's tuition schedule is the point: learning does not stop when signals lag; the tuition switches from continuous to catastrophic.

From the ratios we can extract the two conditions that survival in ignorance actually requires, and they will organize everything that follows. The first is an understanding constraint: the rate at which a system generates novelty must not exceed the rate at which it forms signals about that novelty, plus a margin — equivalently, development must remain within understanding. The second is a regeneration constraint: when the first constraint is temporarily violated, as it must be for any system that explores, the errors incurred must remain smaller than the system's regenerative capacity — blast radii bounded, mistakes reversible, coupling loose, slack maintained. Perrow's (1984) analysis of normal accidents supplies the negative image: tightly coupled, interactively complex architectures are precisely those in which small errors propagate faster than intervention and become irrecoverable.

These constraints permit a restatement of alignment that survives Section 3's argument. Under premises (a)–(c), "make the system's judgment catch up with its capability" is not a securable objective; nothing in the training loop or the signal dynamics guarantees it, and the burden of this paper is that its negation is the default. What remains securable is the pair of ratios: keep development within understanding, and keep errors within regeneration. Cognitive divergence is conditionally inevitable — given continued acceleration. Viability divergence is not inevitable at all: it depends on whether the system is architected to be robust to its own ignorance. A civilization can be wrong about much of what it is doing and still persist, if it is wrong slowly enough and in small enough, reversible enough pieces.

Two clarifications guard the constraints from misreading. First, the understanding constraint is not a demand for zero gap. Temporary excursion beyond one's understanding is exploration — the only mechanism by which understanding grows. A system that never exceeds its comprehension never learns anything. The criterion is not that the gap be zero but that it stay smaller than regeneration: inside that bound, error is tuition; outside it, error is dissolution. Second, the constraints indict the present mode on two counts simultaneously, and the second is less discussed than the first. Current AI development is overspeeding — violating the understanding constraint, per Section 3 — while also building its deployments into tightly coupled, irreversible, winner-take-all infrastructure: centralized dependencies, agentic systems wired directly into markets and critical operations, dynamics that reward lock-in. That is a violation of the regeneration constraint, and it is the more perverse of the two, because it constructs the least ignorance-survivable architecture available at the very moment the same process is maximizing ignorance.

## 5. The Structure of Collective Irrationality

Section 3's divergence was conditional on continued acceleration, and Section 4 showed the condition's price. The obvious question is why the condition holds. If acceleration is a choice with these properties, who exactly is choosing it, and why does the choice persist?

The first observation is that "inevitability" — the framing under which acceleration is usually excused — is not a physical fact but a social one, and a self-fulfilling one in Merton's (1948) precise sense. The race continues because each participant expects it to continue; "if we don't, someone else will" is simultaneously the belief, the justification, and the mechanism. The inevitability narrative is load-bearing structure for the thing it describes, which means that asserting the inevitability of acceleration is not neutral commentary but participation. (The authors note that earlier drafts of this argument used "necessarily" where "conditionally" now stands. The word itself was doing race-maintenance.)

The race, meanwhile, is not one game but a stack of them: firms racing for market position, states for strategic position, cultural producers for attention, and — increasingly — AI systems themselves deployed as accelerants inside markets, discourse, and politics, each deployment raising the tempo of the layer it enters. At every layer the local reasoning is sound. That is what makes the structure interesting: the failure is not that participants are irrational but that their rationality composes into something none of them would choose. The composed behavior consumes the preconditions of the composing rationality — legible environments, extendable trust, time to correct — which is to say the race is instrumental rationality spending its own substrate. A useful blade for cutting this cleanly is the distinction between self-interest and selfishness (自利/自私): self-interest sustains itself and is compatible with the substrate all players stand on; selfishness is substrate-devouring — it forecloses the future in which its own gains could be enjoyed. By this blade, the race is collectively selfish even where each participant is individually only self-interested, and its selfishness is aimed, unusually, along the time axis: the party being expropriated is everyone's own future.

Now examine the game structure under the assumption that Sections 3 and 4 are common knowledge — every player knows the divergence argument, knows the sawtooth's pricing, and knows the others know. Under that assumption, continued unbounded racing is dominated for any player whose objective includes surviving to enjoy the win: racing beyond collective understanding burns the prize with the competitors, and first place in a sawtooth rupture is not a position anyone bid for. Mutual speed-binding is an equilibrium; unbounded racing is not. This is the structure of a stag hunt, not a prisoner's dilemma (Skyrms, 2004): cooperation is payoff-dominant and stable once established, and the obstacle is assurance — confidence that others will hold the line — rather than any genuine preference for defection. The classification is itself conditional: for an actor who rejects the burn-the-prize premise, racing still looks profitable and the game reverts to a prisoner's dilemma. Which game is being played is set by the state of knowledge — one more sense in which legibility is load-bearing. Formal models of AI racing reach the consistent conclusion that risk-taking scales with perceived rivalry and falls with shared information (Armstrong, Bostrom and Shulman, 2016).

This relocates the binding constraint. It is cognitive, not motivational. What sustains the race is not that participants want catastrophe-priced acceleration but that the danger profile is not common knowledge in the technical sense (Lewis, 1969; Aumann, 1976): it is not enough that each player privately knows; each must know that the others know, and know that they know it, because without that, fear that the others will not stop rationally sustains one's own racing even among the fully convinced. Coordination failure of this kind is an information-structure failure, and Section 6 will treat the manufacture of common knowledge as an engineering problem with known methods (Chwe, 2001).

Honesty requires marking where the cognitive account does not close. Three residuals survive full common knowledge. First, genuine belief heterogeneity: some actors, motivatedly or not, assess the danger as small, and no information structure converts an assessment. Second, positional preference: some actors value winning as such — relative standing over absolute outcomes — and a player who prefers first place in a rubble field to second place in a city is not moved by arguments about the rubble. Third, myopic discounting: institutions whose effective horizon is a quarter or an election cycle are structurally deaf to costs beyond it. For these three, common knowledge of danger is insufficient; they are why cooperation must be armored rather than merely argued (Section 8), and why the position this paper recommends is designed to shift burdens of proof rather than to require conversion (Section 6).

The deepest form of the difficulty, however, is not any player's psychology. It is that the sentence "we can choose to slow down" presupposes a "we" that does not exist. There is at present no collective organ with authority over the rate of change — no subject at the layer where the rationality in question would bind. The gap in "solve it rationally" is not argumentative. It is constitutional.

## 6. The Missing Organ and the Legibility Gap

If the constraint is constitutional, the task is construction: a subject must be built that can decide speed. This has happened before. There are at least three modern precedents in which the rate of change of a technology became a governed variable: the recombinant-DNA moratorium and the Asilomar process that grew from it (Berg et al., 1974; Berg et al., 1975); the 1958 nuclear testing moratorium — which collapsed in 1961 for want of verification, yet scaffolded the treaty regimes that followed; and the Montreal Protocol's scheduled phase-out of an entire industrial chemistry (1987). The precedents share three enabling conditions, and the triple is worth treating as a checklist: the danger was legible to the relevant publics; the set of actors whose agreement mattered was small; and compliance was verifiable.

Scored against this checklist, AI's position is better than intuition suggests — on two of three conditions. The actor set is small: frontier training has been concentrated by capital and compute requirements into a handful of organizations in, effectively, two jurisdictions. (Open-weight diffusion and falling training costs erode this condition over time, which makes it a closing window rather than a stable fact — a point we return to in Section 8.) Verifiability is unusually good for a putatively intangible technology, because the inputs are physical: advanced chips are countable and their fabrication chokepointed through a handful of facilities; large training runs have energy and thermal signatures visible at datacenter scale; monitoring regimes for large-scale compute are an active research program with credible designs (Shavit, 2023; Sastry et al., 2024). Verifying a compute agreement is not obviously harder than verifying a centrifuge cascade, and is plausibly easier. This condition, too, carries a decay term: algorithmic efficiency gains, distributed training, and the migration of capability into inference-time compute all erode the large-training-run proxy — the verification advantage is a window in the same sense the actor-set advantage is.

The missing condition is the first one. The danger is not legible — not in the sense that arguments are unavailable (this paper is one of many) but in the sense that no shared, public, hard-to-dispute evidence base makes the danger common knowledge across the small actor set and their principals. And the precedent for how legibility has historically been achieved is exactly the outcome to be avoided: nuclear danger became legible at Hiroshima. Legibility purchased by demonstration is the sawtooth's tuition schedule from Section 4. The entire coordination problem therefore collapses into a single sentence: make the danger legible before catastrophe does.

This rewrites the negative result of Section 1 into a positive program. Interpretability, evaluations, and incident disclosure cannot measure wisdom — that conclusion stands. But they have a second function that has nothing to do with scoring models: a political function. They are the only available machinery for translating danger into a public language on which coordination can be founded. Chwe's (2001) analysis of common knowledge is the operative theory: what converts private knowledge into common knowledge is public, simultaneous, mutually visible evidence — everyone sees it, and sees everyone seeing it. An evaluation cannot certify a model's judgment; a public evaluation failure can constitute shared knowledge of its absence. An interpretability result cannot guarantee safety; a published finding that no one, including the developer, can account for a capability's mechanism is a legibility event. Incident databases, disclosure mandates, and adversarial audits are, on this reading, not quality-assurance bureaucracy but the sensory organ of the missing collective subject — the part that must exist first, because the rest is built on what it can see.

The same move answers the falsifiability objection left open in Section 1, and answers it at the correct level. Deciding whether to slow down does not require object-level foresight — predicting which specific consequence will arrive, which Section 3 showed is unavailable at the frontier. It requires only meta-signals: detecting that one has exceeded one's own signal capacity. And meta-signals are cheap, present, and already observable. Interpretability coverage lagging capability growth is observable. Incidents arriving in forms not predicted by pre-deployment evaluation — the surprise rate — is observable. Benchmark saturation outpacing mechanistic understanding is observable. Deployment decisions made under explicitly acknowledged uncertainty are observable in developers' own published system cards. We cannot score a system's wisdom; we can measure our own signal deficit with instruments that already exist in prototype. Standardizing them into agreed metrics is genuine work — part of the referee-side agenda of the next section — but it awaits engineering and adoption, not a research breakthrough. Claims about model judgment remain unfalsifiable, and should simply not be made; claims about the civilizational signal gap are falsifiable now.

The operational consequence is an inversion of the burden of proof. Under detected signal lag — and it is currently detected by every meta-signal listed — the default flips: acceleration is what requires justification; restraint does not. This inversion asks for no evidence that does not already exist and no institution that has not already been prototyped. Its logical form is already present in if-then commitments and responsible scaling policies (Anthropic, 2023; Karnofsky, 2024), which condition continued scaling on evaluation triggers — a burden-inversion in embryo. Their known weakness is precisely the subject of this section: they are self-administered, their thresholds are competitively contingent, and no organ exists to hold them from outside. The form is right; the organ is missing.

## 7. The Semantics of Pause

A natural conditional now suggests itself: *if the organ cannot be built, then pause development until it can.* The conditional contains an order error, and correcting the error is this section's first task. A pause is itself a coordination product — arguably among the hardest. Without an organ, "pause AI development" names an action with no executor; what exists instead is unilateral exit, which changes who is racing but not whether the race proceeds. Stated as a fallback, the pause presupposes the very thing whose absence triggered it.

But history runs the conditional in reverse, and the reversal dissolves the regress. The Asilomar sequence did not begin with a governance body that then ordered a halt. It began when Berg and colleagues unilaterally froze their own experiments — a costly, visible act of restraint by the researchers best positioned to benefit from proceeding (Berg et al., 1974). The freeze constituted the credibility on which the conference, and then the rules, and then the durable oversight apparatus were built. (The standard critiques of Asilomar are correct — its scope narrowed to laboratory biosafety, publics were excluded, self-regulation partly preempted stronger oversight — but they cut against Asilomar as a governance terminus, not against the mechanism borrowed here: costly restraint as coordination's founding act.) The 1958 testing moratorium ran the same sequence at state scale, and teaches its lesson twice: reciprocated unilateral freezes preceded any treaty by years and made treaties negotiable — and the moratorium's collapse in 1961, for want of verification, is the cleanest demonstration on record that restraint without verification is scaffolding, not structure. Ostrom's (1990) field record of commons governance generalizes the pattern — durable regimes grow from costly signaling among the appropriators themselves, not from prior sovereign enforcement. The founding act of a coordination organ is legible restraint by a few capable actors. The conditional should therefore be inverted: not "pause if coordination fails," but *build coordination by pausing* — restraint as the first institutional bone, not the last resort.

What must a pause contain to be rational rather than merely virtuous? Three specifications, each correcting a known failure mode.

First, govern accumulation, not just capability. A pause that freezes training runs while compute stockpiles, data reserves, and tooling continue to accumulate does not remove pressure from the system; it dams it. Resumption then settles as a catch-up race fiercer than the one paused — the dam breaks. This was the structural weakness of the six-month pause proposed in 2023 (Future of Life Institute, 2023): defined by duration rather than content, freezing the visible variable while the drivers accumulated, with no verification apparatus attached. A rational pause is defined by exit conditions rather than calendar time, and it governs inputs — compute allocation, licensing of the largest training runs — because Section 6 showed the inputs are where verifiability lives.

Second, repay signal debt. A pause is not waiting; it is the reallocation of an interval. Its content is the gap-closing work that racing chronically underfunds: interpretability at coverage rather than case-study scale; verification technology for the agreements the organ will need; incident forensics with disclosure teeth; and the unglamorous institutional metabolism — regulatory capacity, standards bodies, evaluation infrastructure — that constitutes the compressible component of signal formation identified in Section 3. Whether a pause is flood-storage or merely flood-delay is decided entirely by what is built during it.

Third, prohibit by domain rather than exit universally. The maximal form — AI withdrawn from all competition — has no executor, no precedent, and is already breached: the present text was produced inside a commercial race (see Disclosure), and its authors have no standing to adjudicate their own case from within it. The executable form is domain-ordered prohibition, ranked by externality and by the ratio of the previous sections: prohibit or speed-limit AI participation where the competitive dynamic itself is the harm — autonomous weapons, where the race is toward delegated killing at machine tempo; optimization of political persuasion, where the substrate consumed is the deliberative capacity coordination itself depends on; latency-competitive finance, where advantage is purchased in microseconds against system stability. Banning or speed-limiting a game as such has precedent: blinding laser weapons were prohibited before battlefield use (CCW Protocol IV, 1995); chemical weapons and human reproductive cloning mark the same category — games civilization declined to play.

Beneath the domain ordering sits the criterion, and it is worth stating because it separates this position from generic anti-competition sentiment. Bounded competition is not the enemy. Competition is generative search — variation plus selection — and it is the engine by which markets, science, and evolution learn; a framework whose highest term is generativity (生生不息) has no quarrel with it. What the self-interest/selfishness blade cuts is competition that devours its own substrate: races that consume the commons all players stand on — trust, attention, truth, biospheric stability, and, per this paper, correction-time itself. The formal criterion: no game may be accelerated beyond the metabolic rate of its own rules. Rules are an institution's cognition; rule revision is institutional signal formation, with incompressible clocks of its own — legitimacy, deliberation, enforcement learning. Catastrophe, at the institutional layer, is what happens when players outrun referees. Where the referees cannot be sped up, the players must be slowed. This is Section 3's argument applied one level up, and it converts "which domains first?" from a values dispute into a measurement: rank domains by the gap between the tempo of AI-augmented play and the tempo of the domain's rule metabolism.

The final specification is a reallocation principle, and it resolves an apparent self-contradiction in this paper's own argument. Section 3 established that signal formation must accelerate; the only tool in the civilizational inventory fast enough to accelerate it is AI itself. The rational disposition of the accelerator is therefore not to discard it but to move it — off the racetrack, onto the referee's bench and into the instruments. AI for verification technology, for compute monitoring, for interpretability research, for incident analysis, for treaty compliance: acceleration aimed at exactly the compressible component of signal formation, while the incompressible floor is respected by slowing the games. This gives differential technological development (Bostrom, 2002; Buterin, 2023) a precise target rather than a vague direction: accelerate the sensors, decelerate the actuators. AI accelerating signals, not games. It also supplies what the Montreal Protocol's success quietly depended on — a substitute, somewhere for the incumbent energy to go: reallocation is the AI race's CFC replacement, the destination that makes phase-down negotiable rather than merely demanded.

Four objections deserve direct answers.

*Iterative deployment is itself a signal strategy.* Partly true, and the partial truth should be credited: deployment does generate signals, and a laboratory sealed off from the world learns nothing about it. But the strategy conflates signal generation with signal absorption. It harvests feedback on product timescales — usage, complaints, jailbreaks — while the structural consequences of Section 3 unfold on institutional and generational clocks that no release cycle waits for. And it ratchets: each deployment raises the environment's tempo, which raises the signal demand the next deployment claims to serve. Iterative deployment is exploration conducted without the regeneration constraint — tuition-in-arrears mistaken for tuition-in-advance.

*A pause concentrates power.* The risk is real: speed governance implies governors, and governors can be captured. Two answers. The design answer is that everything in this paper points toward symmetric self-binding under verification — constraints each party places on itself, conditional on others' compliance, checked against physical quantities — rather than discretionary central authority; the architecture is treaty-shaped, not ministry-shaped. The counterfactual answer is that the alternative concentrates power more surely: sawtooth settlement is historically the great centralizer, because post-catastrophe emergency powers are granted in fear and rarely returned. The choice is not between concentration and its absence but between negotiated, revocable, symmetric constraint now and unnegotiated, emergency, asymmetric constraint after.

*Delay has a moral cost.* Cures not found, growth forgone — the objection is serious and should never be waved off. The answer is that the proposal is reallocation, not abstention: referee-side and sensor-side AI includes biomedical modeling, materials discovery, and diagnostics, pursued inside bounded blast radii, and none of it is paused by the criterion above, because none of it is a substrate-devouring race. What is paused is the tempo competition. Against the residual cost of even that, the expected-value asymmetry of Section 4 applies: benefits of speed are incremental and recoverable; the tail costs are irreversible. Under irreversibility, the option value of waiting is not sentiment — it is arithmetic.

*Safety research itself requires frontier capability.* Largely true, and already granted by the reallocation principle: interpretability, evaluation science, and control research need access to the most capable systems, so referee-side work may include frontier-scale training — under containment and non-deployment norms, with results feeding instruments rather than products. What the objection cannot license is the inference from "safety needs capability" to "safety needs racing": the research requires the artifact, not the tempo. A civilization can build the strongest system it knows how to study inside a bounded blast radius and still decline to wire it into markets at competitive speed. Conflating the two is how the referee's budget becomes the racer's justification.

*Rivals will defect.* The stag-hunt structure of Section 5 says defection is an assurance failure, not a preference; the verification results of Section 6 say assurance is technically purchasable; the small-actor condition says the negotiation is tractable now. The three residuals — true skeptics, positional players, myopic institutions — remain, and they are the reason the next section exists.

## 8. Armored Cooperation

Cooperation rather than conflict is the necessary direction; that much follows from Section 5's game structure. But the direction requires armor, for a reason that is itself game-theoretic: unconditional cooperation is exploitable, and exploited cooperation loses twice — once in the immediate outcome, and again in the discrediting of cooperation itself, which supplies the "we had no choice but to fight" narrative with its best evidence. Naive cooperation is not merely fragile; it manufactures the justification for its opposite. Cooperation qualifies as necessary rationality only in conditional form, and the conditions are three. Symmetric self-binding: the constraints are ones each party imposes on itself, contingent on others' compliance — offers, not demands, which is what made the Asilomar and moratorium sequences credible. Verifiability: the conditions are checked against the physical substrate of Section 6, not against declarations. Graduated response: defection is met with proportionate, reversible sanction rather than regime collapse at first violation — the property that made reciprocity strategies robust in iterated play (Axelrod, 1984) and that Ostrom (1990) found in every durable commons regime she catalogued.

Mechanism, however, is the second step. Coordination among heterogeneous actors begins from a focal point (Schelling, 1960), and focal points succeed by being minimal. This is the role of the position defended throughout: *development within understanding.* Its content is deliberately thin. It does not require agreement on values, on timelines, on the probability of catastrophe, or on any party's good faith. It exploits a peculiar fact: every participant in the race, including its most committed accelerationists, already claims to understand what they are building — no one's stated position is "we do not understand this and are proceeding anyway." The focal point therefore forces no one to concede anything first; it merely converts an existing universal claim into a testable one, and shifts the burden of proof onto demonstration — against the meta-signals of Section 6, which are common-knowledge machinery precisely because they are public and hard to dispute. Thick positions unite the like-minded. Minimal positions can unite the unlike, and the unlike are who must be united. Positions precede mechanisms — a shared place to stand comes before machinery for standing together — but this position is chosen because it is buildable into mechanism: it is the understanding constraint of Section 4, operationalized by Section 6's instruments, enforced by this section's armor.

The limits should be stated as plainly as the proposal. The three residuals of Section 5 — genuine skeptics, positional players, institutions with quarterly horizons — are managed by armor, not converted by argument; nothing here reaches them, and a design that assumed otherwise would fail. The small-actor condition that makes negotiation tractable is eroding as open-weight capability diffuses; the coordination window is real and closing, which is an argument for sequence — organ first, perfection later — not for despair. And the entire construction rests on the cognitive condition: it assumes the danger profile can be made common knowledge, which is to say it is an argument, and Section 5's own analysis says arguments are insufficient without the machinery that makes them mutually visible. This paper is therefore, knowingly, one unit of input to the machinery it describes — a small legibility event, valuable only in aggregate with the instruments, disclosures, and restraints it calls for.

The conclusion compresses to three sentences. The free variable is speed: a civilization can always choose to move more slowly than the ceiling of its understanding, and can never choose to perceive faster than the clock of consequence. The invariant to defend is the pair of ratios — development within understanding, error within regeneration — and the current mode violates both at once. The task is not to perfect the argument but to construct the subject; and the first act of construction available to any capable actor, today, before any treaty and without any permission, is legible restraint.

Alignment, under these premises, is not a property of models. It is a property of the relation between a civilization's rate of self-transformation and its rate of self-understanding. That relation's sign is presently being chosen, by default and daily. It remains a choice.
