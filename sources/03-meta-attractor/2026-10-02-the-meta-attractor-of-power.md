# The Meta-Attractor of Power
### When winning begins to rewrite the game

A system can become increasingly effective at producing winners while becoming increasingly incapable of sustaining the world those winners inhabit.

The puzzle is not simply why harmful strategies sometimes succeed. It is why their success can make the conditions that reward them more entrenched—until even people who recognize the damage find themselves compelled to reproduce it.

In *Hegemonic logic*, Mattias Forsgren proposes that a dominant order under pressure may preserve its position by transferring costs and risks onto others. His account combines claims about deliberate strategy with an explanation based on convergent institutional incentives. Those geopolitical claims require independent evidence. But the essay raises a more general question: **under what conditions does preserving a position of power become more compelling than preserving the shared conditions on which that position depends?** [Mattias Forsgren](https://mattiasforsgren.substack.com/p/hegemonic-logic)

The Meta-Attractor theory begins with that question, but does not begin with a particular hegemon.

Its central proposition is:

> **When success gives a practice the power to reshape the conditions of future success, that practice can become self-reinforcing across people, institutions, and domains—even while undermining the shared conditions that sustain them.**

The decisive step is not merely that winning strategies spread. It is that **they increasingly shape what can win next**. That recursive relationship is the core of the theory. meta_attractors_v0.1_zh

## 1. Success is not the same as contribution

Every selection process favors something. It does not necessarily favor the continued flourishing of the system in which selection occurs.

Imagine two organizations that depend on the same shared infrastructure. One spends resources maintaining it. The other redirects those resources toward its own expansion while continuing to use what others maintain.

Suppose maintenance costs are immediate, deterioration is delayed, and those harmed cannot make the expanding organization bear the full consequences. Under those conditions, neglect can produce a competitive advantage.

The neglectful organization need not be more capable in every respect. It need only be more capable at securing the particular resources that determine survival and expansion.

If those resources allow its practices to spread, the system begins selecting against some of the behavior that keeps it functioning.

This is the first separation:

\[
\text{success within the selection process}
\;\neq\;
\text{contribution to the conditions supporting it}.
\]

Nothing here requires everyone to be selfish. Nor does it require everyone to become selfish. A population can change because certain practices expand, attract imitation, or replace others, even while many individuals retain their original values.

Smaldino and McElreath demonstrated a version of this distinction in their model of scientific institutions: selection for publication output could favor deteriorating research methods without requiring researchers to consciously cheat. The result concerns a specified model and incentive structure, not an assertion that all scientists behave this way. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27703703/)

The relevant question is therefore not merely, “What do people believe is good?”

It is also, “Which practices receive the means to continue?”

This sharpens the phrase *a game organized around winning*. Relative advantage is an especially powerful source of pressure, but it is not the deepest condition. Even absolute growth can be destructive when its rewards are detached from its consequences.

The problem is not that someone succeeds. It is that **success can be purchased by weakening conditions whose value the selection process does not adequately recognize**.

## 2. Cancer makes the separation visible

Cancer is a particularly revealing biological analogy because it exposes a conflict between levels of organization.

Multicellular life depends on coordinated constraints: cells regulate proliferation, participate in specialized functions, share resources, and maintain their surroundings. Aktipis and colleagues describe cancer through the breakdown of these foundations of multicellular cooperation. What benefits a cell lineage locally can undermine the organism’s functioning. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26056363/)

The result does not require a cell to intend the organism’s destruction.

Cancer cells can evade signals that ordinarily limit growth or trigger cell death. Tumors can induce blood-vessel growth and alter surrounding cells or immune responses in ways that support their survival. Their expansion can therefore involve not only taking advantage of an environment, but changing that environment to make further expansion possible. [Cancer.gov](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/understanding/what-is-cancer)

The analogy becomes precise at this point:

**A locally successful process recruits the larger system into supporting its continuation, while weakening the mechanisms that would constrain its damage.**

This is more informative than saying that cancer is “selfish.” Selfishness sounds like a motive. The mechanism does not depend on motive.

It depends on a mismatch between what is being retained and what must be maintained.

Nor is the defining problem simply growth. Healthy development also involves growth. The distinction is whether expansion remains integrated with the conditions that make the larger system viable—or escapes that relationship and reorganizes those conditions around itself.

The analogy has an important boundary. Human beings are not cells, and societies do not possess a single unquestionable purpose. No institution acquires the right to define everyone else as expendable by claiming to represent “the whole.”

Applied to human systems, shared viability must include the conditions under which different people can live, act, disagree, and correct mistakes. Preserving an organization’s command structure is not equivalent to preserving the people it ostensibly serves.

The cancer analogy concerns a **relationship between local reproduction and shared maintenance**, not a classification of people or nations as diseased.

## 3. The crucial step: success starts selecting the selectors

The initial problem is already familiar: an incentive can reward behavior that damages something outside its accounting.

The deeper problem begins when the winners acquire influence over the accounting itself.

Consider a hypothetical research community established to discover reliable knowledge. Because knowledge is difficult to evaluate directly, it uses publication counts as a convenient allocation signal.

Research practices that produce more publications receive more funding, attract more students, and become more widely copied.

Now add another step. Their practitioners increasingly occupy the positions that determine recruitment, promotion, training, and evaluation. The practices favored by the metric begin shaping the institutions that choose and interpret the metric.

Eventually, newcomers may have to adopt those practices simply to obtain the resources needed to do research at all.

This is a thought experiment about recursive selection, not a claim that every research institution follows this trajectory. Its distinguishing feature is the transition from **being selected by a rule** to **helping reproduce the rule that selects you**. meta_attractors_v0.1_zh

The loop is:

\[
\text{local advantage}
\rightarrow
\text{expansion}
\rightarrow
\text{influence over selection}
\rightarrow
\text{reinforced local advantage}.
\]

Once this loop closes, the system is doing more than rewarding a strategy. It is building an environment in which similar strategies are increasingly likely to succeed.

That environment need not be designed through explicit manipulation. It can emerge through ordinary decisions: whom to hire, what infrastructure to build, which methods to teach, what evidence to collect, and which dependencies to accept.

Each decision can appear reasonable in isolation. Together, they can make the original selection logic increasingly difficult to escape.

The process also need not preserve the same winners.

An organization can fail while its operating logic spreads to its successors. A leader can be replaced by an opponent who discovers that maintaining the position requires many of the same practices. Participants can sincerely reject the system’s consequences while reproducing the behavior on which their continued participation depends.

**The persistent entity is not necessarily a person, an organization, or an ideology. It can be a way of succeeding.**

That is why replacing the occupants of powerful positions may leave the underlying trajectory unchanged.

Structural explanation does not erase responsibility. Different actors can have very different knowledge, discretion, and power to change the conditions. It explains why examining intentions alone cannot account for the recurrence.

## 4. Why this is a *meta*-attractor

An ordinary competitive trap can be described as a problem within a game: given these rules, individually advantageous choices produce an undesirable collective outcome.

A meta-attractor concerns the reproduction of the game itself.

The outcomes of selection reshape the conditions of subsequent selection. Those conditions then favor practices that reinforce them further.

Here, *meta* marks a causal level, not a mysterious force outside ordinary mathematics. Rules, institutions, beliefs, and infrastructure can all be included among a dynamical system’s state variables. The question is whether their coupled evolution sustains a recognizable pattern across changes in the particular actors carrying it.

This is not a claim that earlier research ignored changing environments. Eco-evolutionary game theory explicitly studies feedback between strategies and the environments that determine their rewards, and finds different possible outcomes—including coexistence, cycles, and alternative stable states. Feedback alone does not imply collapse. [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-020-14531-6)

The proposed synthesis focuses on a particular combination: successful practices alter selection conditions, extend that influence across domains, and potentially weaken the independent processes through which their consequences could be recognized and corrected.

A self-reinforcing pattern is not inherently destructive. A knowledge community might use reliable discoveries to attract support, then use that support to improve independent verification.

The destructive case requires an additional relationship:

> **The process becomes better at reproducing itself while the shared conditions of life, participation, or correction become worse.**

Those conditions must be assessed independently. They cannot simply mean “whatever the theorist prefers.”

Likewise, calling something an attractor requires more than observing repetition. A mathematical claim needs specified variables and evidence of attraction or stability. An empirical application needs identifiable mechanisms of persistence.

The theory’s claim is therefore conditional: where success can reinforce its own selection conditions, and where that reinforcement outpaces correction while imposing inadequately returned costs, a destructive pattern can become self-maintaining.

Its stability as a pattern does not establish the viability of the system carrying it.

## 5. How different purposes become subordinate to the same logic

The next step explains why the mechanism can spread beyond its original domain.

Imagine an institution with a substantive purpose: discovering truth, providing care, creating art, or supporting a community.

To pursue that purpose, it needs resources. To obtain resources, it must succeed under some allocation process.

Initially, success is instrumental: the institution seeks resources so that it can fulfill its purpose.

But suppose continued survival increasingly depends on optimizing the allocation metric, while fidelity to the original purpose receives weaker recognition. The institution faces a recurring choice between what sustains its mission and what sustains its position.

If choosing the latter repeatedly determines who survives, an inversion can occur:

**Resources cease to be merely a means of sustaining the purpose. The purpose becomes a means of sustaining the organization that acquires resources.**

This need not happen through a single declaration. It can happen through a sequence of compromises, each defended as necessary to keep the original mission alive.

The wider meta-attractor appears when different institutions become dependent on compatible versions of the same success logic.

For example, an expanding organization might fund training that normalizes its methods. Those methods might become embedded in evaluation standards. The standards might then make other organizations dependent on its infrastructure. What began as one organization’s advantage has become a constraint on how an entire field can operate.

This is cross-domain reinforcement—not merely the resemblance of problems in different places, but actual channels through which success in one domain changes what can succeed in another.

There is also a cognitive dimension.

In such an environment, newcomers encounter a world already shaped by earlier rounds of selection. Practices that were once contingent can appear unavoidable. A person learns that restraint is impractical, that certain questions are irrelevant, or that a particular metric is simply what competence means.

The resulting belief can reinforce the environment that produced it.

The sequence is not necessarily:

“People believe everyone is selfish, therefore they behave selfishly.”

It can also be:

“Institutions repeatedly reward externality-neglecting behavior; people adapt; the resulting behavior is then treated as evidence that no other arrangement could work.”

The theory distinguishes two kinds of closure. **Practical closure** makes alternatives difficult to sustain. **Epistemic closure** makes their merits, or the dominant practice’s harms, difficult to represent in ways that affect decisions. A system can retain abundant information while losing the ability to let relevant information change its course. When_Capability_Outruns_Correct…

This is the strongest meaning of totalization here: not necessarily one ruler or one organization, but fewer viable ways to pursue genuinely different ends.

## 6. Hegemony becomes a possible expression of the mechanism

Forsgren’s essay begins with a geopolitical order and interprets how it might behave under pressure. The Meta-Attractor framework asks an earlier question: how can a mode of success acquire disproportionate influence over the conditions under which others must operate?

In this framework, power has two distinct roles. It can help an actor succeed under existing conditions. It can also help the actor determine those conditions.

The second role closes the recursive loop.

Suppose an actor can preserve its relative position by transferring losses to less powerful participants. Suppose those losses weaken their alternatives and increase their dependence. If that dependence, in turn, expands the actor’s ability to determine future terms, cost displacement can become self-reinforcing.

This yields a possible mechanism of hegemonic reproduction without making “hegemon” the starting assumption.

It also explains why contraction can intensify the pattern. When there is less surplus to distribute, maintaining a relative position may become increasingly separable from maintaining everyone’s absolute conditions. Actors able to shift losses elsewhere can preserve their position while the system deteriorates.

But neither hierarchy nor contraction is sufficient to establish that this is happening. Shared exposure to losses, enforceable constraints, reciprocal dependence, or effective correction could produce a different trajectory.

The general mechanism therefore does not validate Forsgren’s particular geopolitical allegations. It identifies what would need to be demonstrated: the transfer of costs, the resulting change in dependence, the conversion of that dependence into selection power, and the weakening of effective correction.

The explanatory shift is from **the presumed character of the dominant actor** to **the conditions that reproduce a dominant logic**.

That is also why changing who occupies the strongest position is not, by itself, evidence that the mechanism has changed.

## 7. What would distinguish an explanation from a compelling story?

A theory this general faces an obvious danger: it can become a vocabulary for redescribing everything.

To avoid that, each connection must be independently contestable.

It is not enough to observe that a practice is successful and that harm exists. One must establish how the practice’s success changes future selection conditions—and whether those changes actually help similar practices persist.

An informative test would compare otherwise similar systems in which current performance receives comparable rewards, but the ability to convert those rewards into control over future evaluation differs.

If limiting that conversion changes long-run persistence, adaptation, or correction, the recursive mechanism has explanatory value. If it makes no difference, ordinary incentives, fixed dependencies, or some other mechanism may explain the result better.

The theory should also lose force in a particular case when successful actors remain effectively subject to adverse evidence; when costs return soon enough to change their behavior; when alternatives retain independent resources; or when rule changes systematically weaken rather than reinforce the incumbent practice.

Several distinctions matter.

Concentration is not the same as capture. An institution can be large without controlling its own evaluation.

Capture is not the same as damage. A self-reinforcing arrangement can support useful coordination.

Damage is not the same as inevitable collapse. Countervailing feedback may interrupt the process, or the result may be persistent degradation rather than total failure.

And cooperation is not automatically an escape. Cooperation within one group can strengthen its ability to impose costs on outsiders. The relevant question remains: whose success is being reinforced, whose conditions are being weakened, and how can those consequences alter the process?

A satisfactory application must identify both the reinforcing loop and its limits.

The same requirement applies to the theory itself. Treating every objection as evidence that the objector has been captured would reproduce the very loss of correction the theory is intended to explain.

## 8. Why greater intelligence does not automatically resolve the problem

The theory becomes especially consequential when systems acquire more powerful means of pursuing their objectives.

Intelligence can improve a participant’s ability to navigate a selection environment. It does not, by itself, determine what that environment rewards.

An AI system could help a community detect hidden harms, compare alternatives, and coordinate repairs. It could also help a competitor exploit the gap between what is rewarded and what is valuable.

Which effect dominates depends on the objectives, deployment conditions, and feedback loops into which the capability is introduced.

Models of AI-development races already illustrate that competitive conditions can produce conflicts between individual incentives and collective outcomes. They also show that outcomes vary with assumptions and incentives; neither unsafe racing nor the benefits of a particular intervention follow universally. [PLOS](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0244592)

The Meta-Attractor question adds another layer: does success in the race increase influence over how the next stage is evaluated, adopted, and corrected?

An obedient system can serve its operator faithfully while contributing to a harmful multi-actor dynamic. Conversely, an imperfect system might be embedded in institutions that detect failures and limit their spread.

Individual obedience and system-level alignment are different properties.

There is also a question of relative speed. If capabilities expand faster than the ability to obtain independent evidence and act on it, correction can become less effective even while evaluation improves in absolute terms. That is a conditional rate mismatch, not a claim that acceleration is intrinsically harmful. Accelerating all relevant processes equally would not create the same problem.

The resulting question is sharper than whether intelligence is good or bad:

**Does greater capability strengthen our ability to revise the direction of optimization—or strengthen an optimization process whose direction is becoming harder to revise?**

A system may become extremely capable at achieving its current objectives while becoming progressively less capable of reconsidering whether those objectives should govern it.

## 9. Success must remain answerable to what it changes

The opposite of this pathology is not inactivity, helplessness, or the abolition of every contest.

The crucial distinction is between a process that becomes more capable **while remaining responsive to its consequences**, and one that becomes more capable **by weakening the channels through which those consequences can constrain it**.

Under the theory, a reinforcing pattern could take the other direction. Reliable work could generate support for better independent scrutiny. Cooperation could expand the resources that make further cooperation feasible. Increased capability could widen the space of alternatives rather than make participation depend on one dominant method.

But no label guarantees that direction. An institution devoted to “the common good” can itself become self-protective. An evaluator can become dependent on what it evaluates. A corrective movement can reproduce the same pattern if its success acquires immunity from challenge.

The corrective mechanism therefore belongs inside the analysis, not outside it as an assumed benevolent authority.

The dividing line is not simply individual versus collective, competition versus cooperation, or decentralization versus hierarchy.

It is whether a system’s successful practices remain answerable to the conditions they alter—including the ability of affected people to expose failure, sustain alternatives, and change the terms.

This is the central inversion the Meta-Attractor theory describes:

> **A practice begins by succeeding within a world. It then gains the power to reshape that world around its success. In the destructive case, it eventually weakens the very conditions by which its success could be judged and corrected.**

The cancer analogy makes the conflict between local expansion and shared maintenance visible. Hegemonic reproduction is one possible institutional expression. Metric-driven organizations and competitive technology races provide other settings in which the same causal question can be investigated.

They are not identical phenomena, and resemblance is not proof. What may connect them is the recursive conversion of success into the power to reproduce its own selection conditions.

The deepest danger, then, is not merely that a destructive strategy wins.

**It is that winning gives the strategy increasing power to prevent its destructiveness from counting as failure.**
