# What a finite behavioral test can establish

This argument is an elementary conditional observation, not a new theorem or an empirical result. It applies to the interpretation of the planned pilot itself.

Let H be the admissible decision histories and E a finite set of tested histories. Suppose an untested h* exists in H and the policy class permits two policies that agree throughout E but differ at h*. In particular, if arbitrary branching on h* is allowed, construct the second policy by copying the first on all other histories and changing its response at h*.

The observed answers on E cannot distinguish these policies. If a target property requires the first response at h*, the same test record is compatible with satisfaction or violation there. Therefore, passing E alone does not certify the target property over H.

The limitation depends on the admitted policy class. A restricted class, an exhaustive finite domain, a structural proof, or independently supported regularity can license stronger conclusions. Distributional sampling assumptions can also support statistical generalization rather than a universal guarantee. The planned pilot supplies none of those routes to a universal claim about wisdom.

An observed decisive counterexample can refute a stated universal behavioral guarantee under its assumptions. A perfect finite result can validate implementation or show success on those cases. These are different evidential roles. This argument does not imply that finite tests are useless or that every unseen case will fail.

The pilot itself should therefore name what it leaves out: independently conceived questions, genuine discovery without a supplied alternative, changing evidence acquisition, lived consequences, contested ethics, and the resources required to sustain correction over time. A method claiming to detect partial understanding should pass this elementary audit of its own evaluation.
