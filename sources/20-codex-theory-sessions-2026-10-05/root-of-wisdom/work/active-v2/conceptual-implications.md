# Conceptual implications of finite inquiry

This memo derives consequences of the six specified models. It contains no participant results and makes no claim that the consequences are new. The objective throughout is expected total loss: query fees plus the terminal action fee plus residual loss. Cash limits cover fees; deadlines cover both inquiry and action. These assumptions make the stopping problem exact, while also limiting what the examples can establish.

The complementary-evidence pair shows why a question cannot always be valued in isolation. Without inquiry, either state-directed action has expected loss `1 + (0 + 12)/2 = 7`, below the fallback's `8`. Either share alone leaves the decision-relevant state equally likely. Buying one share and immediately acting therefore costs `1 + 7 = 8` at the lower price and `4 + 7 = 11` at the higher price. Together, however, the shares identify the state exactly. The lower-price sequence costs `1 + 1 + 1 = 3`: two queries and a correct terminal action. The higher-price sequence costs `4 + 4 + 1 = 9`, so stopping at `7` is optimal. At the lower price either share is an optimal first query; at the higher price either state-directed terminal action is optimal. A rule that evaluates each question only by the improvement available immediately after its answer would reject both lower-price shares. Finite understanding can require a sequence whose value appears only through the combination of its parts.

The deadline pair makes inquiry's opportunity cost concrete. The uninformed repairs each cost `1 + (0 + 20)/2 = 11` in expectation, so the fallback at `8` is initially preferable. Opening the panel, diagnosing, and repairing costs `1 + 1 + 1 = 3` with zero residual loss, but consumes `1 + 2 + 3 = 6` time units. That sequence is feasible at deadline `6` and infeasible at deadline `5`. In the latter case, diagnosis can still finish, but leaves only two units for a repair requiring three. Following it with fallback costs `1 + 1 + 8 = 10`; opening and then stopping costs `1 + 8 = 9`. The optimal initial choice is therefore fallback at `8`. More information does not restore an action whose opportunity has expired. Useful inquiry must preserve the time and resources needed to act on what it might reveal.

The decision-sufficiency pair separates uncertainty about outcomes from uncertainty about what to do. In the common-action case, option A costs `1 + (0 + 8)/2 = 5` in expectation; option B costs `1 + (6 + 9)/2 = 17/2`; fallback costs `10`. A is optimal in each world separately, even though its residual loss remains uncertain between `0` and `8`. Inspection would identify the world but leave the action unchanged, raising expected loss to `1 + 5 = 6`. In the paired case only B's residual loss in the second world changes, from `9` to `0`. B now costs `1 + (6 + 0)/2 = 4` without inquiry, while inspection enables the appropriate action in either world for total loss `1 + 1 + 0 = 2`. Identical uncertainty and identical information channels can warrant different stopping decisions when the consequences of action change.

For the root-of-wisdom inquiry, these examples give a narrow interpretation of *finite, revisable understanding*. An inquirer can possess enough understanding for a particular decision without resolving every uncertainty. “Enough” depends on the available actions, losses, inquiry sequences, and remaining opportunities. A finite account can consequently support warranted action without being a final account of reality. Its adequacy must be reconsidered when those conditions change. The examples establish this decision-relative possibility; they do not establish that completeness is generally unnecessary for every purpose, or that one optimal stopping rule constitutes wisdom.

The boundary of the pilot is equally substantive. Participants select evidence within a supplied grammar: the worlds, priors, channels, actions, constraints, and losses are all given. They do not discover an omitted cause, invent an experiment, create a new action, or negotiate whose interests the loss function should represent. Ethical wisdom includes questions about ends, obligations, people, and legitimate tradeoffs that this scalar objective simply stipulates. Success here would demonstrate competence in a small active-selection problem. It would not establish conceptual discovery, sound moral judgment, or the adequacy of the supplied model outside its specified setting.

The most useful next question is: **How should an inquirer divide limited effort between improving a decision inside its current model and checking whether that model leaves out something that would change the decision?** A controlled next study could start with apparently decision-sufficient models, disclose that some may be incomplete, and offer bounded opportunities to check a premise and revise a world, consequence, or action assumption. Researchers would specify the fuller reference model and the distribution of omissions before running the study. The central outcomes would be whether inquiry detects consequential omissions, whether stopping claims remain calibrated, and the decision loss incurred by checking too much or too little. This changes the object of inquiry from state uncertainty to the adequacy of the representation; it is more substantive than making the existing puzzles harder. It would still test bounded model revision within a designed setting, with any claim about unrestricted discovery requiring further evidence.
