# Evolution, moral capacities, and the limits of the inference

5 October 2026. Focused primary-source memo for the present discussion, not a comprehensive literature review. Five sources are selected below: four empirical papers and one original evolutionary model. Actual article bodies, methods/results, and the stated locators were read. No direct quotation is used; each source capsule stays below 200 words. The short synthesis is our interpretation, not an additional claim by the authors.

## What the evidence can support

Ren's proposal should receive a positive, qualified answer: moral judgment has biological and developmental mechanisms, and some capacities relevant to human morality have observable nonhuman counterparts. A genealogy of these capacities is a legitimate scientific question. But helping, empathy, guilt, reflective conscience, norm enforcement, and moral justification are different claims. Evidence for one is not automatically evidence for all six.

The most defensible formulation is that evolution supplied and shaped capacities on which human moral life depends, while their expression and content are also matters for developmental and cultural explanation. The studies here support parts of this account; they do not identify a single adaptation called “conscience,” reconstruct its complete evolutionary history, or show that selection optimized a global quantity called generativity.

The central distinction is between **a process spreading under selection**, **something benefiting an organism or group**, and **something being morally justified**. One can argue for a bridge between these descriptions, but evidence that a mechanism has a natural origin does not by itself supply that bridge. Equally, having an evolutionary explanation does not by itself discredit a moral judgment.

## 1. Instrumental helping: Warneken et al. (2007)

[Spontaneous Altruism by Chimpanzees and Young Children](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1896184/), PLOS Biology 5:e184, DOI 10.1371/journal.pbio.0050184. Body read: Results and Discussion, Experiments 1–3; General Discussion; participant/procedure sections.

**Finding and controls.** An initial comparison used 36 chimpanzees and 36 German-speaking 18-month-old children. Goal-directed reaching increased object-return helping; immediate rewards did not. A separate novel task tested nine chimpanzees helping unrelated group members open access to food. The wrong-door control kept partner and food present; roles were not reversed within pairs. Subjects helped more when the recipient attempted the relevant door.

**Limit.** This supports a capacity for targeted helping without immediate material reward, not proof of reflective moral obligation or guilt. The authors themselves leave empathy and evolutionary maintenance mechanisms open. The increased-effort experiment selected previous helpers and no longer distinguished reaching from its control, plausibly because of carry-over. The conspecific sample was small; experimental removal of food competition limits extrapolation to wild contexts. The comparison suggests evolutionary continuity rather than directly observing ancestral selection. These are helping tasks, not a demonstration of an innate universal moral code.

## 2. Shared patterns and cultural variation: Henrich et al. (2006)

[Costly Punishment Across Human Societies — primary PDF](https://coevolution.fas.harvard.edu/sites/g/files/omnuum5841/files/culture_cognition_coevol_lab/files/henrich_et_al_2006.pdf), Science 312:1767–1770. Body read: pp. 1768–1770, Experiments, Punishment results, Conclusions.

**Finding and controls.** The study involved 1,762 adults in 15 populations, using anonymous monetary games, locally translated standardized instructions, comprehension checks, and stakes related to local wages. All sampled populations showed more punishment of less-equal offers, but punishment magnitude varied substantially. Some populations also punished unusually generous offers. Population-level punishment measures covaried with giving in dictator games.

**Limit.** The paper supports widespread norm-related behavior together with variation; it does not establish one moral content shared by every human. The association is consistent with gene–culture coevolutionary models but does not identify a unique causal evolutionary history. Punishment in these tasks is an operational behavior, not a complete measure of conscience, justified punishment, or impartial concern. Shared biology need not imply invariant norms, and variation need not imply an absence of biological contributions.

## 3. A neural contribution, not a “conscience switch”: Koenigs et al. (2007)

[Damage to the prefrontal cortex increases utilitarian moral judgements](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2244801/), Nature 446:908–911, DOI 10.1038/nature05631. Body read: main results and Figs. 2–3; Methods, Subjects and Stimuli and task.

**Finding and controls.** Six people with adult-onset bilateral ventromedial prefrontal lesions were compared with twelve brain-damaged and twelve healthy comparison participants. The lesion group more often endorsed sacrificial actions in high-conflict personal moral dilemmas. Other categories were not shifted in the same way, and the lesion group still rejected low-conflict harmful actions. General intellectual abilities were largely preserved.

**Limit.** This supports a selective causal contribution of emotion-related neural processes to judgment. The study uses a small lesion sample and hypothetical scenarios, not a comprehensive assay of moral life or evolution. It does not establish a single location containing conscience. Nor does a response's experimental label “utilitarian” establish philosophical utilitarianism, greater rationality, moral superiority, or moral depravity. Describing participants as lacking morality would contradict the selective result.

## 4. Selection can connect cooperation to conflict: Choi and Bowles (2007)

[The Coevolution of Parochial Altruism and War — primary PDF](https://sites.santafe.edu/~bowles/2007ScienceParochialAltruismWar.pdf), Science 318:636–640, DOI 10.1126/science.1144237. Body read: printed pp. 636–638 plus the concluding caveat on p. 640; especially behavioral-type definitions, within/between-group selection, event chronology, and simulation results. The PDF starts with the end of an unrelated article; the relevant article begins on its first page below that material.

**Result and status.** This is an original game-theoretic and simulation model, not a direct observation of prehistoric evolution. It specifies inherited altruistic/tolerant versus parochial/nonaltruistic types, within-group costs, and between-group conflict and replacement. Under its parameterized conditions, costly helping of insiders and hostility toward outsiders can support one another's spread.

**Limit.** The result establishes a possible selection mechanism within that model. The authors explicitly deny having demonstrated a warlike genetic predisposition. It does not prove that war created human morality, that the modeled alleles exist, that hostility is inevitable, or that helping requires hatred. It is useful against the inference that evolutionary success must mean impartial good or preservation of every affected group's possibilities. Historical validity and normative approval are separate questions.

## 5. Cooperation and local growth inside cancer: Archetti, Ferraro, Christofori (2015)

[Heterogeneity for IGF-II production maintained by public goods dynamics in neuroendocrine pancreatic cancer](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4330744/), PNAS 112:1833–1838, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1414653112. Body read: Results, Figs. 1–4; Materials and Methods, Cell Lines, Proliferation Assay and Public Goods Game; introduction's explicit level-of-selection statement.

**Finding and controls.** Mouse-derived pancreatic tumor cell lines producing IGF-II were compared with nonproducing lines. Conditioned-medium and mixed-culture experiments showed that nonproducers could benefit from producer-secreted factors. Relative growth and coexistence changed with starting frequencies and medium conditions, matching a nonlinear public-goods model. This is a concrete case of cooperation and competition operating within a tumor.

**Limit.** Experiments concerned cells in culture; spatial conclusions also used simulations. They do not directly measure whole-organism fitness or justify a treatment recommendation. The paper explicitly distinguishes within-body clonal advantage from effects on the organism. “Cooperator” and “defector” name functional phenotypes, not intentions or moral character. Cooperation at one biological level can support a process damaging at another; therefore cooperation, innovation, or reproduction alone is insufficient as a moral criterion.

## How to use the evidence in the philosophical answer

The cancer example strengthens the need to specify **whose continuation, which relations, and what timescale**. It does not establish that cancer cells are morally evil: biological damage and moral blame are different predicates, and nothing here supplies cells with moral agency. Nor is it a decisive objection if 生生 already means sustaining a wider field of living relations rather than maximizing local proliferation. In that richer reading, the theory owes a principled account of the relevant field, competing claims, and why its maintenance should govern judgment.

The same test applies to evolved moral emotions. A judgment can feel authoritative because of its causal history, yet justification still asks whether its reasons survive scrutiny from those affected. The intergroup model makes this issue concrete without licensing the claim that every moral response is selfish or violent. A biologically grounded ethics remains possible; it needs an explicit argument connecting vulnerabilities, interests, reciprocity, or agency to obligations, rather than treating selection itself as moral endorsement.

For a short response, prioritize sources 1 and 3 for biological capacities and source 4 or 5 for the boundary of the evolutionary inference. Source 2 is useful if the answer discusses why shared biology can coexist with different moral norms. Avoid adding the rat-helping literature here: its motivational controls would require a separate, balanced discussion, and these primary sources already support the narrower claims needed.
