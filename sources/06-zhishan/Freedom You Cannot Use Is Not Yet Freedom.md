# Freedom You Cannot Use Is Not Yet Freedom

> freedom = permission + capability + exit power

Western societies often describe freedom as the right to choose.

You may choose your occupation, where to live, what to study, what to say, what to buy, and how to organize your life. No authority is formally assigning you a fixed social role. In this sense, modern liberal societies have achieved something historically precious: they have expanded the sphere of personal permission.

But permission is only one part of freedom.

A person may be legally free to leave a job, yet unable to survive without the next paycheck. They may be free to leave an abusive partner, yet unable to afford another home. They may be free to receive medical treatment, yet unable to pay for it. They may be free to study, travel, create, rest, participate in politics, or care for a family, while lacking the money, time, security, or social support required to do any of these things.

The option exists. The person is not formally prohibited from choosing it. Yet it does not exist as a real possibility in their life.

This is the distinction we need to recover:

> **Freedom is not only the absence of prohibition. It is the presence of usable possibilities.**

## The difference between permission and power

Imagine two workers who are both legally allowed to quit their jobs.

The first has savings, healthcare independent of employment, a stable home, supportive relationships, and enough time to search for something better.

The second has no savings, depends on the job for healthcare, owes rent in two weeks, supports a child, and would become homeless after one missed paycheck.

Both are formally free to quit.

But they do not possess the same freedom.

The first person has an actual choice. The second has a legal permission surrounded by material threats.

This does not mean the second person has no agency. They can still act courageously, improvise, sacrifice, organize, or endure. But agency under severe constraint should not be confused with freedom. A prisoner may make meaningful decisions inside a prison, but those decisions do not make the prison disappear.

A useful formula is:

> **Real freedom requires permission, capability, and the power to refuse.**

Permission means nobody formally forbids the action.

Capability means the person has the resources, health, knowledge, time, and security necessary to act.

The power to refuse means saying no does not immediately lead to destitution, exclusion, or ruin.

When one of these is absent, freedom becomes incomplete.

## What affordability really measures

We often treat affordability as a neutral economic question:

> Can this person pay the price?

But when access to the basic conditions of life depends on payment, affordability becomes much more than an economic measure. It becomes a system for distributing practical freedom.

Housing determines whether someone can leave an unsafe home.

Healthcare determines whether illness destroys their life.

Education affects what futures become possible.

Transportation affects where they can work and whom they can meet.

Childcare affects whether they can participate in public and economic life.

Legal assistance affects whether their rights can actually be defended.

Time away from work affects whether they can recover, learn, organize, create, or care for others.

When these things are unaffordable, people are not merely unable to consume certain products. They are unable to exercise important forms of freedom.

This is why availability is not the same as accessibility.

A society may contain excellent hospitals, universities, homes, technology, transportation, and cultural opportunities. But their physical presence does not make them equally present in everyone’s life.

A hospital can exist without being accessible.

A legal right can exist without being usable.

A choice can appear on paper without becoming a possible path.

For a person who cannot afford something, its availability may be almost imaginary. It belongs to the society in general, but not to the world they are actually able to inhabit.

## When freedom becomes a product

Markets can expand freedom. They can create variety, encourage experimentation, coordinate complex production, and allow people to choose among many goods and services.

But a problem arises when freedom itself becomes dependent on purchasing power.

The market then ceases to distribute only optional goods. It begins distributing escape, security, participation, dignity, and control over one’s own time.

Those with sufficient resources can buy distance from danger, recover from mistakes, reject degrading work, move to safer places, obtain better information, protect their health, and take risks.

Those without resources must accept whatever remains survivable.

In such a society, money does not merely purchase comfort. It purchases the ability to say no.

And the ability to say no is one of the foundations of freedom.

A person who cannot refuse an employer, landlord, insurer, creditor, or abusive family structure is vulnerable to domination even when every agreement is technically voluntary.

The contract may say, “You chose this.”

The circumstances may say, “There was no livable alternative.”

## The promise of deferred freedom

How can a society continue to understand itself as free when so many people cannot use its freedoms?

One answer is that present freedom is replaced by a promise of future freedom.

The story says:

Work hard now.

Accept insecurity now.

Endure an unpleasant job now.

Delay your life now.

Prove yourself now.

Then, one day, you may earn enough to become free.

You may eventually afford a home, healthcare, rest, independence, meaningful work, travel, education, or time with the people you love.

This promise can make present unfreedom psychologically tolerable. A person does not interpret their condition as exclusion from freedom. They interpret it as a temporary stage on the way toward freedom.

But the threshold often keeps moving.

Housing becomes more expensive. Education requires greater debt. Healthcare costs rise. Stable employment becomes harder to find. Retirement moves further away. Technology increases productivity, but the resulting gains do not necessarily return to those doing the work.

The person continues running toward a freedom that recedes as they approach it.

They are not living freely. They are preparing to become free.

And because some people do succeed, their success is presented as proof that everyone had the same opportunity. The promise is protected by exceptional examples.

Structural conditions are then translated into personal judgments:

If you are secure, you earned it.

If you are insecure, you failed.

If you cannot afford freedom, you must not have worked hard enough.

This interpretation hides the fact that freedom has been organized as a scarce reward rather than a common social condition.

## Choice is not the same as freedom

Modern societies often measure freedom by the number of choices available.

There are hundreds of products, services, careers, lifestyles, media channels, and places to visit. This variety is real and often valuable.

But a large menu does not guarantee meaningful choice.

A person may choose among twenty brands of food while being unable to choose whether to work twelve hours.

They may choose among entertainment platforms while being unable to choose where they live.

They may choose among insurance plans while being unable to choose whether illness will bankrupt them.

They may choose what opinions to post online while having almost no influence over the institutions that shape their working conditions, neighborhood, environment, or future.

Consumer choice is one form of choice. It should not be mistaken for the whole of freedom.

A society can offer increasing variety in consumption while decreasing people’s control over the basic structure of their lives.

The number of things one may select can expand while the number of futures one can realistically inhabit contracts.

## Survival can become a system of control

Direct domination says:

> You must obey because I command you.

Economic domination can operate more quietly:

> You are free to refuse, but refusal may cost you your home, healthcare, food, status, or future.

This form of power is difficult to see because the final action appears voluntary. Nobody physically forces the person to accept the job, sign the lease, take the loan, or remain silent.

But power does not need to eliminate choice completely. It only needs to make every alternative unbearable.

When people live close to the edge of survival, they lose more than purchasing power. They lose the margin required for freedom.

They cannot afford to fail.

They cannot afford to pause.

They cannot afford to confront authority.

They cannot afford to experiment.

They cannot afford to care for someone without compensation.

They cannot afford to think beyond the next emergency.

Freedom requires some degree of slack: time, security, space, support, and protection from immediate ruin.

A life with no slack is governed by necessity.

## This is not an argument that everyone must receive everything

The point is not that every desire must be satisfied, that all differences in wealth are illegitimate, or that society must provide every luxury without cost.

No society can make every possible good universally available. Resources are finite, tradeoffs are real, and personal responsibility matters.

The deeper question is which conditions must be secured before we can honestly describe people as free.

There is a difference between being unable to afford a private yacht and being unable to afford medicine.

There is a difference between lacking a luxury and lacking the ability to leave an abusive home.

There is a difference between not obtaining everything one wants and possessing no meaningful alternative to submission.

A free society does not need to guarantee equal consumption. But it should guarantee a sufficient foundation of capability, security, and exit.

People need enough to stand as participants in society rather than as dependents forced to accept whatever powerful institutions offer them.

## Freedom should include freedom from domination

A mature idea of liberty must include more than protection from government interference.

Governments can dominate people, and that danger must remain central. But employers, landlords, monopolies, creditors, platforms, families, and private concentrations of wealth can also exercise coercive power.

The source of domination matters less than the person’s actual condition.

Being controlled by a private institution is not automatically freedom merely because the institution is not the state.

A person is not fully free when another party possesses arbitrary power over the necessities of their life.

This is why freedom should be understood not only as non-interference, but as non-domination.

You are freer when no authority can casually destroy your access to housing, medicine, livelihood, communication, or social participation.

You are freer when you can question those with power without risking total ruin.

You are freer when you have genuine alternatives.

You are freer when the institutions affecting your life must hear your voice, justify their decisions, and remain accountable.

## The goal is not to abolish choice, but to make choice real

The answer is not to replace market domination with bureaucratic domination.

A state can also become paternalistic, rigid, humiliating, or authoritarian. Public systems can deny people agency just as private systems can.

The goal should therefore not be maximum state control or maximum market control.

The goal should be a society in which every person possesses a secure foundation from which they can genuinely choose, refuse, participate, and develop.

That foundation may include:

* access to essential healthcare;
* secure shelter;
* education;
* food and clean water;
* legal protection;
* basic mobility and communication;
* time for rest and care;
* meaningful influence over institutions;
* protection against catastrophic failure;
* realistic ways to leave abusive arrangements.

These are not merely benefits handed to passive recipients.

They are part of the infrastructure of agency.

They allow people to become more responsible, not less, because responsibility becomes meaningful only when actions are not dictated entirely by desperation.

## A better definition of freedom

We should not ask only:

> What are people allowed to do?

We should also ask:

> What are they actually able to do?

> What can they refuse?

> What happens if they fail?

> Who controls the conditions of their survival?

> Can they participate in shaping the institutions that govern them?

> Do they possess enough security to act according to conscience rather than fear?

A society is not free merely because it offers everyone the same abstract permission.

It is free to the extent that people possess real, durable, and socially protected possibilities.

The central principle is simple:

> **A freedom that exists only for those who can afford it is not a universal freedom. It is a privilege expressed in the language of rights.**

And an even simpler version is:

> **Freedom you cannot use is not yet freedom.**

## How to explain this to someone else

Begin with a concrete question:

> Is a person truly free to quit a job if quitting means losing medicine, housing, and food?

Most people will recognize that the answer cannot be a simple yes.

Then make the distinction:

> Legal permission is real, but it is not sufficient. Freedom also requires the practical ability to act and the ability to refuse without facing destruction.

Then explain affordability:

> When access to healthcare, housing, education, time, and security depends entirely on purchasing power, society distributes practical freedom through wealth.

Then clarify what the argument is not:

> This does not mean everyone must receive every luxury or that markets have no value. It means the basic conditions necessary for agency should not be available only to those who can pay the highest price.

Finally, state the principle:

> A free society is not one in which everyone is told they may choose. It is one in which everyone has enough security, capability, and power to make important choices real.

That is the idea worth defending.

Not freedom as a distant reward.

Not freedom as a product.

Not freedom as a story people are told while necessity governs their lives.

But freedom as a lived condition: the real ability to refuse domination, participate in society, care for others, shape one’s future, and continue becoming.
