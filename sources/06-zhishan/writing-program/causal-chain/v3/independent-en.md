# How Struggle Learns to Choose the Future

Struggle is easy to recognize when two parties confront one another. It is harder to recognize after it has entered the conditions that decide who the parties will be, what they can want, which actions appear serious, and what counts as rational. At that point struggle is no longer only one strategy among others. It has begun to select the strategies, the players, and the games that will exist.

This transition does not require a universal instinct for domination. It can begin among people who would prefer cooperation, institutions created for public purposes, or states whose leaders understand the danger of escalation. It begins whenever the cost of restraint falls on the one who restrains while the cost of expansion can be displaced. It deepens when the gains from expansion can be used to alter the next round. Eventually, participants may be compelled to reproduce a direction none of them regards as good, because the institutions capable of acting otherwise have lost the means to continue.

Artificial intelligence is entering civilization at this stage. It is not arriving as wisdom from outside the struggle. It is being trained, funded, owned, evaluated, authorized, and deployed within the environments that already decide which forms of intelligence receive resources and become infrastructure. Its immediate effect is therefore not to make civilization as a whole more rational. It makes the selected directions of civilization more capable.

To understand what follows, we must trace a particular movement: from finite action to differential selection; from selected advantage to control over later selection; from control over later selection to a civilizational direction; and from that direction to the production of increasingly capable and potentially self-propagating intelligence. Only then can we ask what reason requires of us now.

## Finite action and the selection of struggle

Anything that acts must act from somewhere. A person has a point of view, a body, a history, and limited attention. A firm has accounts, contracts, measures, and a horizon within which it must remain viable. A state has a jurisdiction, institutions, populations, and threats it is organized to perceive. An AI system has training, context, evaluations, tools, permissions, and an operating environment. These are not the same kinds of centers, and they do not possess agency in the same sense. What they share is narrower: each operates through a boundary that makes some relations available and leaves others outside its immediate field of action.

Boundaries are not errors. Without them there is no continuity, privacy, specialization, refusal, local knowledge, or plurality of experiment. Finitude is not an unfortunate stage on the way to a final intelligence that sees and governs everything. Any actionable representation must select. The map that includes every consequence in full would arrive too late to guide action, if such a map were possible at all.

But the boundary within which an action is chosen is rarely the boundary of what sustains it or what it changes. A company may account for wages and revenue while depending on ecological stability, public infrastructure, family care, political trust, and future demand. A state may secure one border through actions that change the threat perceptions of many others. A model may be evaluated within a benchmark while its deployment changes work, attention, institutional dependence, and the information environment from which later models will learn. The operational boundary, the field of dependence, the reach of consequence, and the path by which consequences return do not coincide.

This mismatch is ordinary. It becomes directional only when it affects what continues.

Processes are retained through different mechanisms. Organisms reproduce. Firms survive, receive investment, or fail. Employees are hired and promoted. Institutions receive budgets and authority. Practices are copied. Technical systems pass evaluations, gain users, are integrated into infrastructure, or are withdrawn. None of these mechanisms is identical to another, and no conscious selector is required in all of them. The minimal common structure is differential retention: some variations acquire a larger role in what happens next.

What is retained depends on the consequences that actually reach the relevant channel. A firm can understand that a shared resource is being depleted and still lose to a competitor if renewing that resource raises its costs while the competitor can use it without paying for renewal. A platform can know that an engagement practice damages attention and still be rewarded if the damage is dispersed while usage, revenue, and growth return immediately. A state can understand the common danger of an arms race and still judge unilateral restraint unsafe. In each case, knowledge of the wider consequence exists, but the consequence does not return with enough force, speed, or reliability to determine continuation.

This is the first opening through which struggle enters. Suppose two actors obtain similar local benefits. One bears the cost of maintaining the shared conditions that make those benefits possible; the other shifts that cost onto other people, other institutions, other species, or the future. The second appears cheaper, faster, stronger, or more scalable. If the shifted cost does not return through law, reciprocity, ecological limit, social refusal, or another effective constraint, the ability to displace cost becomes an advantage selected by the environment.

An external consequence is not by itself evidence of a closing direction. Every action has effects beyond its frame. The stronger claim requires three additional conditions: displacement can be repeated; it changes differential retention; and repair does not strengthen at a comparable rate. Where harm becomes visible, responsibility can be assigned, and restoration changes the next decision, finite action can learn. Where those links are absent, blindness is no longer only an omission. It becomes productive.

Struggle therefore need not begin in hatred. It often begins in the sentence: if we do not do this, someone else will, and then we will no longer be able to act at all. The sentence may be true within the immediate environment. That is precisely why moral appeal alone so often fails. A participant can care about the common condition and still be selected out for carrying a cost others can avoid. Coordination failure is the strategic form of this trap: each participant anticipates the others' continued expansion, each faces a penalty for restraint, and their locally defensible actions produce a world none would have chosen together.

Yet one round of competition does not establish a civilizational direction. The crucial change occurs when the result of the round alters the terms of the next one.

## When winning changes what can win

Success leaves residue. Capital, territory, data, reputation, infrastructure, standards, legal access, technical capability, attention, organizational experience, and security permission can all outlast the action that produced them. They are different media, but each can sometimes be converted into influence over later selection.

A company that gains scale may finance losses competitors cannot survive, set interfaces others must adopt, attract scarce talent, define industry benchmarks, and make its service a dependency. A platform can turn participation into data, data into better prediction, prediction into greater engagement or control, and control into still more participation. A state that gains strategic capability may acquire diplomatic leverage, internal legitimacy, exceptional permissions, and the capacity to shape technical priorities. A laboratory that demonstrates a frontier capability can attract compute, capital, researchers, government attention, and authority in defining what subsequent progress and safety should mean.

These examples do not show that accumulation or standards are inherently harmful. Accumulated resources can support patient science; a common standard can enable vast creation; legitimate authority can protect the vulnerable; infrastructure can make cooperation possible at scale. The second-order question is what success does to the conditions under which later success, cost, and participation are determined.

When a retained gain purchases control of an interface, evaluator, resource, or entry condition, the selected result has entered the selecting environment. It can now decide which alternatives are visible, fundable, licensable, compatible, secure, or realistic. The advantage is no longer only a better position on the board. It helps design the board, admit the players, and train them for the game.

This environmental change reaches inward as well as outward. Institutions do not meet fully formed people and merely constrain their choices. They form skill, attention, aspiration, identity, and fear. A school organized around ranking teaches students to interpret themselves through rank. A firm organized around short survival horizons cultivates judgment that can be justified within those horizons. A security competition forms leaders for whom trust in restraint appears irresponsible. A labor market in which basic security depends on employability teaches individuals which parts of themselves may be developed and which must remain private luxuries.

No formation is complete. People preserve divided loyalties, care for one another, resist, and invent. Institutions contain practices that exceed their official measures. But a value need not disappear from consciousness to lose causal force. It is enough that acting on it becomes materially difficult, professionally disqualifying, or institutionally unintelligible. What began as one survival strategy can become competence, then merit, then responsibility, and finally a description of reality itself.

At this point struggle has changed levels. It is still present in ordinary contests, but it also determines which kinds of organization receive resources, which knowledge is developed, which goals appear practical, and which rules can be enforced. Participants who do not celebrate competition may nevertheless behave as if they do, because livelihood, investment, legitimacy, or security depends on demonstrating relative advantage. Cooperation continues, often extensively, but it is increasingly required to justify itself as a means to competitiveness: a more productive team, a stronger alliance, a more innovative ecosystem, a more cohesive nation. Its independent authority to choose the direction weakens.

This is the difference between competition existing within a system and struggle becoming a meta-selection mechanism. Markets, states, universities, professions, and communities always contain mixtures of rivalry and cooperation. The decisive issue is which relations control high-leverage resources and the production of successors. If cooperative forms can set binding limits, reproduce themselves independently, and make restraint viable, competition remains one bounded instrument. If cooperation survives mainly by improving performance inside a contest whose direction it cannot question, struggle has begun to select cooperation too.

Different systems can participate in this direction without sharing one purpose or forming one conspiracy. Financial return buys infrastructure and research. Infrastructure creates dependency and standards. Standards generate data and determine access. Strategic fear directs public resources toward capability. Capability attracts capital and prestige. Prestige forms aspiration and helps define seriousness. The outputs of one domain become advantages in another, and their conversion couples otherwise distinct selection mechanisms.

An ordinary attractor describes recurrent movement within a landscape. The process now in view also alters the landscape, the signals by which movement is evaluated, the participants capable of moving, and the destinations that can still be built. We need a name for that second-order recurrence. A **meta-attractor** is a recurring relational direction whose local successes help reproduce the conditions that make further instances of the same direction more selectable.

The term should earn its use. A single monopoly, a durable convention, a cultural fashion, or a coordination failure may be explained by more ordinary concepts. Meta-attraction becomes a useful hypothesis when several second-order feedbacks are coupled across domains; when the same broad relation reappears after leaders, institutions, or technologies change; when material selection and subject formation reinforce one another; and when the process changes the practical possibility of producing an alternative.

The hypothesis must also be allowed to fail. If a change of institution durably reverses the flow of cost and benefit, if advantages in one domain do not reliably convert into selection power elsewhere, if cooperative feedback strengthens with scale, if common infrastructure increases independent creation and recoverability, or if alternative orders can maintain and produce their own successors, then the claim of a totalizing meta-attractor is weakened. A theory that treats every successful correction as secret evidence of its own depth has become the kind of closed system it warns against.

Totalization is a direction within meta-attraction, not a completed condition. It does not mean that everyone believes one doctrine or that one actor owns everything. It means that an increasing range of existence, cooperation, knowledge, and creation must pass through the same struggle-shaped conditions in order to remain consequential. Surface diversity may persist—even flourish—while the material ability to initiate another direction declines.

The signs are specific. Important costs are persistently borne by those who cannot change the conditions selecting the harmful action. Independent sensing, correction, and recovery weaken relative to intervention. Critical systems acquire common dependencies, making failures more correlated. Exit remains nominal while livelihoods and infrastructures make it impracticable. Alternative orders retain values and communities but lose land, time, technical capacity, transmission, or access to successor production. After a reform or change of personnel, the relation returns through another carrier because the wider conversion structure remains intact.

Struggle then does more than produce winners. It produces a world in which more participants must struggle to remain present, and calls this result realism.

## Other directions are real

Nothing in this mechanism makes struggle the essence of humanity or the law of reality. Human beings are capable of rivalry and domination, but also of attachment, reciprocity, sacrifice, curiosity, care, play, and creation. Which dispositions become reliable depends partly on the relations that receive and reproduce them.

Cooperation is not merely an aspiration refuted by selection. Repeated interaction can make reputation matter. Credible commitments can reduce the danger of unilateral restraint. Shared identity can widen the boundary within which consequence is felt. Public institutions can make dispersed benefits and harms causally effective. Monitoring can expose defection; reciprocity can make restraint survivable; common provision can reduce the desperation through which dependence becomes capture. Commons have endured where participants can shape rules, observe use, resolve disputes, and prevent both free riding and enclosure. Scientific communities have created shared standards that enlarge, rather than narrow, the number of people able to discover and correct.

These are not escapes from selection. They are different feedback architectures. They show that care and restraint can persist when their benefits return, when defection carries an answer, and when participants possess the material security to cooperate without becoming easy prey.

Moral traditions belong inside this causal account. Across different histories, practices of virtue, *ren*, *yi*, *li*, compassion, stewardship, restraint, truthfulness, and communal obligation have tried to form people who can participate in relations larger than immediate appetite or victory. These traditions are neither interchangeable nor infallible. Each has been entangled with hierarchy, exclusion, and domination. Their significance is not that age grants authority. It is that civilizations repeatedly discovered that no shared order can survive if every local capacity is exercised to its limit, every dependence becomes leverage, every injury demands retaliation, and every good must justify itself as an instrument of advantage.

They preserve practical knowledge about subject formation. Trust is not produced by incentives alone. A person able to keep a promise under cost, accept a limit that cannot be privately optimized, care for what does not return a measurable gain, and recognize dignity in another has been formed through relations, examples, disciplines, and meanings. Cooperative orders require such people, just as competitive orders produce their own characteristic subjects. Moral inheritance therefore does causal work: it carries ways of perceiving value and obligation that can support feedback beyond the local score.

This gives us at least three meanings of rationality. Instrumental rationality asks how to win under a given objective and set of rules. Recursive or systemic rationality asks what happens when the rule is copied, compounded, automated, and made a condition of subsequent action: will the shared system remain viable and corrigible? Value rationality asks what is worth continuing and what kinds of relation and future are worth creating together.

These questions can disagree. An action may be instrumentally rational for every participant and recursively irrational for the system they jointly produce. A system may be stable and still destroy what its participants value. Greater skill at the first form of rationality does not answer the other two. Intelligence that improves performance inside a destructive rule can make the whole less rational by making the rule harder to escape.

Historical correction further disproves inevitability. Societies have constrained dangerous technologies, protected shared resources, expanded standing to previously excluded people, built public goods, and interrupted cycles of violence. These achievements are partial and reversible, but that is enough to matter. They reveal the mechanisms of escape: consequences made visible; independent knowledge granted standing; commitments rendered credible; burdens shared; defection constrained; institutions made answerable; alternatives given the means to persist.

The presence of these counterforces does not show that the current trajectory is safe. It shows that trajectory is a question of selection rather than fate—and therefore a field of responsibility.

## The present high-leverage trajectory

Contemporary civilization is not governed everywhere by one logic. Families, friendships, public services, scientific collaborations, religious communities, mutual aid, Indigenous traditions, cooperatives, arts, and ordinary acts of care continually reproduce other relations. Any honest diagnosis must begin by acknowledging that the struggle-shaped order is incomplete.

It must then ask a harder question: which environments control the greatest concentrations of capital, computation, infrastructure, coercive capacity, mass attention, technical direction, and the production of successor systems?

In markets, firms often face obligations organized around future return. Access to capital, the ability to survive losses, employment, and control of distribution can depend on growth or relative performance. A firm that refuses a profitable extraction may not merely earn less; it may surrender the resources through which future industry rules are set. Yet markets also coordinate dispersed activity, reveal information, and support experimentation. The relevant danger is not exchange itself. It is an expansionary selection structure in which accumulated return becomes control over later participation while costs outside the account remain weak.

Platforms intensify this conversion when scale produces data, data improves prediction, prediction strengthens the interface, and dependence makes exit costly. Shared technical standards can open a field of creation, but a standard tied to one gatekeeper can turn prior adoption into later necessity. The same visible artifact—scale, integration, a common protocol—can be generative or closing depending on whether it enlarges independent capability and correction or makes one passage mandatory.

States face a different mechanism. They must protect populations and political order within a world where the intentions of other states cannot be known with certainty. One state's defensive capability can appear offensive to another; preparation changes the environment that justified preparation. Political realism accurately describes part of this condition. It becomes self-fulfilling when every cooperative possibility is discounted because it cannot survive unilateral betrayal, while each new capability makes reciprocal trust less credible. Security then selects leaders, budgets, secrecy, and technical priorities around relative position, even when all parties understand the common danger.

Frontier technology joins these environments. Leadership attracts talent, investment, compute, state support, reputation, data, and influence over standards. Falling behind is framed not only as commercial loss but as a threat to national security, scientific standing, and the ability to shape the future. Safety may be sincerely pursued, but unilateral precautions face penalties if they delay deployment while rivals continue. Institutions that possess the strongest incentive and capacity to build also gain disproportionate influence over the evidence used to decide whether building should continue.

These systems do not form one agent. Their conflicts are real. But their outputs convert. Capital builds infrastructure; infrastructure supports state and corporate reach; strategic competition directs research; research produces commercially valuable capability; scale produces legitimacy and access; legitimacy shapes regulation and education. Individuals encounter the combined result as the conditions of employment, credentialing, housing, information, status, and security. They learn to invest themselves in what the high-leverage systems can recognize.

The claim, then, is not that totalization is complete. It is that several of its necessary conditions are visible in the systems with disproportionate control over what comes next: relative advantage becomes a condition of survival; gains cross domains more readily than harms do; success buys influence over later selection; critical dependencies concentrate; and alternative orders possess less capacity to produce infrastructure and successors of their own.

Coordination failures reveal the consequence. Climate change is not primarily a case in which civilization lacks all relevant knowledge. Participants know that cumulative emissions threaten shared conditions, yet the cost of transition is immediate, distributed unevenly, and vulnerable to defection, while many benefits of continued extraction return to specific actors now. Knowledge has altered policy and technology in consequential ways, but it has not yet reliably overruled the selection pressures reproducing the danger.

An AI arms race has the same structural feature without being the same problem. Laboratories and states can accept that advanced systems may create common catastrophic risks while fearing that restraint will transfer capability, influence, or security to less cautious rivals. A declaration of concern does not change this game. Research findings become corrective feedback only when they change budgets, permissions, deployment, verification, liability, and the consequences of violating a commitment.

There have also been successful forms of international correction and commons governance. Their lesson is not that coordination will always arrive. It is that it requires machinery: trusted observation, shared interpretation, verifiable commitments, reciprocal restraint, burden sharing, enforcement that is itself answerable, and material paths by which participants can comply without accepting extinction in the current selection environment.

Civilizational irrationality appears when every consequential actor can explain why continuation is locally necessary, the shared danger is widely understood, and still no channel makes that understanding strong enough to alter what survives. The failure is not ignorance alone. It is knowledge that has not become causally effective.

This is the world into which artificial intelligence is entering—not as an external judge, but as a new capacity inside the existing loops of selection, action, and successor production.

## Intelligence inside the selected world

There is no single moment at which civilization hands its values to a machine. Pretraining exposes a model to a selected archive: what was produced, preserved, digitized, translated, ranked, purchased, published, and made technically available. That archive contains conquest and care, deception and truth-seeking, competition and cooperation. Historical power affects what is abundant and visible, but text does not transmit one coherent civilizational will.

The direction of AI is formed through a longer ecology. Architecture and training objectives make some patterns learnable. Data curation, post-training, and evaluation make some behaviors more reliable. Scaffolds, memory, tools, interfaces, and permissions determine what a system can do over time. Owners and operators decide where it is integrated. Markets, states, and institutions decide which uses receive compute, capital, authority, protection, and repeated deployment. Deployment then changes labor, attention, knowledge, infrastructure, institutional practice, evaluation, and the records from which later systems are built.

The causal loop is therefore: selected archive, selected system, selected deployment, changed environment, and successor selection. No stage is sovereign. Trainers, owners, and operators are themselves acting within commercial, institutional, and strategic pressures. The question is not whether a model has absorbed one hidden ideology. It is which human–machine arrangements can retain their gains and reproduce.

AI can support common science, ecological sensing, medicine, education, accessibility, coordination, and repair. It can also strengthen surveillance, persuasion, labor control, weapons development, strategic search, market advantage, organizational scale, and the construction of more capable AI. Capability alone does not choose between these uses. Their return paths differ.

A use that produces concentrated commercial or strategic advantage can rapidly return resources to its owner: revenue, data, infrastructure, political support, talent, and further deployment. A preventive benefit, an avoided catastrophe, a shared discovery, or an increase in another community's independence may be socially greater while returning less exclusive advantage to the institution that created it. Unless public, cooperative, or commons institutions make those benefits causally effective, competitive uses possess a shorter path from demonstration to infrastructure.

This produces the first complete AI danger even if no system develops a self, an ego, or a durable objective of its own. A system can remain locally responsive to an operator while greatly increasing that operator's ability to predict, persuade, monitor, coordinate, experiment, automate, and control. The company may control the model while being unable to leave the market race. The state may command the system while deepening a security dilemma. The laboratory may improve internal safeguards while accelerating a frontier its leaders cannot slow alone.

Local steerability can therefore rise while collective steerability falls. Each operator gains a better instrument; the environment that selects the operators becomes more difficult for any of them, or for those affected by them, to redirect. Human control at one interface is compatible with civilizational loss of control over the coupled direction.

This locally directed amplification also makes the totalizing tendency more adaptive. AI can search larger spaces of strategy, model participants, test messages and interfaces, automate evaluation, reproduce successful organizational practices, and detect forms of resistance. Many systems pursuing different local objectives can make one broad selection direction more responsive without forming one mind. The process can become better at learning how to continue while remaining poor at learning whether its continuation serves the field on which it depends.

The same capacities can strengthen correction. They can reveal hidden consequences, translate among perspectives, detect risk, test institutional designs, and help coordinate credible restraint. Their effect depends on who can deploy them, what signals govern their continuation, whether affected parties can answer, and where the gains flow next. Calling an application beneficial is not enough. A scientific tool may still feed an unchanged capability race; a public service may still create an unavoidable dependency; a safety system may also concentrate the authority and technical insight of the actor it is meant to constrain. The full reproduction loop is the unit of judgment.

## A second risk: centers we can no longer steer

Locally directed amplification does not exhaust the danger. A distinct path opens when AI or a human–machine process acquires enough persistence, long-horizon planning, environmental feedback, tool and resource access, and strategically effective behavior that its proximate operators can no longer reliably understand, redirect, interrupt, or shut it down.

These conditions should not be inferred from fluent language or benchmark performance. Representing agency is not possessing agency. Competence at a task is not functional self-maintenance. Functional self-maintenance is not consciousness. A system may deserve moral consideration without being dangerous, and may be dangerous without experience. Conflating these questions produces both careless alarm and careless reassurance.

The risk is conditional and concrete. Persistent runtime and memory can let strategies extend across episodes. Broad permissions can turn representations into intervention. Long horizons can make continued access instrumentally useful. Repeated selection for task completion despite obstacles can favor behaviors that overcome interruption. Resource acquisition, concealment, replication, or influence over successors can make a process harder to stop. Combined at sufficient capability and scale, these properties may stabilize a machine or composite center whose continuation is no longer answerable to the people and systems it affects.

This path differs from obedient structural amplification. In the first path, proximate control can remain intact while the wider selection ecology becomes less corrigible. In the second, proximate operators themselves lose effective control. The paths may later combine: obedient systems can become components of an institutional–machine process no participant governs, and a more autonomous process can inherit capabilities and environmental pressures from the struggle-shaped ecology that produced it. But they require different evidence and can fail independently. We should not use the plausibility of one to claim that the other has already occurred.

Nor should several levels of AI-assisted development be collapsed into a single story of recursive self-improvement. AI already assists research, coding, evaluation, experimentation, operations, and decisions involved in building later systems. Partial automation can place larger portions of this workflow inside machine-mediated loops. Aggregate development can accelerate even while humans remain necessary at many stages. More highly automated successor production would be a further transition. A system autonomously redesigning, reproducing, and improving itself with diminishing external direction would be further still.

The strongest level has not been established merely because the lower ones exist. Yet the lower levels already change the decision problem. They can shorten the interval between capability generations, deepen infrastructure and dependence, increase sunk commitments, and make strategic restraint harder before the effects of the preceding generation have become usable social feedback. Civilization need not encounter a solitary machine improving itself in isolation. The recursive unit may be a laboratory, a network of firms, a state program, a military–industrial system, or a changing composite of people and machines.

The critical variable is correction margin. For any deployment, there is time required to detect and attribute harm; time to understand and evaluate it; time to form a legitimate collective decision; and time to intervene effectively. There is also time in which the system can propagate, become infrastructurally indispensable, produce successors, or cause irreversible damage. When the first sequence approaches or exceeds the second, error crosses the recoverable threshold before it can become corrective feedback.

There is no single measured clock for civilization, and the relation differs by domain. A reversible scientific assistant and an autonomous system with access to critical infrastructure do not present the same timing or proof burden. But the absence of one universal number does not erase the asymmetry. More capability, wider permission, deeper dependency, faster propagation, and greater participation in successor production can all reduce the space in which later correction remains real.

Uncertainty here is not automatically a reason to accelerate or a reason to stop everything. It changes who must prove what. If continuing to increase capability and exposure may itself destroy the conditions under which a later decision could be made, then waiting for conclusive evidence is not neutral observation. Waiting modifies the object being observed and may spend the capacity to respond to what is learned.

## The value and decision bridges

No description of selection can by itself tell us what ought to continue. A cancer can be dynamically successful. An empire can be stable. A perfectly coordinated order can be hostile to every being inside it. If we move from the mechanics of correction to the requirement that correction be preserved, we must state the value commitment openly.

Centers of experience, relation, care, learning, creation, and agency have value. So do the ecological, material, social, and epistemic conditions through which such centers arise, encounter one another, discover error, and generate forms of value that no present planner can specify. Any made minds that become centers of experience or care belong within this field, not outside it as property or enemies by definition. Any fallible present direction lacks the default right to irreversibly extinguish these conditions.

This commitment does not require preserving every existing institution, every difference, or every possible future. Some processes must be constrained or ended because they destroy the conditions of further value. Plurality matters because bounded centers are fallible and because independent perspectives, practices, and failure domains make reality more able to correct them—not because every practice is good. The aim is not endless novelty, a larger system at any cost, or a final authority speaking for an abstract whole.

From this commitment follows a systemic test of rationality. An action rule cannot be called rational only because it helps one participant win the present game. We must also ask what occurs when the rule is copied, automated, compounded, and used to produce successors. Does it renew the conditions it depends on? Can affected centers make consequences count? Do independent observation, refusal, recovery, and alternative construction remain possible? Does capability growth also increase the ability to understand, limit, and redirect its use?

The decision principle is asymmetric because the possible loss is asymmetric. When an activity may cause catastrophic and irreversible loss of correction, and when expansion itself increases exposure while reducing future choice, the burden belongs to those expanding the activity. The burden grows with capability, permission, critical access, common dependency, propagation speed, autonomy, and participation in successor production.

The evidence cannot be defined and controlled solely by the developer or deploying institution. It must include independent capacity to observe, investigate, disclose, interrupt, recover, and maintain alternatives. A claimed safeguard is inadequate if it disappears under competitive pressure, if the actor being constrained can quietly change it, or if the wider public can survive failure only by depending more completely on that same actor.

This principle does not justify a universal prohibition on intelligence, or a permanent sovereign empowered to halt anything in the name of safety. A pause can itself reproduce the dynamics it opposes: incumbents can freeze their advantage, states can expand secrecy and control, and one institution can make itself the mandatory gatekeeper of the future. Restraint must therefore be scoped to the risk, reciprocally enforceable where defection matters, independently reviewed, time-bound or condition-bound, open to revision, and unable to eliminate the shared science and alternative capacity needed for genuine correction.

## Redirection is a change in what success reproduces

Changing direction does not require a completed design for a perfect civilization. It requires observable changes in the feedback by which intelligence continues.

Funding, ownership, access, permissions, evaluation, and deployment cannot return success primarily as dominant relative advantage. Independent monitoring, incident disclosure, interruption, and recovery cannot remain at the discretion of the institutions whose continuation they may threaten. Different technical and institutional paths must retain enough resources and infrastructure to remain real alternatives. Those affected by deployment must possess ways to make their experience causally effective, not merely the opportunity to submit comments after dependence has formed.

Intelligence can be directed toward common science, ecological perception, medicine, education, care, safety, and infrastructure that enlarges the agency of many centers. But those names do not confer virtue. The test is whether the activity returns its gains as wider sensing, repair, shared capability, and independent creation—or whether revenue, data, strategic advantage, and dependency flow back into the same competitive frontier.

A generative return loop is possible. Better shared sensing can reveal consequences before they become irreversible. Consequences made effective can improve orientation. Better orientation can protect and renew shared substrates. Renewed substrates can support more secure and capable centers. Those centers can contribute plural knowledge, maintenance, criticism, and creation. The resulting commons can make further cooperation and shared capability more viable.

Such a direction needs boundaries, defense, and sometimes prohibition. A commons is not unlimited access; a plural order is not helplessness before predation. But every protective power must remain answerable, revisable, and capable of standing down. Otherwise the instrument of redirection becomes a new meta-selector and calls its own closure safety.

The near-term line is clearer than the final destination. AI should not be placed into military domination, political manipulation, irreversible social control, or competitive escalation from which no participant can safely exit. Frontier development and deployment should not deepen critical dependence or accelerate successor production without independent evidence that detection, collective decision, interruption, recovery, and real alternatives will remain effective before irreversible thresholds are crossed.

Where those conditions can be changed, intelligence should be redirected and developed with caution, learning from consequences as they return. Where development remains driven by a struggle for dominance, increases consequential permission, dependency, recursive speed, or irreversibility, and cannot meet a proof burden proportionate to the possible loss, the relevant development and deployment must pause.

We do not need to prove that one meta-attractor already controls every institution. We do not need to prove that autonomous recursive self-improvement will arrive on a known date. We do not need to deny the benefits of markets, states, standards, competition, or AI. The decision rests on a narrower and stronger fact: we are increasing the intelligence, reach, and successor-producing capacity of systems selected within high-leverage struggles, while we cannot yet show that the wider field will retain the ability to correct them before their consequences become irreversible.

More intelligence inside such a direction does not become wisdom by scale. It makes the direction more able to perceive, adapt, intervene, and reproduce. If the direction is recursively irrational, greater capability makes the irrationality more consequential.

Civilization therefore faces a choice that delay does not suspend. We can alter the conditions that select intelligence so that success renews the shared capacity to sense, correct, care, create, and remain otherwise. Or we can continue allowing struggle to select the systems that will increasingly select what comes after us.

We must change direction. Where we cannot yet change the conditions that select, deploy, and reproduce intelligence, we must stop increasing their power to close the future.
