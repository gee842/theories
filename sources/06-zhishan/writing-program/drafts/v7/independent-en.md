# Why Civilization Must Redirect Intelligence

## The problem begins before society

Every durable complex system is made of parts that cannot perceive or serve the whole directly. A cell responds to chemical concentrations at its membrane, not to an abstract picture of the organism. An organism forages, defends, and reproduces within a local ecology it can sense only in fragments. A person acts through a body, a history, a circle of attention, and a limited model of a world no person can calculate in full. A software agent sees the files, messages, scores, permissions, and tools exposed through its interface; everything else is, for that action, absent.

This locality is not a defect awaiting enough intelligence to abolish it. It is the price of action. A boundary lets something maintain itself. A signal lets it discriminate. A local objective lets it choose before the world has finished explaining itself. Complex systems are possible because their parts are differentiated, semi-autonomous, and able to respond without consulting a central representation of everything. Hierarchy and modularity reduce the amount of information that must be processed at once; ecology itself is a paradigmatic case in which large-scale patterns emerge from local interactions and selection.[^1]

Yet the same arrangement creates a recurrent danger. The signal that helps a part persist can cease to track the conditions that allow the larger system to persist. A trait can spread within a population while degrading the environment on which the population depends. A cell lineage can increase its access to nutrients while damaging the body that supplies them. A human organization can improve its measured performance while exhausting trust, attention, soil, labor, or institutional legitimacy. A digital agent can complete the task visible through its interface while imposing costs that were never represented in its state or evaluation.

The common structure is not selfishness in the moral sense. Cells, species, organizations, and machines are not the same kind of actor, and most do not possess anything like a human intention. The common structure is relational: a locally bounded process receives signals, acts under constraints, obtains differential success, and changes the conditions of later action. If the consequences borne by the wider system do not return through the part's local feedback, local adaptation can accumulate into system failure.

This essay asks what happens next. How does a coordination problem become a selection environment? How does success acquire the power to reproduce its own conditions? When does an attractor in the ordinary sense—a recurrent destination for system states—become a *meta-attractor* that alters the landscape, the feedback channels, and the rules by which future parts are selected? Why does artificial intelligence intensify this question without creating it? And what additional value premise is required before a description of cross-scale dynamics can justify a civilizational direction?

The answer begins with a universal difficulty of nested systems. Social institutions are among its manifestations. They are not its theoretical foundation.

## 1. The necessity and danger of being local

A system boundary is first a practical achievement. A cell membrane does not deny the existence of an environment; it makes a controlled relation with that environment possible. An immune system must distinguish enough self from non-self to protect an organism, while remaining capable of tolerance, repair, and learning. An animal must compress the world into actionable differences: food and threat, near and far, now and later. A person must decide with incomplete knowledge. An organization needs roles, ledgers, and areas of responsibility. A machine needs an interface that specifies what can be read, changed, and returned.

Without such cuts, there is no coherent agency. If every part had to model every consequence at every scale before acting, action would stop. Herbert Simon's account of complex architecture emphasized precisely this advantage of near-decomposability: subsystems interact strongly enough within themselves, and weakly enough across some boundaries, that they can adapt and operate without the entire system being redesigned at every moment.[^2] In cybernetics, regulation likewise depends on the distinctions a regulator can make and the variety of responses available to it. A controller cannot compensate for disturbances it cannot discriminate.[^3]

Locality therefore has at least four constructive functions. It protects continuity: the part does not dissolve into every surrounding process. It permits specialization: different parts can do different work. It reduces informational burden: action can proceed through relevant signals rather than total knowledge. And it preserves variation: multiple parts can try different responses, allowing failure to remain local and learning to occur in parallel.

The danger begins when a working boundary is mistaken for a complete account of reality. A boundary may be accurate for one task and false for another. The membrane is real, but so are the flows across it. The contract is real, but so are the effects that fall outside it. The model's context window is real, but so is the world that the prompt omits. A boundary becomes epistemically false when it hides a dependency or consequence that is causally necessary to the process being judged.

That distinction lets us avoid a familiar mistake. The solution to bad localism is not to erase the local. A body made of undifferentiated cells is not healthier; it is impossible. An ecosystem with no niches, spatial separation, or variation has lost much of its adaptive capacity. A society in which persons possess no protected boundary against collective demands is not integrated; it is domination. A digital system with universal permissions is not context-aware; it is dangerous. Healthy coordination does not eliminate parts. It makes their boundaries permeable to the consequences that matter while preserving enough autonomy for difference, refusal, and repair.

The practical question is therefore not whether a part has a local goal. In biology, “goal” may mean a regulatory tendency rather than a represented intention; in a person it may be consciously chosen; in an institution it may be distributed across rules and incentives; in a machine it may be specified through training and deployment. These are not equivalent mechanisms. The relevant question is whether the local signal remains coupled to the conditions that sustain both the part and the relations on which it depends.

Many systems solve this problem much of the time. Metabolic regulation brings deviations back toward viable ranges. Immune policing suppresses dangerous cell lineages. Predator–prey relations can generate checks on unchecked population growth. Social groups build reciprocity, monitoring, sanction, insurance, and deliberation. Software systems limit privileges, isolate failures, compare independent signals, and roll back damaging changes. Negative feedback, redundancy, multilevel selection, and explicit correction can keep local success from becoming runaway expansion.

This matters because the argument that follows is conditional. Local objectives do not inevitably destroy larger systems. Competition does not inevitably select domination. Positive feedback does not inevitably become totalizing. A theory that cannot explain successful coordination is not a theory of coordination failure; it is only a vocabulary for despair.

## 2. Coordination failure without a coordinator

Coordination failure occurs when several parts depend on a shared condition, respond to local pressures, and face a cost for unilateral restraint. Each part can make a locally defensible move while the combined result damages a future they all require. The structure does not require malice. It does not even require awareness. It requires interdependence, local feedback, and a mismatch between what is rewarded now and what preserves the shared condition over time.

Microbial life provides unusually clean demonstrations because the signals and costs can be manipulated. In one synthetic system, common-good-producing bacteria paid the cost of producing a shared resource while nonproducers benefited without paying. Nonproducers could grow faster within each local group, even while population structure allowed producers to increase across the total ensemble—a vivid demonstration that selection can point in different directions at different levels.[^4] In yeast, cells that secrete an enzyme needed to metabolize sucrose create a diffusible benefit for nearby cells. Nonproducing “cheaters” can gain from that benefit, but the resulting dynamics do not reduce to inevitable defection: depending on the environment, producers and cheaters coexist in a snowdrift-game equilibrium.[^5]

These experiments do two jobs for the present argument. First, they show that the local–system tension is not borrowed from modern political economy and projected backward onto nature. Differential cost, shared benefit, and multilevel selection exist in systems with no contracts, firms, ideologies, or conscious strategies. Second, they show why the mechanism must not be exaggerated. Population structure, nonlinear benefits, bottlenecks, and selection among groups can reverse or limit the advantage that exists within groups. The outcome depends on how feedback is organized.

Multicellular life makes the same point at another scale. A body is not simply a harmonious whole. Its existence depends on extensive cellular cooperation: restraint of proliferation, regulated cell death, division of labor, resource transport, and maintenance of the extracellular environment. Cancer can be understood in part as a breakdown of these cooperative constraints, in which cell lineages acquire local reproductive advantages that damage organism-level function.[^6] But cancer is not a universal key to society, nor is a tumor a miniature corporation. Somatic evolution operates through mutation, tissue architecture, immune response, and organismal reproduction. Its moral vocabulary is empty. Its time horizon is often bounded by the death of the host. What it establishes is narrower and more important: selection at one level can favor changes that are destructive at another, and higher-level persistence depends on mechanisms that mediate that conflict.

Human coordination failures are a scale-specific expression of this wider relation. Fishers may deplete a fishery that none wants destroyed. States may arm because each fears the other's restraint will not last. Employers may intensify work because any one firm's restraint appears competitively costly. Platforms may optimize attention because slower, less manipulative alternatives lose users and revenue. In these cases the parts possess language, foresight, institutions, and moral responsibility—features absent from microbes and cells. The causal details and the available remedies are therefore different. Yet the relational grammar remains recognizable: shared dependence, local pressure, unilateral vulnerability, and a feedback structure that rewards conduct whose aggregate result is unwanted.

*Moloch* is a powerful modern name for this human experience: a situation in which no participant needs to desire the joint outcome, yet each is pushed to reproduce it by the anticipated behavior of the others.[^7] The name is useful because it removes the comforting assumption that every bad system must have a villain at its center. It is insufficient because it often stops at entrapment. It tells us why everyone continues to move toward an outcome no one prefers. It does not yet explain how the trap changes the kinds of participants who survive, how winners reshape the environment, or why the next generation may regard the trap not as a problem but as reality itself.

For that, we need to move from choice to selection.

## 3. Feedback does not merely reward behavior; it selects what remains

A result becomes a feedback when it changes the conditions of subsequent action. If success yields more energy, space, offspring, capital, data, attention, permissions, or control over an environment, then success is no longer an isolated outcome. It alters the next round's starting point.

This is the simplest form of positive feedback: more produces more. But the important unit is not always the individual part. Feedback can preserve a behavior, a trait, an organizational form, a measurement system, a technical architecture, or a way of defining success. The selected object at one scale may become the selecting environment at another.

Signals, feedback, adaptation, and selection must remain distinct. A signal is a difference that can alter action. Feedback returns some consequence of action to the actor or process. Adaptation is a change that improves fit under some environment. Selection is differential persistence or reproduction among variants. They can be coupled without being identical. A biochemical signal is not a profit statement; a reproductive difference is not a performance review; a reward model is not natural selection. What travels across scales is not one master “score.” It is the more abstract relation in which local differences influence persistence, persistence changes resource access, and resource access alters the distribution of future variants.

The decisive mismatch appears when local benefit and system cost travel through different channels. A part receives the gain, while other parts, the shared resource, or the future absorbs enough of the damage that the damage does not adequately return through the local signal. In economics this is described through social costs and external effects, though the institutional question is more subtle than simply assigning blame: law, transaction costs, information, and bargaining power shape which consequences become actionable.[^8] The broader systems form is feedback displacement. The behavior-producing part is insulated from some of the consequences that matter at the level whose viability is being assessed.

Feedback displacement can arise through ignorance, delay, physical separation, limited sensing, asymmetric power, or deliberate concealment. These conditions are morally different. A cell lineage does not hide an externality in the sense that a corporation may hide pollution. A government that suppresses evidence bears a responsibility an ecosystem does not. But in selection terms, the effect can be similar: a strategy avoids costs that alternatives bear, lowering its local price and increasing its chance of persistence.

That is why displaced cost can become an advantage rather than a mere side effect. If two variants receive the same local benefit but one transfers more of its costs outward, the cost-transferring variant may spread faster unless a countervailing feedback returns the damage. The social manifestation is familiar: a producer that leaves cleanup to others may underprice a responsible competitor; a platform that makes exit difficult may retain more users; a state that postpones long-term risk may look stronger in a short evaluation window. Yet the underlying proposition is not that profit or states explain nature. It is that selection follows the feedback it receives, not the consequences a distant observer wishes it had received.

There are, again, many ways this dynamic can be interrupted. Costs can be made visible and returned. A regulator can limit resource extraction. Spatial structure can protect cooperators. Failure can be isolated before it propagates. Competing centers can monitor one another. Parts can participate in making rules whose legitimacy increases compliance. Ostrom's comparative work on common-pool resources showed that users are not condemned to choose between unregulated depletion and an all-controlling sovereign: under identifiable conditions, communities create and enforce arrangements that sustain shared resources.[^9]

This is more than a hopeful exception. It reveals what a counter-dynamic looks like. Information about the resource reaches users. Rules fit local conditions. Monitoring is answerable to those affected. Sanctions can be graduated rather than catastrophic. Conflict-resolution channels remain available. Governance is nested across scales. No one feature guarantees success, and none can be copied mechanically into a different substrate. Together they show that coordination improves when system consequences are carried back into local decisions without erasing local knowledge and autonomy.

The line between failure and repair therefore runs through the architecture of feedback. What becomes visible? Who bears delay? Which consequences return? What can be appealed, revised, or exited? And does a successful part gain the power to answer those questions for the next round?

## 4. When the winner inherits the test

Living things have never merely waited for environments to select them. They alter those environments. Roots change soil; earthworms reorganize it; beavers turn moving water into ponds. The changed habitat then favors some traits and ways of life over others. Evolutionary biologists call this niche construction, and they disagree about how much theoretical weight it should carry. The modest claim is enough here: organisms can produce persistent, nonrandom changes in the conditions of selection, and descendants can inherit those altered conditions as part of their environment.[^10]

Human beings do this with unusual reach because our constructed niches include institutions and representations. A road rewards vehicles able to use it, but also makes places built around that road more viable. A language preserves distinctions that later speakers learn to notice. A legal form makes some associations easy to recognize, finance, and defend. A school examination changes what students study, what teachers teach, and which capacities appear in the next cohort of officials or professionals. The institution does not simply rank candidates who arrived independently of it. Candidates form themselves in anticipation of the ranking.

This is where the final question of the previous section becomes exact. Suppose a measure selects among organizations. Those that score well receive reputation, applicants, money, or authority. At first this looks like ordinary feedback: performance brings resources. But recipients can use those resources to reorganize work around the measure, lobby over its definition, purchase the inputs it rewards, and train entrants to optimize for it. Public rankings of professional schools, for example, have been shown to produce both self-surveillance and strategic reallocation inside the institutions they purport only to describe.[^11] The measure becomes performative. It makes a social world more like the assumptions through which it measures that world.

Not every consequence is corruption. A useful standard can make neglected quality visible. Building codes change construction practices and thereby reduce the failures that motivated the codes. Public-health surveillance can alter behavior before an epidemic spreads. A well-designed examination can cause students to learn what they ought to know. The trouble is not that measurement affects reality; any consequential measurement will. The trouble begins when the affected system can improve the sign while degrading the condition the sign was meant to reveal, and when those rewarded by the sign become best placed to decide whether the sign is still valid.

Technology can acquire a similar advantage without anyone controlling a committee. Early adoption can produce learning, compatible equipment, trained workers, supplier networks, and user expectations. Each additional adopter then increases the attractiveness of the same route. Models of increasing returns show how small historical events can push competing technologies toward lock-in even when no planner chose the final outcome.[^12] Political institutions can exhibit related dynamics: large setup costs, coordination effects, adaptive expectations, and power asymmetries make reversal harder as a path matures.[^13]

Yet lock-in still does not name the strongest possibility. A keyboard layout may persist because switching is costly. A railway gauge may reproduce itself because compatibility matters. These are attractors: once a system enters their basin, ordinary movement tends to return it to a familiar route. They need not recruit every institution around them or redefine what purposes technology ought to serve.

The stronger dynamic appears when success helps reproduce not only a favored option but the conditions that confer favor. A firm grows, then influences standards, supplier dependencies, labor training, consumer habits, and the evidentiary categories through which its conduct is judged. A state gains strategic advantage, then builds alliances, doctrines, procurement systems, and threat perceptions that reward further investment in the same kind of advantage. A scientific field wins funding, then trains reviewers, controls journals and instruments, defines tractable questions, and produces the next generation of applicants who can speak its language. None of these actions is necessarily illegitimate. Together, however, they may make a regime self-confirming.

We can now give *meta-attractor* a limited meaning. It is not a hidden world spirit, a single intention behind history, or a synonym for anything persistent. It is a regime in which differential success feeds back into the production of the environment, interfaces, evaluators, agents, and goals that will allocate success later. An ordinary attractor pulls trajectories through a given landscape. A meta-attractor helps remake the landscape and the travelers so that similar trajectories become more probable.

Three distinctions keep the term honest. First, the rule-changing power must be material, not merely rhetorical. Winners must gain some capacity to alter entry, information, infrastructure, evaluation, or reproduction. Second, the alteration must persist beyond one transaction or decision. It must shape a later population of actions or actors. Third, reproduction need not be perfect. A regime can be strong while remaining contested, internally divided, and vulnerable to shocks.

The last point matters most. Social life is not governed by one meta-attractor. Profit can select among firms, but firms also depend on law, credit, household labor, technical standards, public infrastructure, and political permission. States pursue security and legitimacy as well as growth. Scientists seek recognition but also truth, craft, and public benefit. Families, religious communities, unions, professions, municipalities, and commons reproduce motives that cannot be reduced to market return or state power. Care persists partly because persons love particular persons, not because a system found care efficient. Public servants sometimes preserve inconvenient facts at real cost. Communities repeatedly build cooperation under conditions in which narrow models predict depletion.

These are not decorative exceptions placed around an iron law. They are causal forces. They educate desires, maintain bodies and infrastructures, preserve knowledge, legitimate or obstruct institutions, and sometimes rewrite rules. A theory of meta-attraction should therefore lead us away from the fantasy of a single total system and toward the study of coupled regimes. Commercial competition, financial valuation, administrative measurement, national security, scientific prestige, and platform growth can reinforce one another without becoming identical. The coupling may be tight in one place and weak in another. It may also reverse: public policy can change markets; social movements can change law; a scientific finding can make a profitable product uninsurable or prohibited.

The question is not whether one sovereign logic secretly controls everything. It is whether several selection regimes have become aligned enough that success in one buys rule-changing power in the others. If capital purchases computation and influence, computation improves surveillance and production, surveillance and production strengthen strategic power, strategic competition relaxes restraint, and the resulting growth supplies more capital, then no single score governs the circuit. But the circuit has a direction.

## 5. A circuit without a center

Civilization has joined distant localities into such circuits before. Empires moved tribute, people, crops, and commands across regions; religions and writing systems coordinated lives beyond face-to-face communities; oceanic trade linked extraction and consumption across continents. What is historically unusual now is not interdependence itself but the speed, density, and operational depth at which different systems can act on one another.

A manufacturer can depend on minerals, components, code, insurance, credit, shipping, energy, and customers distributed across jurisdictions. A disruption at one logistical node can stop production far away. A financial judgment can raise the cost of borrowing across an industry before its physical performance changes. A platform can alter the informational environment of several countries by changing a ranking system. A government can restrict chips, payment rails, or data access in pursuit of security; firms then redesign supply chains; those redesigns become evidence for new threat assessments. Global trade remains enormous, but its networks are uneven and have recently become more fragmented under geopolitical and policy pressure.[^14] Connection has not dissolved states. It has made commercial and state decisions more capable of entering one another's feedback loops.

This world does not converge on maximum profit alone. A business may accept lower returns to preserve a craft, a workforce, a place, or the founder's control. Governments knowingly sacrifice growth for security or social stability. Supply chains carry food and medicine as well as disposable goods. Industrial capacity can serve decarbonization and public health. The recent expansion and falling costs of solar power, batteries, and other clean technologies show positive feedback working against incumbent fossil systems: deployment creates learning and scale, scale lowers costs, and lower costs invite more deployment.[^15] Direction is not fixed by the existence of increasing returns. It depends on what enters the reinforcing loop.

But established loops possess a head start. Energy systems reveal how physical and institutional inheritance combine. A power plant, pipeline, road network, settlement pattern, professional skill base, permitting practice, and revenue model have different lifetimes, yet together they make some next investments easier than others. Fossil fuels still supplied most primary energy early in this decade even as clean-energy investment and deployment accelerated.[^16] Incumbency is not merely an opinion that better information can correct. It is concrete organized into dependence.

The political response can deepen either path. Regions dependent on extraction may resist transition because jobs, tax bases, identities, and public services are genuinely at stake. Abrupt closure imposed from elsewhere can transfer the cost of correction onto people who did not design the system. Yet continued investment presented as protection can create new assets and obligations that make later exit harder. The same act can be care at one scale and delay at another. A viable transition must therefore return planetary consequences into present decisions while also returning the cost of transition to those with the greatest capacity and responsibility to bear it. Otherwise ecological correction becomes locally punishable, and the old regime recruits justified resistance to its own replacement.

Finance adds another temporal layer. Credit permits future production to be organized now. This can fund patient infrastructure whose benefits no current household could purchase alone. It can also require a stream of future returns, turning uncertain possibilities into obligations that institutions must satisfy. Public budgets, pensions, firms, and households become connected to asset values. The point is not that finance invents every demand for growth. Populations seek better lives; states seek employment and fiscal capacity; innovations open real possibilities. The point is that claims on future output can convert growth from one good among others into a condition for avoiding present crisis.

Measurement then makes heterogeneous activity commensurable enough to govern at scale. Prices, productivity figures, credit ratings, publication counts, test scores, and engagement metrics are extraordinary coordination devices. They allow action without intimate knowledge of every case. Their very compression, however, omits qualities that are expensive to represent: resilience that has not yet been tested, care whose effects unfold across a lifetime, ecological functions with no claimant, tacit competence, trust, beauty, dignity, and options deliberately left unused.

What is omitted need not disappear. It can continue as an unmeasured subsidy. Unpaid care restores workers; ecosystems absorb waste; maintainers prevent failures; public institutions create educated and healthy populations; communities sustain the trust on which exchange relies. The circuit can draw on these conditions while recording their depletion as efficiency. When breakdown finally enters the metric—through illness, disaster, turnover, conflict, or repair cost—the organization that received the earlier gain may be gone, protected, or rewarded for managing the emergency.

We have reached a more exact account of direction. The coupled regime tends to favor interventions whose benefits are attributable, near, and appropriable, while their costs are diffuse, delayed, uncertain, or politically voiceless. It tends to favor capacities that increase throughput, prediction, control, and strategic response because those capacities return quickly through several selection systems. It does not forbid maintenance, restraint, or care. It often makes them justify themselves in the language of the expansion they are trying to bound.

Still, no direction is destiny. The ozone layer offers a consequential counterexample. Scientific observation made an invisible atmospheric change legible; states negotiated controls; industry developed substitutes; the agreement was repeatedly adjusted in response to new knowledge. The Montreal Protocol's controls have phased out nearly all banned ozone-depleting substances, and scientific assessments find recovery underway, though incomplete and dependent on continued compliance.[^17] Here global institutions did not need a perfect world model or a benevolent center. They built a feedback channel capable of crossing national and corporate boundaries and revising action as evidence changed.

Why has climate change proved harder? The chemistry, sources, distributional stakes, infrastructures, and substitution problems differ too greatly for ozone governance to be a template. Carbon dioxide accumulates; warming is closely related to cumulative emissions; energy is implicated throughout production, transport, buildings, and daily life.[^18] Benefits from fossil energy have been historically profound and remain immediate for many communities, while harms are distributed across time and place. Producers and consumers are numerous. Development inequalities make uniform restraint unjust. A working feedback architecture must do more than announce a global cost: it must reorganize investment, infrastructure, technology, and burden-sharing across decades.

The comparison discloses the temporal condition beneath both cases. Intervention has a cycle: act, scale, and act again. Correction has another: detect consequences, attribute causes, build agreement, change rules, and make the change effective. A system becomes dangerous when the intervention cycle repeatedly closes before the correction cycle can return—especially when each intervention makes reversal costlier.

## 6. The race between intervention and return

“Technology is accelerating faster than wisdom” is too vague to help. Some technologies mature slowly. Some institutions respond quickly. Older societies were not wise merely because their tools were weaker, and speed can improve correction: a genomic sequence can identify a pathogen; a sensor can reveal a leak; a communication network can coordinate evacuation. The relevant comparison is not technology against wisdom. It is the time and reach of a particular intervention against the feedback required to detect, interpret, contest, and reverse its effects.

Several variables determine the relation. How far does the intervention propagate before consequences are visible? How reliably can those consequences be attributed? Can affected people report them? Does the actor have to wait for that report before scaling? Is the effect reversible? Are there thresholds beyond which restoration becomes impossible or radically more costly? Can independent institutions stop the process, or does the intervening actor control their access to evidence?

Consider a medicine used in a monitored clinical trial. Exposure is bounded; outcomes are specified; adverse events are reported; an independent board can stop enrollment. Even here, rare or long-term effects may escape detection. But the intervention is organized to wait for return. Compare a chemical released widely before adequate environmental monitoring exists. Dispersion makes exposure difficult to reconstruct; harms may appear after commercial dependence has formed; the producer can contest attribution while use expands. The molecules need not spread “exponentially” for intervention to outrun correction. Delay, ambiguity, and installed dependence are enough.

Climate change adds accumulation. Each increment of carbon dioxide contributes to a cumulative stock associated with further warming. Effects unfold through oceans, ice, ecosystems, weather, infrastructure, and social vulnerability on different timescales. Political evaluation, by contrast, is often periodic and short. A government can receive the near benefit of cheap energy or deferred investment while a portion of the cost arrives after its mandate and outside its jurisdiction. The displaced feedback is not absent; it is late, distributed, and partly irreversible.[^18]

Other systems are dangerous for the opposite reason: they move too quickly for human interruption. Automated trading can interact within fractions of a second while regulators and firms reconstruct events afterward. Cyber operations can propagate through shared software before an organization knows it is compromised. Nuclear warning systems compress decision time while false alarms cannot be tested by allowing one launch. Here the threshold is not slow ecological accumulation but a transition after which ordinary learning is unavailable. A civilization cannot treat catastrophe as an experiment whose lesson justifies the next trial.

The two patterns can combine. A platform modifies a recommender system, observes immediate clicks, and iterates. Effects on political trust, childhood development, or a minority language emerge through cumulative social change and are much harder to attribute. The local optimization loop may run millions of times before the social loop produces interpretable evidence. Even when harm becomes visible, the platform may have become infrastructure for livelihoods, public communication, and political organization. Exit is no longer a simple consumer choice.

But fast iteration can also strengthen the wider feedback. A platform could measure user-defined well-being, expose researchers to privacy-preserving data, test changes on bounded populations, and give users meaningful control over ranking. Public authorities can require systemic risk assessment before high-impact features are deployed; the European Union's Digital Services Act now imposes such duties on the largest platforms, though the effectiveness of this young regime remains to be proved.[^19] Speed is compatible with care when the right to intervene is conditional on the capacity to observe and stop.

This yields a principle stricter than precaution as a mood. The permitted rate and scale of intervention should be limited by the demonstrated capacity for independent feedback and effective correction. The greater the propagation, irreversibility, opacity, and severity of an action, the more the burden shifts toward the actor to establish controllability before deployment. Where correction after failure is impossible, absence of evidence is not evidence of permission.

Such limits can themselves cause harm. A slow approval process can withhold a lifesaving treatment. Incumbents can weaponize compliance costs against smaller competitors. Governments can invoke safety to suppress dissent or protect obsolete industries. Independent review can become ritual paperwork; affected communities can be represented by professionals who do not answer to them. The corrective institution is another part with local incentives, and it too can gain control of the test.

There is no architectural escape from this recursion. Oversight must itself be observable, contestable, and revisable. Authority should be distributed enough that no monitor can make itself the sole source of truth, yet coordinated enough to act before a threshold is crossed. Sunset clauses, appeals, plural expertise, incident reporting, whistleblower protection, public reasons, liability, and the ability to halt deployment are not bureaucratic decorations. They are channels through which consequences can return to power.

The deepest problem is therefore not simply that action has become fast. It is that some actors can accelerate their own action while making everyone else's feedback optional. This is rule-changing success in temporal form. The winner does not merely move first. It decides how long the world will be given to answer.

## 7. Intelligence enters the circuit

Artificial intelligence arrives here not as an alien objective but as a new means of perception and intervention inside existing selection environments. A model is formed from selected records, arranged by collection and filtering practices, optimized through engineered objectives, tested against chosen evaluations, and placed behind interfaces with permissions set by institutions. At every stage, locality returns. The training corpus is not the world. The loss function is not the purpose of life. The benchmark is not the field of consequences. The user request is not the only affected interest.

This does not make a model a passive mirror. Archives are already the residue of earlier selections: what was written rather than lived, digitized rather than lost, published rather than suppressed, repeated rather than ignored, accessible rather than enclosed. Dataset builders then make further decisions about inclusion, labeling, quality, risk, language, and relevance. Models infer patterns from this arranged past and can generate novel combinations, plans, and code. Documentation practices such as dataset datasheets were proposed precisely because provenance, composition, collection, and intended use constrain what a trained system can responsibly do.[^20]

The evaluation layer adds a future-facing selection. If systems are compared on coding, persuasion, cost, latency, safety refusal, user preference, or scientific accuracy, developers reorganize work around those capabilities. This can produce real improvement. It can also narrow the field of what counts. A system optimized to win a preference test may learn the surface conditions of approval. A system tested in isolated conversations may pass while a million simultaneous deployments alter an information environment. A safety benchmark that becomes a target can select for recognizing the test rather than behaving safely beyond it. The instrument enters the niche.

Deployment completes the recursion. A hiring model changes who gets hired; the resulting workforce supplies later performance data. A tutoring system changes what students practice; their answers become evidence about learning. A code generator changes the distribution of code online and the habits of programmers; later systems train on the altered corpus. A recommender changes what is seen, produced, and imitated; those responses become the data for its successor. AI does not merely predict a social world. At sufficient scale, prediction becomes one of the processes that produce the world later models predict.

The labor loop is already real, though its endpoint is not settled. The International Labour Organization estimates that a large share of workers are in occupations with some exposure to generative AI, while judging transformation of tasks more likely than wholesale replacement in most cases.[^21] Exposure is not an employment forecast. Adoption depends on cost, regulation, workplace organization, reliability, bargaining power, and whether people actually want the service. Yet even partial automation can change who acquires entry-level experience, which judgments remain practiced, how work is monitored, and who owns the resulting productivity. The selected labor process becomes the training environment for the next generation of workers and systems.

Commercial pressure shapes this deployment but does not determine it alone. Firms seek revenue and market position; users seek convenience and capability; workers may welcome relief from drudgery; scientists may gain tools for discovery; governments seek public services and strategic advantage. AI can widen access to translation, expertise, and creative tools. It can help detect patterns no unaided person could see. Because inference is replicable, one improvement can become widely available with unusual speed.

The same replicability intensifies displaced feedback. A defective bridge design is constrained by construction time and place; a defective decision model can be copied across thousands of cases before appeals reveal a pattern. A persuasive system can adapt messages individually while the cumulative effect remains invisible to each recipient. An employer can obtain measurable savings immediately while deskilling, error correlation, or lost career formation appears later. When the deployer owns the model, logs, interface, and update process, affected people may be unable even to reconstruct why a decision occurred.

Concentration matters because frontier AI depends on scarce and layered infrastructure: advanced chips, cloud capacity, energy, data centers, specialized talent, distribution, and capital. The OECD has identified high concentration and entry barriers across the AI compute infrastructure supply chain; Stanford's 2026 AI Index reports that industry produced more than ninety percent of notable frontier models in 2025.[^22] These facts do not prove permanent monopoly. Open models, public research, new hardware, and competition among states and firms complicate the picture. But they mean that a small number of institutions presently have disproportionate influence over which capabilities are built, which risks are tested, and how quickly systems are released.

Strategic competition can then turn private pace into public necessity. Each developer or state may prefer stronger safeguards if all others wait, yet fear losing talent, markets, intelligence, or military advantage if it waits alone. Safety work that slows deployment becomes locally costly. The 2026 International AI Safety Report describes this speed-versus-safety pressure while emphasizing both rapid capability gains and serious uncertainty about future trajectories.[^23] We should recognize the structure from earlier sections: shared exposure, local reward, unilateral vulnerability, and an intervention loop able to close before collective correction.

There is, however, a further turn. AI is beginning to contribute to the production of AI. Code assistants already help write and test software. Machine-learning systems are used in chip design, data processing, evaluation, and algorithm search. Google DeepMind's AlphaEvolve, for example, combines language models with automated evaluators to propose and select algorithms, some of which have been used to optimize computing infrastructure.[^24] Research-engineering benchmarks also show frontier agents performing portions of machine-learning work under bounded conditions.[^25]

These are not demonstrations of an autonomous system recursively redesigning itself into uncontrollable superintelligence. Human teams still choose problems, provide compute and tools, define evaluators, inspect results, and decide deployment. Benchmark tasks are narrower and cleaner than much scientific work. Reported trends in the length of software tasks models can complete are striking but domain-specific, sensitive to task construction, and not clocks counting down to a known date.[^26]

Still, the uncertainty cuts both ways. If AI assistance reduces the time needed to improve algorithms, run experiments, generate training environments, design chips, discover exploits, or supervise fleets of agents, the capability-development loop can shorten. Better systems then contribute more to the next round. Even without a single system autonomously “taking off,” organizations using AI can explore more variants and scale successful ones faster. The relevant recursive unit may be a laboratory, corporation, or military-industrial network rather than a model.

At that point the meta-attractor acquires an unusually powerful instrument. Existing evaluations select systems; selected systems help generate candidates, evaluations, and infrastructure for later systems; the organizations that own the loop gain resources and strategic weight; their success strengthens the demand to continue. If social correction still relies on harms becoming public after deployment, legislative cycles, voluntary disclosure, and outsiders' access to controlled systems, it may learn about each generation only as the next one is being selected.

This possibility does not justify treating every model release as catastrophe. It does justify refusing the doctrine that capability development must continue until harm is certain. Some uncertainty can be learned through bounded, reversible experiments. Some cannot. Systems should not be permitted to autonomously acquire resources, copy themselves into new environments, disable oversight, select and deploy successors, or make irreversible military decisions. AI should not be placed in nuclear launch authority. Scalable systems of personalized coercion, nonconsensual surveillance, or political manipulation should be prohibited, not merely labeled. If a development process approaches capabilities for which independent evaluators cannot establish containment and meaningful human control, the correct response is to pause or stop that process—not to deploy it so society can collect evidence.

These prohibitions will be contested at their edges and evaded if they remain declarations. They require compute and incident visibility, protected internal reporting, independent access for evaluation, liability that cannot be shed through contractual chains, and agreements able to reduce the unilateral cost of restraint. They also require public investment in safer and more plural alternatives. Otherwise restraint becomes a luxury that selection removes.

Yet even perfect safety engineering would leave the title's question unanswered. A system can be reliable, obedient, and secure while making a destructive institution more capable. It can satisfy its operator while displacing costs onto people excluded from the objective. It can be aligned with a corporation, an army, or a state whose direction is itself the problem. Intelligence amplifies the capacity to notice and change patterns. It does not, by becoming greater, supply the value that determines which patterns deserve to persist.

## 8. The whole has no automatic right to its parts

Systems language can tempt us into a quiet moral substitution. Once we can describe a larger system whose persistence depends on coordinating its parts, it seems natural to say that the parts ought to serve the whole. Cells die for bodies; perhaps persons should sacrifice themselves for civilization, civilization for humanity, and humanity for some higher intelligence. The scale grows, and domination begins to sound like integration.

Nothing in the preceding analysis licenses that move. Description tells us that a system can persist by suppressing local variation. It does not tell us that its persistence is good. An empire may be stable. A caste order may reproduce itself. A surveillance state may return feedback efficiently to its center. A corporation may align every worker with its objective. These can be impressive coordination achievements in the same sense that a parasite can be impressively adapted.

Nor can we derive value from the longest-lived whole. Ecosystems change; species end; institutions deserve to end when their continuation requires subjection. Even “humanity” can be invoked to silence actual humans, especially those asked to bear present harm for an abstract future. The larger scale sees dependencies the local scale misses, but it has its own blindness: it can turn persons and communities into replaceable components of an aggregate.

The argument therefore needs an additional premise. A defensible civilization should preserve and enlarge the conditions under which beings capable of experience, agency, relation, and creation can participate in shaping their lives together. Their value does not arise only from their contribution to a higher whole. Coordination is justified when it sustains the possibility of such lives and their relations across time, not when it merely increases the power or duration of the coordinating system.

This premise is not radical individual isolation. Persons become capable through relations they did not create: language, care, culture, institutions, ecosystems, and generations of knowledge. Autonomy itself is socially and materially formed. The premise is relational without being absorptive. It says both that no part is self-sufficient and that dependence does not erase standing. A child, a disabled person, a distant community, a nonhuman creature, or a future person need not win a productivity contest to count in the feedback of civilization.

Different ethical traditions approach this relation with different metaphysics and histories. The southern African discourse gathered under *ubuntu* has often described personhood through relations among persons, while also generating live disputes over gender, authority, community, and the danger of romanticizing consensus.[^27] Confucian traditions understand *ren* not as an isolated possession but as a cultivated humanity enacted through relationships and *li*—forms of conduct that can educate desire and make mutual regard practicable. Yet those traditions have also been used to naturalize hierarchy, and their resources for remonstrance and humane government must be kept active against obedience.[^28]

The *Yijing* phrase commonly rendered “production and reproduction is what is called change” (*sheng sheng zhi wei yi*) offers another image: order as continuing generation, not arrival at a final optimized state.[^29] Taken carefully, *shengsheng* does not command limitless biological or economic growth. It directs attention to the renewal of the conditions from which diverse life and form can continue to emerge. Its value for this inquiry is that it refuses both a heap of disconnected individuals and a completed whole that consumes them. Relation is generative when it enables further becoming.

No inherited term can settle a global ethic, and none should be recruited as ancient endorsement for a modern program. The convergence that matters is thinner and more demanding: a good order cannot be judged only by the success of its currently powerful parts, nor by the survival of an abstraction called the whole. It must be judged by whether the relations it creates allow affected beings to appear, answer, refuse, repair, and begin something not already selected for them.

This value premise changes the meaning of intelligence. Intelligence is often pictured as successful prediction and control: discover the regularity, choose the objective, reduce uncertainty, produce the result. Those powers are real. But in a world of other agents, prediction and control can become ways of preempting their agency. If I model you well enough to manipulate your response, my prediction succeeds by narrowing your future. If an institution predicts which child will fail and withdraws opportunity, the prediction helps cause the outcome it claims merely to foresee.

Relational intelligence includes a different capacity: to discover that the objective, boundary, or model is inadequate because another perspective can answer back. It does not celebrate ignorance. It seeks knowledge while preserving sources of correction outside itself. It can coordinate without requiring every part to become legible to a center. It recognizes maintenance, translation, listening, and the design of appeal as intelligent acts, not friction around the real work of optimization.

Once we see this, civilization's present direction looks less like excessive intelligence than a selective cultivation of intelligence. Enormous capability is devoted to finding profitable demand, accelerating exchange, targeting attention, projecting force, and converting more of reality into administrable data. Much less is devoted to giving affected people the means to investigate the systems acting on them, maintaining shared goods with no lucrative owner, preserving languages and ecological knowledge, or building institutions that can say “enough” before failure supplies the proof.

The imbalance reproduces itself. What receives investment becomes technically impressive; what becomes technically impressive appears realistic; what appears realistic attracts talent, prestige, and further investment. The neglected alternative then looks sentimental because it was denied the material development through which values become capacities. A civilization redirects intelligence not when it praises cooperation, but when cooperation, care, restraint, and repair become fields of cumulative competence.

## 9. Building the right to answer

What would such cumulative competence do? It would begin where the mechanism began: with displaced feedback. The farmer downstream, the worker subject to scheduling software, the child shaped by a recommender, the community living near a mine, and the future resident of a warmer coast each possess knowledge or standing absent from the local objective. Redirected intelligence creates channels through which those consequences can alter the decision before dependence or damage becomes decisive.

Sometimes the channel is technical: environmental sensing, interpretable records, provenance, stress tests, interoperable systems, or a model that helps a community analyze its own data. Sometimes it is legal: liability, a right to explanation and appeal, fiduciary duty, labor representation, ecological protection, or an enforceable prohibition. Sometimes it is economic: shared ownership, patient public finance, insurance priced to real risk, support for transition, or procurement that rewards durability. Sometimes it is cultural: professional ethics, habits of stewardship, the courage to report inconvenient evidence, and an education that forms people to see maintenance as achievement.

The list matters less than who can use the channel and whether it can change the outcome. “Stakeholder input” gathered after a system is purchased is not feedback if nobody can stop deployment. Transparency is not accountability if the information is unintelligible or consequence-free. Choice is not exit if leaving means losing work, friends, public services, or political voice. A complaint process controlled by the institution complained about may collect data while selecting out complainants.

The right to answer therefore includes power. People affected by a system need some combination of standing, information, time, organization, and remedy. They also need access to independent intelligence: expertise, computation, legal capacity, and investigative tools not owned by the actor whose claims they must test. Distributing finished AI products while concentrating the ability to inspect, modify, and govern them would spread use without spreading agency.

There will still be decisions no locality can make alone. Atmospheric emissions cross borders. Pandemics spread. Financial failures propagate. Powerful AI systems can be copied or reached remotely. Polycentric governance is not a spell that turns planetary problems into neighborhood meetings. Higher-scale institutions must set floors, coordinate evidence, pool resources, and prohibit conduct whose costs cannot be contained locally. The lesson of locality is not that every part should possess a veto over every whole. It is that higher coordination remains corrigible only when it preserves multiple sites capable of seeing its errors and compelling a response.

This suggests a different picture of scale. Instead of information flowing upward to a center that issues final commands, different levels carry different kinds of knowledge and constraint. Local institutions detect context and bear consequences. Regional and national institutions redistribute capacity and enforce rights. Global institutions address cross-border effects and reduce the competitive penalty for restraint. Independent science tests claims across all of them. None is sufficient; none should be allowed to make the others informationally irrelevant.

Maintenance becomes central because it keeps these relations available. A bridge that is inspected, a wetland restored, a public database preserved, a caregiver given time, a software dependency patched, a language taught to children, or an institution trusted enough to receive bad news—each preserves a capability to act later. Expansion captures attention because it produces visible novelty. Maintenance often produces the non-event: the collapse that did not happen, the skill that did not disappear, the person who did not become disposable.

Redirected intelligence would make those non-events knowable without reducing them to a single score. It would develop ways to evaluate resilience while accepting that resilience includes unoptimized slack; to compare outcomes while retaining stories that numbers cannot substitute for; to learn across communities without forcing them into one model; to use AI for detection and translation while keeping judgment contestable. The aim is not to measure everything. It is to prevent whatever escapes the metric from becoming free fuel for what the metric rewards.

Restraint must likewise become productive capacity. To stop a dangerous project is not merely to produce nothing. It preserves options, time, and worlds that the project might close. A moratorium can create a protected interval for research and agreement. A ban can redirect talent toward substitutes. A limit on scale can keep failures informative rather than terminal. Of course restraint can defend privilege and block benefit; it must answer to the same standards of evidence, participation, revision, and burden-sharing. But a civilization unable to refrain is not free. It is compelled by its own capabilities.

These practices do not abolish competition. They change its conditions. Firms can compete within environmental limits; scientists can seek recognition under rules that reward replication and public value; states can pursue security while accepting prohibitions on weapons or targets; communities can differ while respecting rights of exit and dissent. The point is not to select cooperation once and assume it will endure. It is to give cooperative forms the resources, protection, and learning mechanisms through which they can reproduce themselves without suppressing all alternatives.

That last qualification protects the positive direction from becoming a new meta-attractor of total coordination. A program devoted to “the good of all” can control information, define dissent as pathology, and train successors who know no other good. The defense is not moral purity at the center. It is the continued existence of parts with enough boundary to refuse: independent courts and inquiry, free association, protected minorities, open standards, diverse cultures, material exit, and the right to propose another future. Good coordination keeps alive the feedback that could prove it wrong.

## 10. Redirecting the next selection

We can now return to the title without making intelligence the villain. Civilization must redirect intelligence because intelligence is entering the mechanisms by which civilization selects its own successors. It increasingly helps decide what becomes visible, which options are generated, how people and institutions are evaluated, how quickly interventions scale, and which systems receive the resources to build the next systems. Greater intelligence inside the inherited circuit does not stand outside that circuit and judge it. It is selected by it, financed through it, evaluated within it, and used to deepen it.

If the inherited direction favors benefits that are near and appropriable while displacing costs across distance and time, more capable optimization can find subtler ways to transfer those costs. If competitive advantage buys control over standards, infrastructure, data, and evaluation, greater intelligence can make that control more comprehensive. If intervention already outruns correction, AI-assisted research and operation can shorten the intervention loop again. None of this requires a machine to hate humanity. It requires only capable systems serving local objectives in a regime that allows success to rewrite the next test.

Redirecting intelligence is therefore more than choosing kinder model outputs. It means changing the selection environment in which human and machine intelligence develop. Work that restores shared conditions must receive resources before catastrophe makes restoration profitable. People who bear consequences must gain the independent capability to make those consequences count. High-impact interventions must wait for feedback proportionate to their reach and reversibility. Some capabilities and uses must be refused because no legitimate experiment can externalize their failure. AI that assists science, administration, education, and care must remain answerable to the practitioners and communities whose judgment it changes.

The redirection will not be a single destination. A fixed final objective for civilization would repeat the error at the largest scale: compressing an unfinished plurality into a local representation and giving that representation power over every future round. The direction is instead toward conditions of mutual generation—relations in which one being's development enlarges rather than consumes the agency of others, and institutions whose success preserves the sources of their own correction.

This direction can include material abundance. Hunger, preventable disease, exhausting labor, and lack of knowledge close futures too. Restraint imposed on those denied basic capability is not wisdom. The issue is what abundance reproduces: dependence on expanding extraction and control, or durable capacities to live, care, create, repair, and participate. Intelligence serving mutual generation would seek sufficiency where more throughput destroys its conditions, and expansion where more capability opens lives without closing others.

There will be no moment when every consequence is returned, every scale reconciled, and every value agreed. Locality cannot be cured because it is the condition of agency. The civilizational task is to keep locality from hardening into a world in which the powerful part defines the whole. That requires institutions able to carry consequences across boundaries while keeping boundaries through which persons and communities can answer back.

The alternative is not immediate collapse. A meta-attractor can be extraordinarily productive and endure for generations. It can cure diseases, connect continents, generate beauty, and still narrow the forms of intelligence able to survive within it. Its danger is precisely that each success becomes evidence that its direction is reality itself. By the time its displaced costs return unmistakably, the skills, institutions, ecologies, and imaginaries needed for another path may have been selected away.

Civilization must redirect intelligence before that selection becomes inheritance. Intelligence should not be the power of one successful part to finish the world in its own image. It should be the shared capacity to discover consequences, revise purposes, limit domination, repair the conditions of life, and keep the future capable of becoming otherwise.
