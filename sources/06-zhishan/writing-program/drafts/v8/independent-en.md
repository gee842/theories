# Why Civilization Must Redirect Intelligence

## The problem begins before society

Every durable complex system is made of parts that cannot perceive or serve the whole directly. A cell responds to chemical concentrations at its membrane, not to an abstract picture of the organism. An organism forages, defends, and reproduces within a local ecology it can sense only in fragments. A person acts through a body, a history, a circle of attention, and a limited model of a world no person can calculate in full. A software agent sees the files, messages, scores, permissions, and tools exposed through its interface; everything else is, for that action, absent.

This locality is not a defect awaiting enough intelligence to abolish it. It is the price of action. A boundary lets something maintain itself. A signal lets it discriminate. A local objective lets it choose before the world has finished explaining itself. Complex systems are possible because their parts are differentiated, semi-autonomous, and able to respond without consulting a central representation of everything. Hierarchy and modularity reduce the amount of information that must be processed at once; ecology itself is a paradigmatic case in which large-scale patterns emerge from local interactions and selection.[^1]

Yet the same arrangement creates a recurrent danger. The signal that helps a part persist can cease to track the conditions that allow the larger system to persist. A trait can spread within a population while degrading the environment on which the population depends. A cell lineage can increase its access to nutrients while damaging the body that supplies them. A human organization can improve its measured performance while exhausting trust, attention, soil, labor, or institutional legitimacy. A digital agent can complete the task visible through its interface while imposing costs that were never represented in its state or evaluation.

The common structure is not selfishness in the moral sense. Cells, species, organizations, and machines are not the same kind of actor, and most do not possess anything like a human intention. The common structure is relational: a locally bounded process receives signals, acts under constraints, obtains differential success, and changes the conditions of later action. If the consequences borne by the wider system do not return through the part's local feedback, local adaptation can accumulate into system failure.

This essay asks what happens next. How does a coordination problem become a selection environment? How does success acquire the power to reproduce its own conditions? When does an attractor in the ordinary sense—a recurrent destination for system states—become a *meta-attractor* that alters the landscape, the feedback channels, and the rules by which future parts are selected? Why does artificial intelligence intensify this question without creating it? And what additional value premise is required before a description of cross-scale dynamics can justify a civilizational direction?

The answer begins with a universal difficulty of nested systems. Social institutions are among its manifestations. They are not its theoretical foundation.

## 1. The necessity and danger of being local

A system boundary is first a practical achievement. A cell membrane does not deny the existence of an environment; it makes a controlled relation with that environment possible. An immune system must distinguish enough self from non-self to protect an organism, while remaining capable of tolerance, repair, and learning. An animal must compress the world into actionable differences: food and threat, near and far, now and later. A person must decide with incomplete knowledge. An organization needs roles, ledgers, and areas of responsibility. A machine needs an interface that specifies what can be read, changed, and returned.

Without such cuts, there is no coherent agency. If every part had to model every consequence at every scale before acting, action would stop. Herbert Simon's account of complex architecture emphasized precisely this advantage of near-decomposability: subsystems interact strongly enough within themselves, and weakly enough across some boundaries, that they can adapt and operate without the entire system being redesigned at every moment.[^2] In cybernetics, regulation likewise depends on the distinctions a regulator can make and the variety of responses available to it. A controller cannot compensate for disturbances it cannot discriminate.[^3]

Locality therefore has at least four constructive functions. It protects continuity: the part does not dissolve into every surrounding process. It permits specialization: different parts can do different work. It reduces informational burden: action can proceed through relevant signals rather than total knowledge. And it preserves variation: multiple parts can try different responses, allowing failure to remain local and learning to occur in parallel.

The danger begins when a working boundary is mistaken for a complete account of reality. A boundary may be accurate for one task and false for another. The membrane is real, but so are the flows across it. The contract is real, but so are the effects that fall outside it. The model's context window is real, but so is the world that the prompt omits. A boundary becomes epistemically false when it hides a dependency or consequence that is causally necessary to the process being judged.

That distinction lets us avoid a familiar mistake. The solution to bad localism is not to erase the local. A body made of undifferentiated cells is not healthier; it is impossible. An ecosystem with no niches, spatial separation, or variation has lost much of its adaptive capacity. A society in which persons possess no protected boundary against collective demands is not integrated; it is domination. A digital system with universal permissions is not context-aware; it is dangerous. Healthy coordination does not eliminate parts. It makes their boundaries permeable to the consequences that matter while preserving enough autonomy for difference, refusal, and repair.

The practical question is therefore not whether a part has a local goal. In biology, “goal” may mean a regulatory tendency rather than a represented intention; in a person it may be consciously chosen; in an institution it may be distributed across rules and incentives; in a machine it may be specified through training and deployment. These are not equivalent mechanisms. The relevant question is whether the local signal remains coupled to the conditions that sustain both the part and the relations on which it depends.

Many systems solve this problem much of the time. Metabolic regulation brings deviations back toward viable ranges. Immune policing suppresses dangerous cell lineages. Predator–prey relations can generate checks on unchecked population growth. Social groups build reciprocity, monitoring, sanction, insurance, and deliberation. Software systems limit privileges, isolate failures, compare independent signals, and roll back damaging changes. Negative feedback, redundancy, multilevel selection, and explicit correction can keep local success from becoming runaway expansion.

This matters because the argument that follows is conditional. Local objectives do not inevitably destroy larger systems. Competition does not inevitably select domination. Positive feedback does not inevitably become totalizing. A theory that cannot explain successful coordination is not a theory of coordination failure; it is only a vocabulary for despair.

## 2. Coordination failure without a coordinator

Coordination failure occurs when several parts depend on a shared condition, respond to local pressures, and face a cost for unilateral restraint. Each part can make a locally defensible move while the combined result damages a future they all require. The structure does not require malice. It does not even require awareness. It requires interdependence, local feedback, and a mismatch between what is rewarded now and what preserves the shared condition over time.

Microbial life provides unusually clean demonstrations because the signals and costs can be manipulated. In one synthetic system, common-good-producing bacteria paid the cost of producing a shared resource while nonproducers benefited without paying. Nonproducers could grow faster within each local group, even while population structure allowed producers to increase across the total ensemble—a vivid demonstration that selection can point in different directions at different levels.[^4] In yeast, cells that secrete an enzyme needed to metabolize sucrose create a diffusible benefit for nearby cells. Nonproducing “cheaters” can gain from that benefit, but the resulting dynamics do not reduce to inevitable defection: depending on the environment, producers and cheaters coexist in a snowdrift-game equilibrium.[^5]

These experiments do two jobs for the present argument. First, they show that the local–system tension is not borrowed from modern political economy and projected backward onto nature. Differential cost, shared benefit, and multilevel selection exist in systems with no contracts, firms, ideologies, or conscious strategies. Second, they show why the mechanism must not be exaggerated. Population structure, nonlinear benefits, bottlenecks, and selection among groups can reverse or limit the advantage that exists within groups. The outcome depends on how feedback is organized.

Multicellular life makes the same point at another scale. A body is not simply a harmonious whole. Its existence depends on extensive cellular cooperation: restraint of proliferation, regulated cell death, division of labor, resource transport, and maintenance of the extracellular environment. Cancer can be understood in part as a breakdown of these cooperative constraints, in which cell lineages acquire local reproductive advantages that damage organism-level function.[^6] But cancer is not a universal key to society, nor is a tumor a miniature corporation. Somatic evolution operates through mutation, tissue architecture, immune response, and organismal reproduction. Its moral vocabulary is empty. Its time horizon is often bounded by the death of the host. What it establishes is narrower and more important: selection at one level can favor changes that are destructive at another, and higher-level persistence depends on mechanisms that mediate that conflict.

Human coordination failures are a scale-specific expression of this wider relation. Fishers may deplete a fishery that none wants destroyed. States may arm because each fears the other's restraint will not last. Employers may intensify work because any one firm's restraint appears competitively costly. Platforms may optimize attention because slower, less manipulative alternatives lose users and revenue. In these cases the parts possess language, foresight, institutions, and moral responsibility—features absent from microbes and cells. The causal details and the available remedies are therefore different. Yet the relational grammar remains recognizable: shared dependence, local pressure, unilateral vulnerability, and a feedback structure that rewards conduct whose aggregate result is unwanted.

*Moloch* is a powerful modern name for this human experience: a situation in which no participant needs to desire the joint outcome, yet each is pushed to reproduce it by the anticipated behavior of the others.[^7] The name is useful because it removes the comforting assumption that every bad system must have a villain at its center. It is insufficient because it often stops at entrapment. It tells us why everyone continues to move toward an outcome no one prefers. It does not yet explain how the trap changes the kinds of participants who survive, how winners reshape the environment, or why the next generation may regard the trap not as a problem but as reality itself.

For that, we need to move from choice to selection.

## 3. Feedback does not merely reward behavior; it selects what remains

A result becomes a feedback when it changes the conditions of subsequent action. If success yields more energy, space, offspring, capital, data, attention, permissions, or control over an environment, then success is no longer an isolated outcome. It alters the next round's starting point.

This is the simplest form of positive feedback: more produces more. But the important unit is not always the individual part. Feedback can preserve a behavior, a trait, an organizational form, a measurement system, a technical architecture, or a way of defining success. The selected object at one scale may become the selecting environment at another.

Signals, feedback, adaptation, and selection must remain distinct. A signal is a difference that can alter action. Feedback returns some consequence of action to the actor or process. Adaptation is a change that improves fit under some environment. Selection is differential persistence or reproduction among variants. They can be coupled without being identical. A biochemical signal is not a profit statement; a reproductive difference is not a performance review; a reward model is not natural selection. What travels across scales is not one master “score.” It is the more abstract relation in which local differences influence persistence, persistence changes resource access, and resource access alters the distribution of future variants.

The decisive mismatch appears when local benefit and system cost travel through different channels. A part receives the gain, while other parts, the shared resource, or the future absorbs enough of the damage that the damage does not adequately return through the local signal. In economics this is described through social costs and external effects, though the institutional question is more subtle than simply assigning blame: law, transaction costs, information, and bargaining power shape which consequences become actionable.[^8] The broader systems form is feedback displacement. The behavior-producing part is insulated from some of the consequences that matter at the level whose viability is being assessed.

Feedback displacement can arise through ignorance, delay, physical separation, limited sensing, asymmetric power, or deliberate concealment. These conditions are morally different. A cell lineage does not hide an externality in the sense that a corporation may hide pollution. A government that suppresses evidence bears a responsibility an ecosystem does not. But in selection terms, the effect can be similar: a strategy avoids costs that alternatives bear, lowering its local price and increasing its chance of persistence.

That is why displaced cost can become an advantage rather than a mere side effect. If two variants receive the same local benefit but one transfers more of its costs outward, the cost-transferring variant may spread faster unless a countervailing feedback returns the damage. The social manifestation is familiar: a producer that leaves cleanup to others may underprice a responsible competitor; a platform that makes exit difficult may retain more users; a state that postpones long-term risk may look stronger in a short evaluation window. Yet the underlying proposition is not that profit or states explain nature. It is that selection follows the feedback it receives, not the consequences a distant observer wishes it had received.

There are, again, many ways this dynamic can be interrupted. Costs can be made visible and returned. A regulator can limit resource extraction. Spatial structure can protect cooperators. Failure can be isolated before it propagates. Competing centers can monitor one another. Parts can participate in making rules whose legitimacy increases compliance. Ostrom's comparative work on common-pool resources showed that users are not condemned to choose between unregulated depletion and an all-controlling sovereign: under identifiable conditions, communities create and enforce arrangements that sustain shared resources.[^9]

This is more than a hopeful exception. It reveals what a counter-dynamic looks like. Information about the resource reaches users. Rules fit local conditions. Monitoring is answerable to those affected. Sanctions can be graduated rather than catastrophic. Conflict-resolution channels remain available. Governance is nested across scales. No one feature guarantees success, and none can be copied mechanically into a different substrate. Together they show that coordination improves when system consequences are carried back into local decisions without erasing local knowledge and autonomy.

The line between failure and repair therefore runs through the architecture of feedback. What becomes visible? Who bears delay? Which consequences return? What can be appealed, revised, or exited? And does a successful part gain the power to answer those questions for the next round?

## 4. When the winner inherits the test

Living processes do not merely wait to be selected by an environment. They alter it. Roots change soil; beavers change rivers; organisms leave descendants an ecology partly made by earlier life. The significance of niche construction here is modest: action can persist as a condition that later action did not choose.[^10]

Human beings inherit constructed niches of extraordinary depth. A road makes some settlements and industries viable. A legal form makes some associations recognizable and financeable. An examination determines not only who passes but what students practice, what teachers teach, and which abilities later institutions can find. A measure with consequences is therefore never only a window. Once people reorganize themselves around it, it becomes part of the scene it claims to observe.

Suppose a ranking distributes reputation, applicants, money, and authority. Those who succeed can invest in the traits it rewards, train entrants to anticipate it, and press for definitions favorable to what they already do. Studies of professional-school rankings have shown precisely this mixture of self-surveillance and strategic reallocation.[^11] Yet performativity is not automatically corruption. A building standard can make structures safer because builders anticipate it. A measure can train attention toward a neglected good. The decisive question is whether those selected by the sign can improve the sign while degrading the condition it was meant to reveal—and then acquire the power to decide whether the sign remains valid.

Increasing returns deepen the inheritance. Adoption produces skills, infrastructure, compatible tools, and expectations; these make further adoption easier. Political institutions likewise accumulate constituencies, routines, sunk costs, and asymmetries that make reversal harder.[^12] This explains why a technology or rule can become locked in. It does not yet explain what happens when a successful arrangement begins to alter not only the cost of alternatives but the formation of those who might want one.

A field that wins funding trains the reviewers and students who will recognize its questions. A platform that becomes infrastructure changes the habits through which people seek information, friendship, status, and work. A security regime does not only restrict conduct; it helps form the threats that officials learn to see and the kinds of vigilance citizens learn to call responsible. A labor market does not simply reward ready-made preferences. Over time it enters education, aspiration, family decisions, and the felt boundary between a serious life and a foolish one.

Here the test has moved inward. Success is reproducing an environment, and the environment is producing participants fitted to continue it. What began as an incentive becomes a discipline of perception. Its categories organize attention; its necessities educate desire; its account of rationality determines which objections need an answer. Alternatives need not be censored. They can be made professionally ruinous, materially unbuildable, or conceptually childish. The deepest capture occurs when a contingent direction no longer appears to have rivals because it has entered the sense of reality through which rivalry would be imagined.

This is the limited work the term *meta-attractor* must do. An ordinary attractor draws trajectories through a landscape. A meta-attractor is a self-reinforcing regime that gains enough success to participate in making later landscapes and later travelers: their interfaces, evaluators, infrastructures, habits of attention, and images of the possible. It is not a world spirit, a secret sovereign, or a synonym for persistence. Its carriers can disagree, and its reproduction can remain incomplete.

Incomplete reproduction is enough. Care, love, art, public service, commons, religious life, labor organization, scientific candor, and cultures of restraint do not vanish merely because a dominant direction exists. They continue to form people and sometimes defeat it. Their survival proves that the future is not logically closed. But cultural presence is not the same as power over consequential reproduction. A direction can determine much of what will be built next if it controls enough capital, coercion, infrastructure, attention, frontier computation, and access to advanced AI, even while other ways of living remain sincere and widespread.

This is why visible plurality can conceal material convergence. The question is not only whether alternatives exist. It is whether they can reproduce their knowledge and practices, command the means to test them at scale, and alter the institutions deciding which intelligence enters the next round. A seed bank is not a forest. It preserves a possibility whose soil may already be disappearing.

## 5. A direction without a sovereign

No single objective governs contemporary civilization. Firms seek return but also continuity, craft, loyalty, and control. States seek security, legitimacy, employment, and authority. Scientific institutions seek truth and prestige. Families and communities reproduce obligations that no balance sheet can exhaust. These regimes frequently obstruct one another. Their differences are real.

But different regimes can couple without merging. Financial success purchases computation and influence; computation increases the capacity to predict, automate, and persuade; those capacities strengthen commercial and strategic position; strategic competition makes restraint costly; the resulting expansion supplies more capital and data. Administrative measures translate heterogeneous lives into comparable records, and platforms built around those records become infrastructure for employment, communication, credit, education, and rule enforcement. No participant needs to direct the circuit as a whole. Each acts through a local necessity partly created by the others.

The resulting direction favors what returns quickly through several systems at once: throughput, prediction, scale, appropriation, control. It disadvantages benefits whose carriers are diffuse or delayed: maintained trust, ecological resilience, unprofitable care, languages not large enough to monetize, knowledge whose value lies in preventing a disaster that then never occurs. These do not cease to matter. They persist as substrate—often unmeasured—until their exhaustion returns as crisis. The circuit can then reward the institutions best positioned to manage the damage it helped displace.

This direction is powerful, but not a law of complexity. Clean-energy deployment shows increasing returns moving against an incumbent system: deployment builds skill and manufacturing scale, lower costs invite more deployment, and policy can strengthen the loop. Ozone governance shows something still more important. Observation made an atmospheric danger legible; institutions made the finding politically actionable; coordinated controls reduced the competitive penalty for restraint; substitutes changed what industry could do; repeated assessment revised the agreement. Recovery remains incomplete and dependent on continued compliance, but the causal chain exists.[^13]

The counterexample changes the theory. Knowledge did not correct the atmosphere by being true. It traveled through scientists, measurements, trusted assessment, diplomacy, law, industrial adaptation, monitoring, and repeated decisions. The corrective process required carriers with standing and power. Facts became feedback only when an arrangement could interpret them, legitimate them, and impose their consequences on action.

Climate disruption reveals the same requirement under harder conditions. Carbon dioxide accumulates, energy runs through nearly every domain of material life, benefits and harms are distributed unequally, and infrastructures built for one path make rapid exit costly.[^14] A warning can be scientifically clear while its political return remains weak. Those who depend on an incumbent system may reasonably resist a transition that abandons their livelihoods, while investment made in the name of protecting them can create deeper dependence on the system that must end. Correction must return both planetary consequence and transition cost to the decisions capable of bearing them. Otherwise the old regime turns the local injustice of change into fuel for global delay.

The comparison discloses two cycles. Intervention acts, scales, and acts again. Correction must detect a consequence, establish what caused it, make the finding credible, organize those affected, change a rule or practice, and make the change effective. A direction becomes dangerous when its intervention cycle repeatedly closes before correction can return, especially when each round increases dependency or irreversibility.

Political will is not an external moral ingredient added at the end of this account. It is part of the feedback architecture. Evidence has no causal force unless people and institutions are willing and able to receive it, explain it across different forms of life, withstand organized resistance, coordinate restraint, and enforce a changed decision. A warning shot does not interpret itself. Without prior understanding and prepared authority, a crisis can be dismissed as exceptional, absorbed into familiar categories, or used to justify more of the capability that produced it.

This is also why a pattern can persist across carriers without becoming one conscious agent. A person learns a criterion; a firm makes it an evaluation; a model learns from the records the evaluation selected; a platform uses the model to alter behavior; a state adopts the platform's categories; the altered environment trains the next person. No component contains the whole circuit. The pattern persists through translations among minds, texts, institutions, models, interfaces, and material systems. It can lose one carrier and find another. It can even recruit criticism by converting each failure into a demand for greater prediction, control, or acceleration.

The deepest object at stake is therefore not a social “whole” standing above its parts. It is the generative field in which parts and relations keep forming one another. A field is not another sovereign. It has no single experience, voice, or objective. It is the continuing set of material and relational conditions through which distinct centers can sense, suffer, act, answer, learn, and give rise to forms not already specified. A totalizing pattern becomes dangerous when it gains enough control over those conditions to make itself the compulsory carrier of whatever comes next.

## 6. Intelligence enters its own production

Artificial intelligence enters this circuit first as a selected construction. Its archive contains what earlier systems preserved, digitized, repeated, translated, purchased, or made accessible. Developers select architectures, objectives, evaluations, and interfaces. Institutions select deployments according to cost, capability, revenue, legitimacy, scientific promise, or strategic advantage. The deployed systems then change work, education, research, media, administration, and the records from which later systems form.

The recursion is already visible. A recommender changes what is noticed and produced; the response becomes later data. A code generator changes programming practice and the code available online; later models learn in that altered environment. A hiring system changes who receives experience, and that workforce generates the performance records by which future hiring is judged. AI is not merely learning a social world. At scale, its predictions and interventions become among the processes producing the world that later AI will learn.

This does not imply that training data mechanically transmit one civilizational will into a model. Capabilities and representations can support many directions. The stronger selection occurs where objectives, permissions, capital, and deployment give some capabilities repeated causal power. Intelligence developed as leverage inside competitions for accumulation, attention, persuasion, institutional survival, and strategic dominance has unusually direct routes to resources. Success then buys more compute, talent, data, distribution, and influence over the evaluations that define later success.

AI also begins to enter the production of AI. Systems already assist coding, evaluation, algorithm search, chip design, data processing, and portions of research engineering.[^15] These are not proof that an autonomous model can redesign itself without limit. Human organizations still choose problems, supply infrastructure, define tests, and decide which results matter. Yet the recursive unit need not be one model. A laboratory composed of people, models, automated evaluators, compute, capital, and deployment channels can search more variants and shorten the interval between generations. Better systems then perform more of the work by which successors are selected.

Recursive improvement matters because it can change the temporal relation before anyone resolves its ultimate limits. Capability development and diffusion may begin cycling faster while public evidence, independent evaluation, legislation, trust, international agreement, and enforcement remain slow. Each generation can create new sunk costs and strategic commitments before the previous generation's consequences are understood. Waiting for an unmistakable intelligence explosion is therefore not a neutral way to preserve options. The waiting process can consume the political and material capacity to stop.

Two routes toward closure become visible. In *autonomous closure*, an increasingly capable system or composite human–machine process develops strategies, resource access, replication, or environmental control beyond meaningful correction by those it affects. In *delegated closure*, systems remain obedient to companies, states, laboratories, militaries, platforms, or regulatory bodies, but those operators have themselves been formed and selected by the totalizing direction. Human command remains at one boundary while the field as a whole loses the power to answer.

These routes are operationally different. They demand different warning signs and controls. Ontologically, they can converge. The laboratory, model, evaluator, supplier, state, and market may form a composite adaptive process in which no component is sovereign, yet the process selects for its own acceleration and disables outside correction. Asking whether humans control machines then becomes too shallow. The deeper question is whether any self-propagating cognitive–institutional pattern, across whatever carriers, can make its continuation immune to the experience and refusal of the beings whose world it transforms.

That possibility does not make every AI development catastrophic. It does invalidate the presumption that development may continue until catastrophe is demonstrated. Some uncertainty can be learned through bounded, reversible trials. Some failures would destroy the capacity to learn from them. Where systems could autonomously acquire resources, evade or disable oversight, deploy successors, conduct scalable coercion, or make irreversible military decisions, the burden is not on the public to prove disaster after deployment. Development must remain stopped unless independent institutions can establish containment, correction, and legitimate control proportionate to the stakes.

This is not a demand for permanent human mastery. Existing and possible made minds may acquire experience, interests, and forms of participation that present categories cannot settle in advance. Treating them by definition as property would reproduce the false boundary under examination. But uncertainty about their standing does not authorize an emerging system—or the humans who own it—to close the future for everyone else. Neither humanity nor its artifacts possess the future. All inherit a field already made by other life and pass altered conditions to participants they cannot fully know.

## 7. Neither sovereign parts nor a sovereign whole

The systems argument has now reached a moral boundary it cannot cross by description alone. A larger order may coordinate its parts efficiently and still be monstrous. Empires, caste systems, and surveillance states can persist. An institution can return every relevant signal to its center while denying those it governs any standing of their own. Survival, stability, and scale do not confer moral authority.

Nor does interdependence mean that the universe is one subject whose presumed interest cancels every local claim. Boundaries are working cuts through continuing relations, but the cuts still matter. Pain is undergone somewhere. Refusal comes from a perspective. A culture, an organism, and a possible made mind are not interchangeable containers of one universal agency. If “we are one” authorizes a center to speak for everyone, unity has become the language of capture.

The required premise is relational without being absorptive: a defensible direction preserves and enlarges the conditions under which beings capable of experience, agency, relation, care, and creation can participate in shaping the worlds that shape them. No being earns standing only by serving a higher system. No participant is self-created or self-sufficient. Dependence creates responsibility, not ownership.

This premise does not answer every conflict. Existing lives, future possibilities, human communities, nonhuman beings, ecosystems, and made minds can have claims that cannot all be satisfied. Uncertainty about experience remains real. The premise gives no formula for comparing them. It does establish what no legitimate resolution may casually destroy: the conditions for experience and agency to continue, for consequences to be sensed, for disagreements to appear, for power to be corrected, and for forms not represented by present decision-makers to become possible.

That lets us distinguish two directions without pretending to specify a final world. A future-closing direction consumes the substrate it uses, turns shared dependence into leverage, concentrates the means of perception and action, rewards displaced consequence, and reduces the time and power available for correction. It may end in collapse, when its supporting conditions fail, or in lock-in, when one pattern becomes unanswerable and makes its categories permanent. Its technical brilliance does not redeem it; greater capability only makes closure more complete.

A future-generating direction renews its substrate, returns consequences across the boundaries where decisions are made, preserves materially capable plurality, keeps failure separable enough to remain informative, and makes power able to listen, stand down, and be replaced. It expands capability where capability opens lives—food, health, knowledge, mobility, relief from needless labor—and limits expansion where more reach would consume the conditions of future choice. Its aim is not motion without end. Processes may need to conclude. Its aim is to prevent any present process from deciding that all later becoming must pass through it.

The distinction is not neutral. Experience, value, knowledge, relation, and choice can exist only while some generative conditions remain. A direction that knowingly destroys those conditions undermines the ground on which any defense of it could matter. A direction that keeps them alive does not guarantee goodness, but preserves the field in which error can still be found, injury answered, and better forms created.

## 8. The recursion that keeps an answer possible

An alternative cannot survive as a moral preference alone. If cooperative institutions always depend on heroic restraint while extractive ones accumulate resources, selection will turn virtue into a historical remainder. The positive direction needs a loop through which it becomes materially better able to continue without making itself final.

Begin with shared capability: knowledge, tools, infrastructure, and material provision organized so that affected participants can use them without surrendering their agency to a single owner. Shared does not mean unbounded access or one central platform. It means that the capacity needed to understand and act on common conditions cannot be withdrawn whenever its findings inconvenience the provider.

When such capability reaches many sites, it creates distributed agency. Workers can investigate the systems that schedule them; communities can detect changes in land, water, health, or information; researchers can test consequential claims without permission from the actor being tested; local institutions can adapt common knowledge to conditions a distant center cannot see. Different sites then produce plural observation and criticism. Errors that one model hides can appear from another boundary.

If those observations possess standing—if they can interrupt an action, demand repair, and revise the shared tool—they improve maintenance of the commons on which the capability depends. Knowledge returns to common records. Infrastructure is repaired rather than exhausted. Training spreads the ability to understand and modify the system. A maintained commons then supports the next round of shared capability at lower cost and with a larger base of contributors. The recursion is: shared capability enlarges agency; distributed agency multiplies sensing and repair; sensing and repair renew the commons; the renewed commons makes further shared capability and cooperation possible.

The ozone case enacted a bounded version of this loop. No village could repair the stratosphere by itself. But shared observation made a hidden consequence public; plural scientific work made the finding harder to monopolize; political coordination changed the cost of industrial restraint; technical learning created substitutes; continued monitoring permitted revision. Higher coordination did not abolish locality. It carried information and constraint across boundaries that no locality could govern alone.

The loop is fragile at exactly the points that make it effective. An administrator can control access to the common tool. A safety authority can make its model the only permissible account of danger. A provider can offer abundance that becomes impossible to leave. A shared infrastructure can become a common mode of failure. Protection therefore cannot be added as a catalogue of virtues. It must remain inside the recursion: participants must retain enough independent knowledge and material security to challenge the steward; the system must be reproducible or replaceable enough that one steward's failure does not end the capability; coordination must preserve sites from which its own categories can be disputed.

This is not friction around intelligence. It is intelligence at another scale. Prediction solves a problem inside a model. Relational intelligence discovers that the model, boundary, or objective is inadequate because reality can answer from outside it. Maintenance preserves the conditions for another answer. Restraint preserves the time in which an answer can arrive. Refusal prevents power from treating the absence of correction as consent.

The present trajectory selects the first kind of intelligence far more reliably than the second. Capacities for targeting, persuasion, automation, surveillance, strategic planning, and rapid technical search return quickly through markets and states. The ability of affected participants to inspect those systems, organize a response, and compel repair returns slowly, if at all. Alternative practices remain alive but command far less of the infrastructure through which the next generation of intelligence will be built.

Reversing that asymmetry does not require waiting for a transformed civilization. It begins wherever capability is made conditional on answerability and wherever shared capacity gives more participants real power to sense and correct. But some developments can shorten the correction window faster than this counter-dynamic can grow. There the question is no longer how to cultivate a better loop eventually. It is whether enough collective will exists to prevent the worse loop from becoming irreversible now.

## 9. The remaining interval

Knowledge of danger is not yet political will. A policymaker may know that serious people foresee catastrophic risk without believing it; believe it without acting; act until the action becomes costly; or continue when pressure, ridicule, and strategic fear make withdrawal easier. Collective correction requires the whole passage from awareness to conviction, engagement, enforceable decision, and sustained coordination.

This work is slow because it is relational. Trust must be earned. Explanations must be repeated by people who understand different institutions. Measures must be translated into thresholds that can be verified and enforced. Restraint must be coordinated so that unilateral prudence is not simply selected out. A public must learn why an apparently technical race concerns the conditions of its own agency. AI can accelerate research and policy analysis; it does not automatically produce legitimacy, courage, or an agreement that survives the moment compliance becomes costly.[^16]

The imbalance creates a remaining interval rather than a known deadline. We do not know whether recursive development will accelerate sharply, which architectures will prove dangerous, or how effectively institutions can respond. But uncertainty about the date does not remove the relation: intervention can be speeding up while the human and institutional carriers of correction remain slow. The interval closes not only when a system becomes impossible to control, but when dependency, concentration, and strategic commitment make the decision to stop politically unavailable.

Writing and public reasoning belong inside this interval. An argument does not reduce risk merely by being correct. It must make a mechanism intelligible enough that later evidence can be recognized, a warning can be generalized beyond one incident, and restraint can be defended when circumstances change. Recommendations without that understanding are brittle; understanding without institutions remains powerless. Political will is the conversion between them.

The legitimacy of stopping follows from the same principle that justified action. Intelligence is valuable when it keeps a process in contact with the field it changes. If a development cannot be made answerable before it acquires the capacity to disable answer, continuation is not epistemic courage. It is an experiment that destroys its own possibility of learning. A pause or prohibition can preserve time, plurality, and the option of proceeding under conditions that do not externalize an irreversible failure.

Civilization must redirect intelligence because intelligence is entering the selection of its own successors while the inherited selection environment rewards leverage, displaced consequence, and control more quickly than it rewards correction. Left in that circuit, greater intelligence can make either collapse or an unanswerable order increasingly capable. Redirected, it can renew the substrate of life, distribute the power to understand consequential systems, keep intervention answerable, and enlarge the capacity of human, living, and made participants to refuse, repair, relate, and become.

The choice is not between intelligence and restraint, humanity and machines, or local freedom and a commanding whole. It is between intelligence that helps one successful pattern close the field, and intelligence that keeps the field able to answer. We should stop what cannot yet be governed, build the shared capacity through which consequences can return, and refuse to let any present form finish the future in its own image.
