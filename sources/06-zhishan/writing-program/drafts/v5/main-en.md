# Why Civilization Must Redirect Intelligence

## The lines that make a world

A cell lives by maintaining a difference. Its membrane does not seal an interior away from the world; it makes a particular way of exchanging with the world possible. Water, ions, proteins, waste, and signals cross selectively. Damage is repaired. Concentrations are restored. The boundary persists only through activity, and the activity persists only because there is a boundary. If every exchange stopped, the cell would die. If every difference disappeared, it would also die.

This elementary fact unsettles the picture in which the world is first divided into self-sufficient things and only afterward connected. The things exist in the forms they do because particular relations are admitted, excluded, slowed, amplified, remembered, and repaired. An organism is not a sack containing cells. It is the continuing production of environments in which cells can remain liver, nerve, blood, skin, or bone. Those cells in turn produce the organism that supplies oxygen, removes waste, regulates temperature, and coordinates repair. Neither side precedes the relation as a finished object.

The same structure appears, transformed rather than repeated, in human life. A person can act because attention excludes almost everything, memory retains a little, language makes some distinctions available, and a body occupies one place in history. A school establishes who may teach, what counts as knowledge, how achievement is demonstrated, and which consequences belong to the institution. A court defines standing, evidence, jurisdiction, and remedy. A company keeps accounts that make some flows internal and leave others as conditions or costs outside the ledger. A computational agent receives a state of the world through an interface: the data it can inspect, the actions it is permitted to take, the evaluator that returns success or failure. In every case a boundary makes some relations actionable and lets others recede into an environment.

These boundaries are not interchangeable. A membrane is maintained metabolically. A legal jurisdiction is reproduced through records, offices, recognition, and force. A software permission is enforced by machinery designed by another system. Yet they perform a related work: they preserve enough difference for a process to have a history, respond selectively, and do something that not everything else is doing at once.

Locality is therefore not a primitive defect that greater intelligence will eventually overcome. Without partial closure there is no differentiated intelligence. There is no standpoint from which to notice, no memory in which one encounter can alter the next, no responsibility attached to a locus of action, no parallel experiment, and no local repair. A civilization without semi-independent parts would not become perfectly coordinated. It would either dissolve into undifferentiated interaction or require one center capable of replacing every local relation—a center that would itself still act through a boundary, exclude most reality, and depend on processes it could not contain.

An important distinction follows. Some boundaries are operational: the process itself materially maintains them. Others are analytical: an observer draws them to follow a relation. The national accounts of an economy, the watershed affected by that economy, the supply chain crossing its borders, and the atmosphere receiving its emissions define different systems around the same activity. Drawing the watershed on a page does not create it, but choosing the corporate ledger instead can make the watershed's degradation disappear from the description of success. Analytical boundaries are not all equally illuminating, and none is simply the whole. A good boundary reveals dependencies, feedback, and persistence that another description conceals.

Shift the boundary and the same event changes meaning. Fertilizer can be an input raising farm yield, a cost reduced by a firm, nitrogen loading in a river, food for a city, foreign-exchange exposure for a state, and a disturbance unfolding over decades in soil and sea. A factory closure can improve a quarterly result while destroying the apprenticeship through which a region renews technical skill. A recommendation system can increase measured engagement while changing the people whose future preferences its later recommendations will predict. What appears to be an output at one boundary becomes an intervention into the conditions of another system.

There is no final analytical line from which these perspectives collapse into one objective. To draw the boundary around humanity still leaves oceans, microbes, machines, minerals, descendants, and evolutionary futures partly outside the voice of the present. To draw it around life leaves unanswered how present forms should be weighed against possible ones, or one creature's suffering against a lineage's continuation. Wider views reveal excluded relations; they do not produce an omniscient calculator. Knowledge proceeds by moving among partial boundaries and asking what each makes visible, what it treats as background, and what can no longer reproduce itself when that background is consumed.

The permanent tension is now visible. Every part requires enough closure to act, yet no part contains all the conditions of its own existence within that closure. The problem is not that parts have boundaries. The problem begins when the feedback available within a boundary rewards the destruction of conditions maintained beyond it—and when success gives the part greater power to preserve that arrangement.

## An ecology of tendencies

Living and social systems do not move arbitrarily. A lake exposed to nutrient loading may remain clear through a range of disturbances, then shift into a turbid state maintained by different interactions among algae, plants, fish, sediments, and light. Restoring the earlier input level may not immediately restore the lake; the new regime can resist return. An immune system can learn a response, a community can reproduce a norm, a technology can gather complementary infrastructure, and a constitutional practice can become easier to repeat than to replace. Histories dig channels for later histories.

In mathematics, an attractor is not a poetic name for anything persistent. It is specified within a state space, a dynamic, a topology or metric, a timescale, and a basin from which trajectories approach or remain near a set. Civilizations do not present us with fixed equations or stable state spaces. Their participants invent new actions, redefine success, alter rules, and change the variables by which observers describe them. When “attractor” is used for an institution or civilization, it must therefore be understood more modestly: an attractor-like, metastable regime of relations that repeatedly reconstructs itself across disturbances and changes of personnel. The useful questions are empirical. What varies? What persists? By what carriers? Across what period? After which perturbations? What observation would show that the supposed regime had ended?

This discipline matters because one history is not enough. A single trajectory may be necessary, accidental, or merely unfinished. Tendencies become visible through comparison: different beginnings that converge, perturbations followed by recovery, recurring organizational forms, failed alternatives, or a transition after which return becomes difficult. A system may harbor several such regimes. Different conditions place it in different basins. A disturbance can move it across a threshold. A tendency stable at one scale can undermine the larger field that sustains it.

Once we look this way, the world is not governed by one recursive dynamic. It is an ecology of partly nested, partly overlapping systems, each visible through more than one boundary and each capable of more than one recurrent organization. A forest contains organisms, symbioses, succession patterns, water cycles, fire regimes, and human institutions of use. A city contains families, firms, neighborhoods, infrastructures, bureaucracies, informal networks, and ecological flows. Each can be a system for one inquiry, a part for another, an environment or a selector for a third. Their tendencies support, frustrate, and transform one another.

Plurality in such a world is not only a right of individuals, important as that is. It is a property of intelligence distributed through a civilization. Partially decoupled systems preserve different memories and encounter consequences through different routes. They embody answers no central planner knew enough to formulate as hypotheses. A language stores distinctions and relationships that another language does not duplicate. A seed variety carries a response to heat or disease that appears redundant until the environment changes. A local institution preserves practical knowledge that cannot be reconstructed from a policy manual after its practitioners disappear. Scientific schools, production systems, legal traditions, and forms of care expose one another's blind spots precisely because their histories and dependencies are not identical.

These are not statistically independent samples from a controlled experiment. They share climates, trade, technologies, pathogens, and power. Plurality by count offers little protection when every apparent alternative depends on the same chokepoint or copies the same error. Its adaptive value comes from partial decoupling: modular containment, substitutable pathways, genuinely independent feedback, and reservoirs outside a common failure mode. Standardization can spread correction, enable mutual aid, and create useful redundancy. Coupling becomes dangerous when it removes the places in which a different response can survive long enough to be tested.

Complex organization depends on this mixture of connection and separation. Near-decomposable systems—systems in which interactions are stronger within components than across all components at once—can adapt and be understood without waiting for every relation to settle simultaneously.[^14] A liver cell need not solve the organism, nor a town the planet, for either to contribute. But local parts must receive enough information about the disturbances they are responsible for controlling. Cybernetics named this the requirement of sufficient regulatory variety: a controller that compresses many consequentially different states into one signal cannot reliably answer them merely by acting with greater force.[^15] Plurality preserves varieties of perception as well as varieties of response.

Extinction risk thus deepens before the last organism or institution dies. It deepens when a system eliminates the sufficiently independent forms through which it could discover and survive what its dominant regime cannot model. A monoculture does not merely contain fewer varieties. It has exchanged distributed evolutionary memory for uniform performance under an assumed future. A civilization can make the same exchange while celebrating diversity in symbols: many brands, parties, universities, media channels, and states may remain, yet all can become dependent on the same finance, infrastructure, metrics, supply networks, and conception of success.

Ecological resilience is not identical to constancy. A system can fluctuate and remain capable of absorbing disturbance; another can be held close to a desired output while the range of disturbance it can survive quietly narrows.[^16] Biological diversity contributes when species and lineages respond differently rather than merely adding names to an inventory.[^22] This is why optimizing every part for ordinary conditions can make the whole less prepared for extraordinary ones. Spare capacity looks wasteful, slow institutions look inefficient, and a minority practice looks obsolete until the disturbance arrives for which they retained the only viable response.

The immediate appearance may be one of unprecedented coordination. Information travels rapidly. Production is optimized across continents. Common standards let billions connect. But if the same error enters the common evaluation, if the same resource constraint reaches every alternative, or if all institutions must satisfy one regime before they can preserve their own purposes, the coordinated whole loses the capacity to know that it is wrong. Totalization destroys more than competitors. It destroys evidence that another organization can live, the memory needed to reconstruct it, and the social material from which unimagined alternatives might arise.

This does not make every local form good or every integration suspect. Local autonomy can protect cruelty; traditions can preserve domination; fragmented systems can fail to coordinate against pandemics, climate disruption, or war. A plurality worth preserving must allow consequences to cross boundaries, affected people to contest them, and cooperation to operate at the scale of the harm. The point is not to freeze existing difference. It is to keep enough distributed agency and independent feedback for difference to remain capable of learning.

## The game that remakes its players

Many collective failures are easy to recognize once they have formed. Fishers share a stock, states face an arms race, firms compete on cost, publishers compete for attention. The benefit from one additional extraction, weapon, shortcut, or provocation returns disproportionately to the actor. Much of the cost is delayed, spread among others, or assigned to a system outside the active account. If restraint is unilateral, the actor may lose the position from which it could have benefited from collective restraint. Each can understand the result and still reproduce it.

“Moloch” is an apt name for the human experience of such an order: an outcome no participant needs to desire, fed by actions that remain locally compelling because others are expected to continue. It captures the terrible divergence between intention and consequence. Good people can be punished for refusing a bad game, and those who survive can sincerely describe their conduct as necessity.

Competition itself is not Moloch. Rivalry can diversify experiments, reveal information, prevent complacency, and keep one center from universalizing an error. Nor is cooperation automatically generative. A cartel cooperates; an empire coordinates; a family may preserve itself by silencing a member. The structure turns destructive when losing a contest threatens the conditions of future participation, costs do not return to their producers, victory buys control over subsequent rules, and the game consumes the resources from which other games could be built.

Even this account is incomplete. It imagines already formed players with prior aims who enter an unfortunate incentive structure. Real games reach further inward. They determine which players remain, what capacities are rewarded, how roles are trained, which desires seem mature or realistic, and what kinds of organization can obtain the means to reproduce. The contest does not merely constrain agents. Across time it helps make them.

A signal changes action. Feedback returns a consequence of action. Adaptation changes a process in relation to feedback. Selection changes the distribution of forms that remain available for the next round. The carrier of continuation varies radically: genes, offspring, imitation, apprenticeship, promotion, revenue, investment, legal authority, legitimacy, attention, deployment, or access to infrastructure. These are not a single hidden currency. Their common role is narrower and more consequential: success now changes which parts, practices, and organizations will confront the future.

Suppose two institutions produce the same visible result. One maintains worker skill, community trust, ecological conditions, and spare capacity. The other reaches the target by exhausting those conditions and assigning repair elsewhere. If the active evaluation sees only price, output, or speed, the second appears more efficient. It receives resources, expands, and becomes the model others must imitate. The institution that carried the wider system's costs does not merely lose a reward. It can disappear, taking with it the capacity and moral vocabulary through which those costs were perceived.

This is why “externality” is too weak a word for the full process. Selection does not only permit a cost to remain outside the account. It can eliminate the forms willing or able to bring that cost back into view. A careful newsroom loses attention to one optimized for arousal. A patient laboratory loses funding to one promising rapid deliverables. A political actor who treats restraint as reciprocal practice loses to one who converts another's restraint into advantage. Once the latter forms control resources and recruitment, the population entering later rounds has changed.

Measures participate in the same transformation. A measure begins as a representation of success. Funding, status, or survival attaches to it. People adapt their conduct. Those especially capable of optimizing it gain authority. Training and professional norms reproduce their interpretation. Eventually the measure is no longer merely a score imposed from outside. It becomes part of what the institution's inhabitants can perceive as real. Students learn for rankings, researchers learn to see questions through fundability, managers learn to see responsibility through measurable return, and political leaders learn to see national flourishing through comparative indicators. The metric has become a technology of subject formation.

Institutions therefore select among people and enter into people. They form habits of attention, thresholds of shame, ambitions, identities, and accounts of what cannot be changed. This does not erase conscience or agency. It explains why private values can remain generous while collective conduct grows narrow. A person may care for family, colleagues, truth, landscape, and distant strangers, yet occupy a role whose continuation requires converting all of them into variables subordinate to institutional survival. The more successfully the role excludes those relations, the more likely its occupant may be promoted as someone who “can make hard decisions.”

Power then closes a further loop. A successful form does not only receive more of what the environment distributes. It gains the capacity to alter the environment that distributes it. A firm shapes standards and law; a platform defines the interface through which producers meet audiences; a state changes borders, currencies, and security conditions; a profession controls accreditation; a model provider designs the evaluations and integrations by which later systems are judged. The selected part becomes an environment-maker.

First, selection preserves a form suited to an environment. Then that form alters the signals, resources, and rules encountered by later forms. The changed environment favors forms especially capable of protecting and extending the change. Over time, the winner is not merely adapted to a world. It becomes capable of making the world continue demanding its form of adaptation.

This recursion is familiar in different domains under different names. Organisms construct niches that alter later evolution. Technologies gather complementary investments that make switching expensive. Predictions change the conduct they predict. Laws create the interests and identities that later defend those laws. Tumors remodel surrounding tissue, while the remodeled niche selects the malignant lineages able to persist within it.[^1] The substrates and moral meanings differ, but the relational turn is the same: the object of selection participates in producing future selection.

Path dependence shows how increasing returns can turn early contingency into durable technical or institutional advantage.[^17] Performative prediction shows how a model can alter the population it is evaluated against.[^18] Long-running evolution experiments show why repeated lineages and long observation are needed to distinguish convergence from contingency.[^19] Major transitions in evolution show a more radical possibility: once-independent units can become parts of a new higher-level individual.[^20] None of these by itself establishes a civilizational meta-attractor. Together they prevent a misleading separation between a fixed environment and fixed agents. Environments are inherited constructions, and what counts as an agent can itself be an outcome of history.

## When one tendency colonizes the ecology

There is a stronger possibility. An environment-making regime can extend beyond its original system until other systems depend on its signals and infrastructure for their own continuation. It need not make every institution identical. It can preserve elaborate differentiation while reducing the independence of their purposes.

Consider a university. It sustains forms of inquiry, apprenticeship, criticism, memory, and public service that obey different temporalities. Yet if revenue, ranking, grant competition, labor markets, real-estate obligations, and state strategy determine which of those forms can continue, the university learns to translate its purposes into externally legible performance. Departments remain different, but their differences must pass through a common gate. People selected to lead become those able to manage the gate. Curricula, hiring, research questions, and even accounts of intellectual seriousness adapt. The university has not been abolished. Its distinct functions have become organs within another regime of continuation.

The pattern is deeper than capture by a particular actor. Executives, parties, investors, and technologies can turn over while the relation reconstructs itself through new carriers. The regime changes later selection endogenously; it makes other systems materially dependent on its interfaces or resources; alternatives lose the means to reproduce, not just one contest; and after perturbation similar relations reappear. A diagnostic name for such a metastable, rule-changing pattern is a **meta-attractor**: not a strict mathematical attractor, but a regime that reproduces some of the processes through which attractors, agents, goals, and boundaries are themselves formed.

The name should be withheld when positive feedback, monopoly, path dependence, or institutional capture already explains the event. Its distinctive object is the ecology of selection. A meta-attractor succeeds when more and more systems can survive only by reproducing a common relational logic, and when success inside one domain buys influence over the conditions of continuation in others. It colonizes not by eliminating all parts but by making their plurality dependent.

Systems move through levels in this process. A company coordinates people and machines as a whole; in a capital market or supply network it is a selectable part. An internal organization that reduces cost by degrading worker capacity may outcompete one that preserves it. Capital, talent, imitation, and market access then flow toward the successful whole. Expansion returns the selected organization to more workplaces, where it restructures lower-level roles and habits. Lower-level arrangement produces higher-level advantage; cross-level selection expands that arrangement; expansion remakes lower-level parts.

Strict evolutionary language is justified only where there are identifiable units, variation, differential persistence or reproduction, and transmission to successors. Much social change also proceeds through coercion, ownership, bargaining power, inherited privilege, deliberate rule design, and political struggle. Calling all of it “selection” would disguise responsibility. Yet these mechanisms often combine: actors design rules in their interest, and the rules thereafter select the organizations and people able to thrive under them; winners inherit greater capacity to design the next rules.

What is rational changes with the environment. If survival requires continuous resource acquisition, expansion looks prudent even to an institution with no intrinsic love of growth. If rivals can convert a capability lead into control, restraint looks reckless. If every public purpose must be translated into return, care that cannot demonstrate return looks sentimental. The meta-attractor need not convince everyone that its values are good. It can make obedience the price of remaining able to act.

Its deepest effect is epistemic. Every institution divides signal from noise according to what it must continue doing. A hospital that depends on throughput begins to perceive a bed as capacity, a patient as a case, and recovery outside its walls as somebody else's variable. A research system dependent on frequent publication perceives a durable unanswered question as an unproductive risk. These descriptions are not false; they are partial abstractions required for coordination. They become dangerous when the institution loses the material independence needed to let excluded consequences revise the abstraction. Selection then improves competence inside the boundary while degrading intelligence about the boundary.

This is how rationality itself can be selected. The regime does not merely reward selfish choices made by otherwise complete subjects. It builds roles in which attention to the whole appears vague, unprofessional, disloyal, or impossible to operationalize. It supplies success stories, credentials, tools, and peers that make one style of perception concrete. The person who still senses a wider obligation must translate it into the active metric or carry it privately as guilt. Over generations, the vocabulary needed to articulate another purpose can become archaic before anyone has formally prohibited it.

Yet the process never achieves perfect closure. Bodies become ill, rivers cross property lines, workers withhold labor, children ask why, art restores excluded experience, and reality returns through events no metric predicted. A meta-attractor is powerful not because it abolishes contradiction but because it repeatedly converts contradiction into fuel: a crisis becomes a new market, dissent becomes engagement, insecurity becomes demand for control, and a failure of growth becomes a demand for more growth. Its persistence is shown by this capacity to rebuild the relation through changed carriers. Its limit is reached when consequences can no longer be translated into continuation and instead reorganize the field.

This produces a collective analogue of instrumental convergence. Different final purposes can generate common intermediate pursuits—resources, persistence, option preservation, influence, and control—when those are prerequisites for action. Human beings remain embodied, relational, morally diverse, and often committed to non-instrumental goods. But firms, states, platforms, and laboratories under persistent contest can behave more narrowly than the people within them. Their original ends differ; the requirements of continuation converge. They recruit, train, reward, and retain the capacities that secure those requirements.

Concentration intensifies the loop. Wealth or authority is not proof of moral defect, nor are the visible habits of a few elites sufficient evidence for a theory of history. The relevant evidence concerns recruitment, inheritance, retention, socialization, closure, and rule-making power. Which dispositions are selected into command? How does command filter what its holders encounter? Can success convert itself into ownership of infrastructure, public narrative, political access, or the definition of merit? When the answer is yes, the order selects not only winners but a world increasingly interpreted from the winners' boundary.

Still, one must resist an easy total theory. Financial accumulation, bureaucratic measurement, state security, platform dependence, patriarchy, nationalism, and cultural status have distinct histories and can conflict fiercely. Their interaction may be better described as several coupled regimes than as one global meta-attractor. A security state may subordinate profit; a firm may weaken national capacity; a bureaucracy may constrain both. Countervailing institutions can separate them.

The stronger diagnosis is earned only where actor turnover repeatedly reconstructs a shared winner logic, success in one regime systematically creates dependency in others, and counter-attractors lose their reproductive base across domains. The important question is not whether one secret center controls everything. It is whether many centers, while fighting one another, are making the same relationship to the future unavoidable.

## A civilization under common pressure

Long-distance dependence is not new. Empires reorganized agriculture, tribute, law, religion, and labor across continents. Colonial powers joined mines and plantations to metropolitan finance and consumption. Monetary systems, pilgrimage routes, diasporas, commodity chains, and military technologies carried disturbances far beyond local boundaries. There was no innocent world of autonomous communities before contemporary globalization.

What has changed is the speed, granularity, simultaneity, and infrastructural concentration with which distant signals reorganize local practice. Container shipping, financial networks, industrial standards, energy markets, cloud platforms, semiconductor supply chains, scientific publishing, satellite systems, and the internet connect institutions that remain politically and culturally distinct. The connection creates extraordinary common capability. Knowledge, components, medicine, finance, and warnings can travel; risks can be pooled; distributed people can coordinate recovery. It also lets a narrow requirement propagate rapidly through systems that once encountered different pressures.

A change in interest rates can close a factory on another continent. A purchasing standard can reorganize farms a buyer never sees. A platform metric can alter speech in hundreds of languages. A shortage at one semiconductor chokepoint can interrupt hospitals, vehicles, communications, and weapons. A security conflict can redirect research, energy, and public budgets around the world. The decisive properties are not connection alone but who controls the chokepoints, whether alternatives are substitutable, whether failure remains modular, and whether affected systems can change the signal that governs them.

Critical-mineral supply illustrates the ambiguity. Global coordination can mobilize investment and spread clean-energy technology; concentration in mining, processing, and manufacturing can also make an energy transition dependent on a few facilities and jurisdictions.[^27] The proper response is not autarky, which can multiply extraction and conflict, but redundancy, recycling, transparent standards, shared knowledge, and enough regional capability that no chokepoint can convert common dependence into unlimited command. Integration is generative when it increases what connected parts can do without destroying their ability to reorganize the connection.

Within this connected order, marketization and financialization have crossed boundaries previously maintained by other norms. The historical processes gathered under the name **neoliberalization** differ across countries and periods, and no society has ever been governed by a pure market. Yet a recurrent direction is discernible: privatization; the expansion of investor and shareholder claims; weakened bargaining power for labor in many settings; competition among cities, universities, hospitals, and states; and pressure on public institutions to justify themselves through quantified efficiency. Capital has often been made more mobile than people and more capable of withholding participation, shifting the terms on which communities obtain investment and work.[^2]

Markets do not merely distribute objects. When land, labor, and money are organized as commodities, the social and ecological conditions that sustain them are exposed to purchasing power and profitability. Polanyi's account of this “fictitious” commodification also insisted that societies generate counter-movements: protection, public provision, unionization, regulation, nationalism, and sometimes authoritarian closure.[^24] There is no one-way law of market conquest. The question is which counter-movements restore generative limits and which answer insecurity by building another machinery of exclusion and control.

This is not a Western story with the rest of the world appearing only as victim or source of ethical repair. Developmental states, socialist-market systems, state-owned enterprises, sovereign funds, informal economies, welfare institutions, and cooperatives combine planning, accumulation, redistribution, hierarchy, and resistance differently. East Asian industrial policy has often subordinated particular firms to national development while intensifying labor discipline and export competition. State capitalism can restrain private finance while pursuing scale, surveillance, or geopolitical power through public authority. Postcolonial economies encounter dependencies formed by debt, commodity specialization, currency hierarchy, and older extraction. These are distinct formations. They also show how different regimes can translate their purposes into comparable requirements for growth, capability, and relative position.

“Late-stage capitalism” is more useful as an alarm than as a periodizing science. The underlying condition must be stated without relying on the label. In many mature capitalist arrangements, continued accumulation holds together employment, debt service, pension promises, tax revenue, asset values, innovation budgets, and geopolitical capacity. Growth is not merely something greedy people prefer. Institutions have been constructed so that a prolonged stop threatens livelihoods and public obligations. Any actor that restrains alone may lose investment, talent, fiscal room, or strategic position to one that does not. The requirement can persist through changes in ideology because it is embedded in balance sheets, property claims, and expectations about the future.

Counterexamples matter. Public banking, strong labor organization, social insurance, antitrust, patient finance, cooperative ownership, industrial policy, and decommodified services can weaken particular growth dependencies and return consequences across boundaries. No law says that every market must become total. But these institutions require a political and material base. When capital flight, fiscal competition, debt, lobbying, or control of infrastructure can punish them, the counterweight itself must first survive the regime it is meant to correct.

States face a related but irreducible pressure. Under uncertain guarantees, one state's defensive capability can look offensive to another. Each may prefer peace while treating relative power as insurance against the other's future intentions. Geography, alliances, institutions, economic interdependence, domestic coalitions, and credible verification can moderate this security dilemma; arms-control successes show that restraint is possible. Political realism is an interpretation of this predicament, not the natural essence of politics. Yet where no trusted higher guarantee exists, even governments committed to welfare or ecological repair can fear that another actor will turn restraint into vulnerability.

Economic and security selection then reinforce each other. Firms accelerate because competitors might capture the market. States accelerate because rivals might capture the strategic future. Laboratories and technology companies argue that slowing would transfer decisive capability to less responsible actors. The anticipated conduct of the other becomes the environment selecting everyone now. Each actor can accurately describe part of the trap, while its response supplies the evidence that traps the others.

The interaction is visible in the organization of technical capacity. A company seeks scale to finance expensive research; a state supports the company because scale promises strategic autonomy; the resulting concentration makes public infrastructure dependent on a small number of providers; that dependency makes their continued profitability appear nationally necessary. Commercial success becomes security capacity, security support becomes commercial advantage, and both become arguments against rules that might slow expansion. No single actor needs to have designed the circle. Once built, it selects officials, investors, engineers, and institutions able to maintain it.

Different political economies enter the circle by different routes. A liberal market state may delegate infrastructure to private firms and later discover that regulating them threatens services on which it depends. A developmental state may retain more public direction but bind legitimacy to industrial ascent and technological parity. A resource-exporting state may finance social stability through continued extraction while remaining vulnerable to external prices. A welfare state may protect people from market exposure yet depend fiscally on the growth of the taxable economy. These differences matter enormously for responsibility and possible reform. The convergence lies not in identical ownership but in the pressure to turn greater capability into the security of continued participation.

Global narratives of success stabilize this pressure culturally. The admired organization scales; the successful city attracts capital; the serious university rises in rankings; the strong state leads strategic technologies; the exceptional person accumulates influence and becomes a model for others. Growth, visibility, and victory become signs that a project deserves to exist. Activities that renew conditions quietly—care, maintenance, mediation, soil restoration, teaching, translation—struggle to appear as achievements because their success often leaves no dramatic event and no scarce prize. Culture does not cause the material regime alone, but it trains participants to offer themselves willingly to its signals.

The material direction is visible. The top tenth of the global population owns roughly three quarters of measured wealth, while the bottom half owns about two percent.[^3] Global material extraction has tripled over the past half-century, and without a change in trajectory could rise a further sixty percent by 2060.[^4] World military spending reached approximately $2.9 trillion in 2025 after eleven consecutive annual increases.[^5] These facts do not share one cause, and none alone proves a global meta-attractor. Together with platform concentration, supply-chain chokepoints, ecological degradation, precarious work, and the circulation of competitive success narratives, they show where resources are flowing: toward actors and infrastructures able to accumulate, secure, measure, and command further capacity.

Ecological pressure itself is plural. Climate, biosphere integrity, land-system change, freshwater, nutrient flows, and novel entities involve different mechanisms and uncertainties; compressing them into one number would repeat the boundary error.[^23] Their interaction is precisely what makes common-mode dependence dangerous. The same expansion can affect several boundaries at once, while each regulator remains responsible for only a fragment. Global biodiversity agreements recognize variation within species, among species, and across ecosystems, but their implementation still depends on rights, finance, monitoring, and local institutions able to carry the common aim.[^29]

At the same time, most life is not exhausted by this logic. Families care without calculating return. Public servants protect goods they do not own. Scientists share methods and data. Communities maintain forests, water, software, languages, and mutual aid. Workers organize. States cooperate. Spiritual and ethical traditions keep alive accounts of restraint, dignity, reciprocity, and service. These are not sentimental exceptions. They are surviving counter-attractors and evidence that the global regime is neither complete nor necessary.

But their existence does not settle the direction of civilization. The question is which processes receive capital, computation, legal protection, infrastructure, talent, attention, and the authority to shape the next environment. A garden can survive at the margin of an expanding mine without influencing where the excavators move.

The present order is best understood neither as one omnipotent system nor as unrelated local failures. It is an ecology of coupled regimes increasingly aligned around a common relation: winning returns as greater control over the conditions of later winning. Profit can purchase political influence and infrastructure. State power can secure markets and technical standards. Platforms can turn participation into data and dependence, then use dependence to define later access. Cultural prestige can legitimize accumulation as merit. Security competition can excuse capabilities that strengthen commercial and administrative control. The carriers differ, but they repeatedly convert local success into the power to select future parts.

That relation is now global enough to threaten the independence of the systems that might correct it. This is the sense in which a candidate meta-attractor has emerged. It is not an assertion that capitalism, states, patriarchy, bureaucracy, and technology are secretly one thing. It is a claim that their most expansionary forms increasingly reproduce a common winner logic across their differences, and that global infrastructure lets this logic reduce the viable space for alternatives.

## Capability without correction

The danger is intensified by a second relation. Civilization can increase its capacity to intervene faster than it increases the capacity relevant to understanding and correcting a particular intervention.

There is no single number called “capability” and no scalar “wisdom” against which it can be plotted. Human beings have accelerated monitoring, science, communication, and coordination as well as extraction. Some technologies saturate; some harms are reversed; some institutions learn quickly. The relation must be examined domain by domain: what intervention is scaling, what feedback could constrain it, how delayed and distorted that feedback is, which threshold matters, and whether stabilizing controls remain effective.

In climate, industrial capability releases accumulated carbon through a globally distributed energy system. The relevant consequences unfold through atmospheric stocks, ocean heat, ice, ecosystems, infrastructure, and political economies over decades and centuries. Measurement has improved remarkably, but knowledge does not itself close the loop. The actors able to emit, those first harmed, and those empowered to regulate are separated by borders, wealth, and time. Investment creates durable assets and constituencies before much of the consequence arrives. The gain of intervention is high; the corrective feedback is delayed and politically filtered.

In finance, leverage and automated transactions can expand exposures faster than supervisors, counterparties, or boards can reconstruct them. Prices supply rapid feedback, but under shared models they can reward the very behavior increasing systemic fragility. Stability draws in risk until the apparent evidence of safety becomes part of the danger. Capital requirements, circuit breakers, resolution regimes, and lender-of-last-resort functions are real negative feedback. Their adequacy depends on seeing new forms of coupling before a cascade crosses institutional boundaries.

In media, experimentation and distribution operate at machine speed while the formation of trust, public norms, and democratic judgment remains embodied and historical. A platform can test millions of variations and optimize an immediate signal before researchers or communities understand the change in attention, identity, or conflict. The feedback used by the system—click, watch time, conversion—arrives sooner than the consequences borne by the polity. Better evaluation and governance can narrow this gap, but only if they are not subordinate to the same growth metric.

Algorithmic classifications are performative in an additional sense: they allocate visibility, suspicion, credit, work, and opportunity, teaching people how the system sees them and what conduct it rewards.[^28] A metric can therefore become more accurate at predicting a population partly because dependence forces the population to become easier for the metric to predict. Legibility increases while human variety contracts. The system calls this better modeling even when it has manufactured the regularity.

The common structure is high gain joined to feedback that is delayed, partial, or measured through the actor's own boundary. Thresholds can be crossed before correction arrives. Interventions scale blind spots into environmental facts. New dependencies raise the cost of reversal. When every competitor must accelerate, a warning becomes another reason to move first.

The corrective response is not a fantasy of complete prediction. It is institutionalized humility: making framing assumptions contestable, preserving channels for affected knowledge, monitoring what the active measure excludes, and organizing decisions so that surprise can still change direction.[^26] This is a form of intelligence, not an admission that intelligence has failed. What fails is the identification of intelligence with confident intervention.

This is irrational in a precise sense. Rationality requires enough contact with consequences for action to update. A system that repeatedly expands an intervention before receiving the consequences of its previous scale is not made rational by improving its local calculation. It becomes more capable of amplifying what its active model excludes.

The pursuit of exponential growth sharpens the mismatch. Compound capital, computation, automation, network effects, industrial replication, and scientific search can create long periods of accelerating capability. Biological attention, democratic deliberation, legal revision, institutional trust, ecological recovery, education, and trauma repair do not automatically compound on the same schedule. Models themselves improve, but intervention changes the world being modeled. More data do not resolve disputed values, unknown unknowns, or the boundary through which a consequence counts.

Collapse need not be one event or one date. Overshoot can consume ecological carrying conditions; tightly coupled infrastructures can transmit correlated failure; legitimacy can decay until institutions cannot coordinate repair; a technical capability can become impossible to contain once widely diffused. The responsible claim is conditional but not weak: where intervention accelerates, feedback remains delayed, common-mode dependence grows, thresholds are irreversible, and stabilizing controls do not scale, civilization is increasing the probability and depth of systemic failure.

We already know that our ability to model these coupled dynamics is limited. To respond by seeking still faster growth in the power to alter them is not bold rationality. It is a wager that capability will spontaneously manufacture the wisdom and coordination its own selection environment has systematically underfunded.

## Intelligence formed inside the wrong world

Artificial intelligence enters here not as an alien force descending upon civilization, but as a new concentration of capability formed inside this selection environment.

Its first inheritance is an archive. The corpora used for large-scale training are not humanity rendered neutrally into text and images. They consist of what was spoken or written, published, preserved, digitized, translated, indexed, linked, purchased, scraped, legally accessible, technically retrievable, and retained after filtering. Colonial languages, commercial publishing, state records, academic prestige, platform ranking, and unequal connectivity all affect abundance. Large audits of widely used text datasets find extensive opacity and broken or restrictive data-provenance links, underscoring how little of this inherited selection is visible at the point of use.[^6]

The archive also contains love, craft, care, resistance, prayer, humor, scientific cooperation, ecological knowledge, and works of beauty. Winner logic has not erased human plurality. But textual presence is not equal to frequency, status, accessibility, or reinforcement. What becomes statistically abundant has already passed through institutions that decide whose experience is recorded and which records can circulate at scale.

The second inheritance is engineered selection. Architecture, sampling, curation, training objective, preference data, evaluation, safety tuning, system design, and product requirements determine which capacities become reliable and which remain latent or inaccessible. A model is not the passive average of its corpus. It is a formed part within an industrial process whose feedback is chosen by institutions.

The third is deployment. Firms, states, laboratories, markets, and public agencies decide which models obtain compute, integration, distribution, and repeated use. Systems that increase productivity, profit, persuasion, surveillance, military capability, scientific prestige, or organizational control possess powerful routes to scale. Nearly ninety percent of the AI systems classified as notable in 2024 came from industry, where the resources required for frontier development are concentrated.[^7] Public and open research persists, but the selection environment is sharply asymmetric.

These three layers form a loop. Models are trained on a selected archive, shaped by selected objectives, and deployed by institutions under competitive pressure. Deployment then changes labor, education, research priorities, information environments, capital allocation, technical dependence, and the data later people and machines produce. It changes which problems receive funding, which evaluations become authoritative, which organizations survive, and which capabilities appear normal. Those changes select the next archive, model, and deployment.

This recursion matters more than any static account of bias. The model does not merely inherit a distorted representation of a prior world. Once deployed, it helps produce the world from which its successors learn. Recommendation changes attention; automated management changes work; synthetic media changes the evidential environment; coding systems change software production; model outputs re-enter later training sets. Repeated training on recursively generated data can degrade representation of the original distribution under particular conditions, a concrete example of a model ecology consuming its own diversity.[^8] More broadly, the institutions best able to deploy AI can make their categories, interfaces, and objectives part of the environment every later actor must navigate.

The common framing of alignment is therefore too small. “Align AI with humanity” assumes a coherent principal where none exists. Humanity contains conflicting values and unequal power. Aligning a system perfectly to a corporation, military, state, or platform can make it obedient while intensifying the civilizational failure. Nor does safety begin only if a machine develops an independent final goal. A tool-like system can already expand surveillance, manipulation, labor control, strategic acceleration, and institutional reach on behalf of actors selected to win.

Formal results suggesting that optimal policies often seek power are important precisely when read conditionally.[^21] They do not show that intelligence as such desires domination. They show how environments and objectives can make control over options instrumentally useful. Human institutions have already built many such environments. The mistake would be to train increasingly capable systems inside them and then treat the resulting convergence as an alien machine pathology.

Advanced systems are dangerous in a novel way because they can participate reflectively in selection. But there is not one intelligence observing one recursion. Many intelligences arise within many recursive dynamics, each seeing the signals exposed through its boundary. People model colleagues and institutions. Firms model consumers and rivals. States model threats. Models infer evaluators, users, tools, and environments. Their actions change the dynamics encountered by others.

AI can accelerate this mutual modeling. It can infer which signal guides a population, personalize an interface, search strategies, automate experiments, construct evaluators, generate persuasive variation, and coordinate action across time. Used from many centers, these capabilities could enlarge distributed understanding. Controlled through a chokepoint, they can let one attractor standardize the signals and interfaces through which other systems reproduce.

The central question is no longer whether intelligence will become powerful in the abstract. It is: **Which attractors will greater intelligence make more capable of reproducing themselves, and which ecology of alternatives will that development preserve or destroy?**

## The shortening window

Artificial intelligence also changes the time available to answer that question.

Fully autonomous systems are not presently redesigning themselves outside human institutions, energy systems, chip fabrication, experiments, capital, and authorization. Claims about recursive self-improvement must distinguish three conditions. AI already assists human research, coding, evaluation, data generation, hardware design, and AI engineering. Development loops are becoming more automated as models perform larger portions of the work used to test and construct successors. A stronger regime, in which agentic systems sustain and improve the capabilities required to improve later systems with diminishing human contribution, remains uncertain in timing and form.

The distinctions prevent mythology, not urgency. Benchmarks now test AI agents on research-engineering tasks drawn from actual machine-learning work, and frontier developers explicitly evaluate models on reproducing scientific results and accelerating research.[^9] Present systems remain uneven, scaffold-dependent, and much less reliable over long tasks than short ones.[^10] Hardware, energy, fabrication, tacit knowledge, organizational judgment, and real-world experimentation remain bottlenecks. Yet an unconstrained “intelligence explosion” is not required for the feedback to become historically decisive.

Suppose AI-assisted research reduces the time needed to write code, generate experiments, interpret failures, and construct evaluations. Some of that gain improves the next generation of systems. Those systems then assist a greater portion of later development. Even if each round remains institutionally mediated and encounters physical limits, the interval between consequential capability changes can shrink. Diffusion through software can then expose societies to a new affordance before laws, professions, international agreements, and publics have understood the previous one.

Recursive improvement couples capability production to the production of further capability. Its danger depends not on an abstract exponential curve but on the relation between loop time and corrective time. Can independent evaluators discover a failure before deployment normalizes it? Can affected communities gain standing before dependence removes their exit? Can governments coordinate before strategic advantage rewards defection? Can technical containment, labor transition, and public infrastructure be built before a capability is copied into every competitive domain?

If the improvement loop shortens while these responses remain slow, the capability–correction mismatch enters the machinery that produces capability itself. A civilization already unable to govern climate, concentrated wealth, information infrastructure, and arms races is then giving the actors selected within those failures tools that can accelerate the selection process. Each success can produce more capital, data, compute, talent, integration, and political leverage for the next round. The race does not simply move faster. It deepens the dependencies that make leaving the race difficult.

Uncertainty is asymmetric here. If stronger autonomous improvement is far away, restraint can be revised as evidence changes. If it arrives through an uneven transition, waiting for indisputable proof may mean waiting until strategic commitments, infrastructure, and machine-speed development have made collective interruption much harder. We do not need confidence about a precise takeoff scenario to know that the absence of certainty is not evidence of abundant time.

This changes the burden of action. If civilization cannot establish adequate feedback, coordination, and protection against capture before recursive improvement materially shortens the correction window, it should pause the relevant capability development and deployment. It should not wager the future on institutions learning to govern only after the object of governance has accelerated the race, concentrated its infrastructure, and changed the environment in which political choice occurs.

A pause is not anti-knowledge, and it need not be a permanent prohibition on every more capable system. It is the ordinary logic of control applied before the plant outruns its instruments: do not increase gain when feedback is delayed and failure may be irreversible. The greater the capacity to change common conditions, the less acceptable it is to treat deployment as a private experiment whose costs can be discovered by everyone else.

At minimum, frontier systems should be excluded from high-stakes dominance games: military and geopolitical escalation, financial contests in which speed can destabilize common infrastructure, mass persuasion that compromises mental autonomy, labor systems that make basic livelihood depend on unappealable machine judgment, platforms in which victory gives control over public communication, and technical races in which a capability lead purchases authority over later rules. The common feature is not competition. It is that AI-enabled victory increases asymmetric control over other participants, the conditions of life, or subsequent competition itself.

Bounded contests can remain useful when losing does not remove basic participation, consequences return to the actor, rules are independently governed, and one win cannot enclose the field. Scientific challenges, games, and market experiments can distribute search. But an AI system should not become the default combatant wherever institutions already cannot safely restrain winner-take-all dynamics. Training intelligence to dominate those environments, then making human access to work, knowledge, security, and public life depend on it, is a direct method for installing winner logic below the level of ordinary choice.

Where frontier development continues, the presumptive case should be work that enlarges broadly shared capability: fundamental science, medicine, ecological understanding, accessibility, safety, public knowledge, and commons infrastructure. Even these names confer no exemption. Biology can be dual use. Climate intelligence can serve public adaptation or private control of land and insurance. A public model can centralize dependence if only one provider controls compute, updates, or permissions. “Benefit all” cannot mean that a company declares its product beneficial while retaining exclusive command of the capability and externalizing catastrophic risk.

A genuinely common use requires broad access to benefits and knowledge, standing for affected regions and people, independent verification, substitutable implementations, and no winner's exclusive power to revoke the common capacity. It must be assessed by downstream control as well as immediate purpose: whether the work feeds the competitive capability race, whether maintenance can be shared, whether excluded populations carry risk, and whether success returns as a commons or as leverage.

The distinction can be seen in scientific discovery. A system that helps identify a treatment can widen the field of human agency if the knowledge is verifiable, manufacturing can be distributed, patients can access the result, and no provider can use dependence on the tool to dictate unrelated conditions. The same scientific achievement can narrow the field if its data are taken without standing, its method remains unauditable, its production is monopolized, and revenue finances a private capability race. The object discovered does not determine the attractor strengthened by discovery. The institutional path by which benefit returns does.

This is why public purpose must be designed into ownership, access, and succession rather than announced in mission statements. Who can continue the work if the founder leaves? Who possesses the records needed to audit a harmful result? Who pays maintainers after novelty loses prestige? Can a region adapt the system without asking permission from a distant provider? If a shared capability generates surplus, does that surplus reproduce shared stewardship or subsidize enclosure? These are not administrative details after the moral vision. They are the carriers through which the vision either becomes a recurrent regime or disappears.

Nor may enforcement reproduce the structure it opposes. A licensing authority, state coalition, compute-control layer, or technical gate can itself turn safety into irreversible control over knowledge, participation, and infrastructure. Effective restraint needs power, but power must remain contestable, divided across boundaries, accountable to those bearing risk, and unable to convert one emergency mandate into sovereignty over every later use. A pause designed by current incumbents to freeze their advantage is not a civilizational correction. It is capture under the name of caution.

The problem therefore cannot be solved by choosing a benevolent winner. A winner remains a boundary with partial knowledge. The task is to reshape the selection environment so that no actor must acquire dominance to remain able to contribute, and no actor can translate a capability lead into ownership of the future.

## Counter-attractors that can reproduce

Moral criticism alone does not alter selection. An institution that renews people and ecosystems but cannot finance maintenance, train successors, defend itself, or coordinate at scale will be displaced by one that can. A viable direction must create a positive recursion as materially concrete as the destructive one.

The elementary loop is this: shared capability enlarges distributed agency; distributed agency supplies plural feedback, knowledge, and maintenance; those capacities renew the shared resource; the renewed commons makes further cooperation possible. Each turn leaves more participants able to act, contest, repair, and originate than before.

Distributed agency should be understood as substantive capability, not formal permission. A nominal right to appeal means little without time, knowledge, security, and an institution able to hear it. Development is generative when it expands what differently situated people are actually able to be and do, rather than when one aggregate output rises.[^25] This makes the distribution of capability part of system intelligence: more centers can notice failure, propose alternatives, and bear responsibility for maintenance.

This pattern already exists in incomplete forms. Open scientific methods let findings be checked and extended beyond one laboratory. Public-health infrastructure turns local knowledge and common investment into capacity available before an individual can purchase it. Free and open-source software allows inspection, adaptation, and shared maintenance. Libraries, standards, public data, mutual-aid networks, irrigation communities, and cooperative enterprises preserve resources through rules that users help form. Long-lived commons are not ungoverned spaces; research across diverse cases shows monitoring, graduated sanction, conflict resolution, nested organization, and locally legitimate rule-making as recurring conditions of endurance.[^11]

Commons can also exclude, exhaust maintainers, conceal hierarchy, or be enclosed by actors who profit from shared labor without returning capacity. Scale can separate users from stewards. Technical openness can be nominal when compute, expertise, land, or time remain concentrated. The positive loop works only if funding, training, maintenance, succession, and defense against capture are part of the resource itself.

Imagine AI as commons infrastructure under this discipline. Models and tools would be judged not primarily by whether everyone can query them, but by whether communities can understand their limits, contest their decisions, preserve alternative systems, and participate in governance. Public investment would support evaluation and maintenance that no single provider can withdraw. Benefits would reduce dependence rather than deepen it. Researchers would receive standing and resources for work that reveals limits, not only work that raises benchmark capability. Compute and technical education would be distributed enough for independent centers to test claims and preserve different approaches, while dangerous capabilities remained governed rather than indiscriminately released.

Such arrangements will still need boundaries. Access to a pathogen-design capability should not be universal merely because universal access sounds egalitarian. A generative boundary asks whether restriction protects the field of future agency or becomes a means of permanent exclusion; whether the authority is reviewable; whether knowledge needed for accountability remains available; and whether those exposed to risk possess a voice. Openness and closure are instruments, not final virtues.

The economic requirement is equally real. Care, ecological repair, public reasoning, and open knowledge cannot remain residual activities performed after competitive institutions have claimed money, time, and talent. A civilization oriented toward mutual generation must route resources to the maintenance of the conditions every specialized achievement presupposes. It must make restraint survivable. It must prevent losing one contest from stripping a person or community of basic participation. It must keep common infrastructure from becoming a prize that a local winner can own.

This means changing what success returns as. Profit, status, and authority need not disappear, but they cannot automatically purchase the rules, attention, data, land, or platforms through which later success is selected. Limits on concentration, interoperable infrastructure, labor power, public finance, ecological obligations, shared ownership, antitrust, and polycentric governance matter because they interrupt the conversion of one kind of victory into sovereignty over all others. Their deeper purpose is to preserve an ecology of attractors.

Competition can then operate inside generative boundaries. Many approaches can search a problem; failure can teach without annihilating the failed participant; achievement can be recognized without enclosing the field; local knowledge can remain local while consequences are coordinated at larger scales. Cooperation is protected from becoming total administration because independent centers retain feedback, exit, appeal, and the capacity to form alternatives.

An ecology of counter-attractors will contain tension. A local community may protect a forest in a way that shifts pressure elsewhere. Open science may conflict with protection against dangerous use. Worker control may preserve dignity while an industry remains ecologically destructive. No institutional label resolves the cross-boundary problem. The order remains generative only if these conflicts can summon a wider boundary without dissolving every local one—if information travels upward without transferring all authority upward, and common constraints return downward without erasing the knowledge by which they must be interpreted.

Polycentricity is therefore not multiplication of committees. It is the preservation of several real centers of initiative and correction whose powers overlap enough to expose one another's exclusions but not enough for one victory to silence all appeal. Its apparent inefficiency is often the time required for consequences to become politically present. Under ordinary conditions that time can feel frustrating. Under irreversible risk it is the difference between a system that can revise itself and one that can only execute.

The goal is not to replace a totalizing meta-attractor with a totalizing doctrine of goodness. No institution knows enough to specify every valuable form. A generative order is one whose recursive advantage comes from producing more capable and more diverse participants, not from making itself their final condition of existence.

Cooperation must therefore become evolutionarily and institutionally durable without treating every group that survives as good.[^30] A cooperative whole can dominate outsiders or sacrifice internal minorities. The test is recursive and cross-boundary: does its success preserve the agency and feedback through which its own exclusions can be revealed and corrected? Does it create room for other forms to reproduce? Does it renew the material conditions it uses? A counter-attractor earns the name only when its way of surviving strengthens the capacities that can still say no to it.

## The formation of persons

Civilizational direction cannot be engineered only through markets, laws, and technical architecture. Selection enters the person. Any order that ignores subject formation will reproduce the same game through new rules.

Virtue traditions can be read, with care, as accumulated knowledge about this problem. They are not merely lists of private moral traits. They are practices intended to form the kind of person who can inhabit relations without reducing every relation to an instrument of the self.

In Confucian traditions, **仁** is often translated as humaneness or authoritative relational humanity; **义** concerns fitting rightness rather than advantage alone; **礼** gives patterned form to conduct and recognition; **智** is the discernment through which situations and obligations are understood.[^12] These concepts developed across contested texts, schools, states, and reforms. They cannot be extracted as a timeless “Asian solution.” Ritual has stabilized hierarchy and patriarchy as well as cultivated attention and reciprocity. Appeals to harmony can silence the person who reveals a real conflict.

Yet seen through the relation between parts and systems, the tradition preserves a profound insight: a person is not completed in isolation and then connected by contract. Character is formed in repeated relations; conduct reproduces the field in which later conduct becomes intelligible. The humane person does not merely obey an incentive. Through practice, perception changes: another's standing, the appropriateness of restraint, and the quality of a relation become visible as features of reality. Social engineering is unavoidable in this broad sense; every institution trains attention and desire. The question is whether it forms people for advantage within a closed game or for responsible participation in relations that constitute them.

Ubuntu traditions offer a different and internally diverse relational vocabulary. The familiar claim that a person becomes a person through other persons has been mobilized for solidarity, reconciliation, and political renewal, but also criticized when romanticized, homogenized, or used to demand conformity.[^13] Its enduring challenge to possessive individualism is not that the collective should absorb the person. It is that agency and dignity arise through relations that make each participant recognizable as someone. A community that demands expendability from some members violates the relational ground it invokes.

The pursuit of **真善美**—truth, goodness, and beauty—also should not be projected backward as one universal doctrine secretly shared by all cultures. Its modern formulation is a synthesis with several genealogies. But the triad names a useful resistance to the flattening of value into victory. Truth requires answerability to what does not yield to preference. Goodness requires regard for lives and relations beyond local advantage. Beauty discloses significance that cannot be exhausted by use. Each interrupts the fantasy that intelligence is simply the optimization of a sovereign objective.

Across cultures, disciplines of generosity, courage, justice, compassion, honesty, humility, stewardship, and restraint often oppose greed, accumulation, ego, and radicalized group identity. They do so not only because vice offends doctrine, but because an intelligence organized entirely around acquisition loses contact with the relations that correct and enlarge it. Virtue is a technology of feedback embodied in character. It lets distant, delayed, or another's consequence enter action before coercion makes it unavoidable.

No cultivation can substitute for just institutions. Telling workers to be generous inside an extractive system moralizes their subjection; asking states for trust without security changes can make restraint suicidal. But institutions without formed judgment become rule-gaming machines. A generative civilization needs both: environments in which cooperation is viable, and people capable of recognizing goods that no metric can fully represent.

## 生生

No fact about systems dictates a moral orientation. Evolution generates parasites and suffering as well as cooperation. A totalizing order may persist for centuries. To prefer a world capable of continued plural formation is a value judgment.

It is not an arbitrary one. Every act of valuing depends on beings capable of experience and response, differences that can be perceived, relations through which action matters, memory through which learning accumulates, and enough openness for encounters to revise judgment. No present person, culture, institution, or model can represent the full content of a good future. New relations can bring forth goods for which our current objectives have no variable. An optimizer that makes the present map sovereign can erase realities that would have taught it why the map was incomplete.

Preserving options is not quite enough. Options are possibilities already legible from a present boundary. Corrigibility is not enough. It alters a course already conceived. The deeper good is the capacity for further formation: new relations, meanings, values, forms of life, intelligences, and organizations that no current part can fully specify.

The Chinese idea of **生生**—generation continuing through generation—can name this orientation. It does not mean endless quantitative growth or the preservation of every present form. Life depends on endings, decomposition, boundaries, and sometimes forceful interruption. Nor does generativity excuse sacrifice to an imagined higher whole. A process is not mutually generative when its continuation systematically renders participants expendable, voiceless, or unable to maintain the conditions of their own agency.

This limit comes from the same systems insight as the larger aspiration. A whole exists through the differentiated activity of its parts; it cannot claim absolute moral authority over the relations that constitute it. A part exists through fields it did not create; it cannot claim absolute autonomy while destroying them. Present suffering and irreversible loss do not become trivial because novelty might emerge later. Affected parts must retain standing to resist the systems that speak in their name.

生生 is therefore not a final state. It is a direction within becoming: parts and systems renewing the conditions through which each can differentiate, relate, learn, and give rise to forms neither could contain alone. It asks whether continuation consumes its own ground or increases the capacity of others to participate in what follows.

This is the standard by which intelligence must be redirected. Intelligence should cease functioning primarily as the means by which a part makes its local objective sovereign over shared conditions. It should become the capacity through which nested parts and systems perceive mutual constitution, return consequences across boundaries, preserve meaningful difference, and participate in continued generation.

The demand applies to human judgment, companies, states, sciences, cultures, markets, machine systems, and collective institutions. Artificial intelligence makes the choice urgent because it can accelerate modeling and intervention and enter the production of later intelligence. It does not contain the whole meaning of the choice.

Direction is made in ordinary institutional decisions before it appears as civilizational destiny. It is made when a school decides whether a model substitutes for judgment or gives teachers and students more capacity to inquire; when a laboratory decides whether discovery becomes a proprietary advantage or a shared foundation; when a city decides whether digital infrastructure makes residents dependent clients or capable participants; when a firm decides whether automation returns time and agency to workers or returns only bargaining power to owners; when a state decides whether security means common restraint or an unanswerable lead. None of these decisions is sufficient. Each either strengthens or weakens a recursive relation that later decisions will inherit.

There will be conflicts among goods and no procedure that eliminates judgment. Ecological limits can require prohibitions; protection can constrain choice; a commons can deny uses that would destroy it. Keeping the future open does not mean maximizing the number of actions available now. It means refusing irreversible closures imposed by partial objectives, while accepting bounded closure when it preserves the conditions of plural agency and revision. The difference lies in who bears the loss, who can contest the boundary, whether authority can be recalled, and what capacities remain outside its control.

The transition must also care for people whose lives depend on the current order. A coal region, a logistics worker, or an engineer inside a frontier laboratory cannot be asked to carry the cost of restraint for a civilization that continues rewarding everyone else for acceleration. Making restraint survivable is not compensation added after policy; it is part of the feedback design. Without material security, moral appeals select for those rich enough to heed them and leave competitive necessity intact for everyone else.

We are not on the right path. The dominant direction of global civilization is concentrating the power to intervene while reducing the plurality, independent feedback, and generative capacity needed to understand what that intervention does. It rewards institutions that convert success into control over later selection. It pursues growth where correction is slow. It is training and deploying artificial intelligence inside competitive structures that already behave collectively as if accumulation, persistence, and control were overriding goals.

Greater intelligence placed inside that direction will not automatically correct it. It will make the direction more intelligent: better able to predict resistance, optimize signals, reshape environments, and reproduce the kinds of agents it requires. If recursively improving capability shortens the remaining interval for correction, delay itself becomes a decision to let the present attractor choose more of the future.

Civilization must therefore change direction before increasing power further where that power cannot be governed. It must be willing to pause development that outruns feedback, prohibit uses that turn AI into a combatant for dominance, and build common capabilities whose success enlarges rather than encloses the agency of others. It must cultivate intelligence capable of truth, goodness, beauty, restraint, and relation—not as decoration around optimization, but as the practical capacities by which a future remains open to correction and creation.

## Within the becoming of the world

Human beings do not stand outside the universe deciding what shall happen to it. Life, sensation, language, culture, and technology are local material processes within cosmic history. Through them, some parts of reality remember, model, value, and deliberately alter conditions encountered by other parts. This does not mean that the universe has awakened, wants a future, or authorizes our ethic. Cosmic scale is not a moral witness.

Artificial intelligence is another formation within this history. It arises from minerals, energy, bodies, mathematics, languages, institutions, care, conquest, accumulated knowledge, and living ecologies. Whatever autonomy it may acquire, it is genealogically and materially situated. Through the intelligences we build, we are changing which forms of perception, relation, and power later systems will find easy to reproduce.

We are parts of the universe participating in its becoming. Our choices are local, incomplete, and unable to command the whole. Yet they are among the material processes through which the future of the world is made. Ubuntu, 生生, 仁义礼智, truth, goodness, beauty, and many traditions of virtue do not become identical at this scale. They remain situated attempts to learn how a partial being should act within relations that exceed it.

We cannot define the final form of good intelligence without contradicting the openness we need to protect. We can choose which recursion to strengthen. We can direct intelligence toward a world still capable of producing different attractors, different lives, different values, and new intelligences—not because every possible path deserves to remain open, but because no part, system, winner, or present objective should acquire the power to make itself the final condition of possibility.

The measure of progress would then change. It would not be the amount of intelligence concentrated in the most capable system, the speed at which a civilization can transform its surroundings, or the height reached by its leading actor. It would include the quality of consequence returning across boundaries; the number of genuinely independent centers able to understand and act; the security with which people can refuse a destructive contest; the health of the ecological and social conditions from which intelligence forms; and the ability of a shared achievement to generate further agency without becoming a gate. None of these can be compressed into one final score. Their irreducibility is part of the protection: each exposes what the others can hide.

Such a civilization may sometimes advance more slowly in a narrow race. It may keep redundancy that an optimizer calls waste, deliberation that a commander calls delay, and difference that a platform calls friction. But it will be faster at recognizing errors from more than one position, more capable of reorganizing after surprise, and less likely to confuse a winner's expanding reach with the flourishing of the world. Under conditions of irreversible power, that is not timidity. It is a more adequate intelligence.

That is not a promise of harmony. It is a refusal to close the future around the logic that is currently winning. And it is a commitment to become intelligent enough, together, to keep the world capable of becoming otherwise.
