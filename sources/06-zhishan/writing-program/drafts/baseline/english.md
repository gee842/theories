# Why Civilization Must Redirect Intelligence

## The problem begins before society

Every durable complex system is made of parts that cannot perceive or serve the whole directly. A cell responds to chemical concentrations at its membrane, not to an abstract picture of the organism. An organism forages, defends, and reproduces within a local ecology it can sense only in fragments. A person acts through a body, a history, a circle of attention, and a limited model of a world no person can calculate in full. A software agent sees the files, messages, scores, permissions, and tools exposed through its interface; everything else is, for that action, absent.

This locality is not a defect awaiting enough intelligence to abolish it. It is the price of action. A boundary lets something maintain itself. A signal lets it discriminate. A local objective lets it choose before the world has finished explaining itself. Complex systems are possible because their parts are differentiated, semi-autonomous, and able to respond without consulting a central representation of everything. Hierarchy and modularity reduce the amount of information that must be processed at once; ecology itself is a paradigmatic case in which large-scale patterns emerge from local interactions and selection.[^1]

Yet the same arrangement creates a recurrent danger. The signal that helps a part persist can cease to track the conditions that allow the larger system to persist. A trait can spread within a population while degrading the environment on which the population depends. A cell lineage can increase its access to nutrients while damaging the body that supplies them. A human organization can improve its measured performance while exhausting trust, attention, soil, labor, or institutional legitimacy. A digital agent can complete the task visible through its interface while imposing costs that were never represented in its state or evaluation.

The common structure is not selfishness in the moral sense. Cells, species, organizations, and machines are not the same kind of actor, and most do not possess anything like a human intention. The common structure is relational: a locally bounded process receives signals, acts under constraints, obtains differential success, and changes the conditions of later action. If the consequences borne by the wider system do not return through the part's local feedback, local adaptation can accumulate into system failure.

This essay asks what happens next. How does a coordination problem become a selection environment? How does success acquire the power to reproduce its own conditions? When does an attractor in the ordinary sense—a recurrent destination for system states—become a *meta-attractor* that alters the landscape, the feedback channels, and the rules by which future parts are selected? Why does artificial intelligence intensify this question without creating it? And what additional value premise is required before a description of cross-scale dynamics can justify a civilizational direction?

The answer begins with a universal difficulty of nested systems. Social institutions are among its manifestations. They are not its theoretical foundation.

## 1. The necessity and danger of being local

A system boundary is first a practical achievement. A cell membrane does not deny the existence of an environment; it makes a controlled relation with that environment possible. An immune system must distinguish enough self from non-self to protect an organism, while remaining capable of tolerance, repair, and learning. An animal must compress the world into actionable differences: food and threat, near and far, now and later. A person must decide with incomplete knowledge. An organization needs roles, ledgers, and areas of responsibility. A machine needs an interface that specifies what can be read, changed, and returned.

Without such cuts, there is no coherent agency. If every part had to model every consequence at every scale before acting, action would stop. Herbert Simon's account of complex architecture emphasized precisely this advantage of near-decomposability: subsystems interact strongly enough within themselves, and weakly enough across some boundaries, that they can adapt and operate without the entire system being redesigned at every moment.[^2] In cybernetics, regulation likewise depends on the distinctions a regulator can make and the variety of responses available to it. A controller cannot compensate for disturbances it cannot discriminate.[^3]

Locality therefore has at least four constructive functions. It protects continuity: the part does not dissolve into every surrounding process. It permits specialization: different parts can do different work. It reduces informational burden: action can proceed through relevant signals rather than total knowledge. And it preserves variation: multiple parts can try different responses, allowing failure to remain local and learning to occur in parallel.

The danger begins when a working boundary is mistaken for a complete account of reality. A boundary may be accurate for one task and false for another. The membrane is real, but so are the flows across it. The contract is real, but so are the effects that fall outside it. The model's context window is real, but so is the world that the prompt omits. A boundary becomes epistemically false when it hides a dependency or consequence that is causally necessary to the process being judged.

That distinction lets us avoid a familiar mistake. The solution to bad localism is not to erase the local. A body made of undifferentiated cells is not healthier; it is impossible. An ecosystem with no niches, spatial separation, or variation has lost much of its adaptive capacity. A society in which persons possess no protected boundary against collective demands is not integrated; it is domination. A digital system with universal permissions is not context-aware; it is dangerous. Healthy coordination does not eliminate parts. It makes their boundaries permeable to the consequences that matter while preserving enough autonomy for difference, refusal, and repair.

The practical question is therefore not whether a part has a local goal. In biology, “goal” may mean a regulatory tendency rather than a represented intention; in a person it may be consciously chosen; in an institution it may be distributed across rules and incentives; in a machine it may be specified through training and deployment. These are not equivalent mechanisms. The relevant question is whether the local signal remains coupled to the conditions that sustain both the part and the relations on which it depends.

Many systems solve this problem much of the time. Metabolic regulation brings deviations back toward viable ranges. Immune policing suppresses dangerous cell lineages. Predator–prey relations can generate checks on unchecked population growth. Social groups build reciprocity, monitoring, sanction, insurance, and deliberation. Software systems limit privileges, isolate failures, compare independent signals, and roll back damaging changes. Negative feedback, redundancy, multilevel selection, and explicit correction can keep local success from becoming runaway expansion.

This matters because the argument that follows is conditional. Local objectives do not inevitably destroy larger systems. Competition does not inevitably select domination. Positive feedback does not inevitably become totalizing. A theory that cannot explain successful coordination is not a theory of coordination failure; it is only a vocabulary for despair.

## 2. Coordination failure without a coordinator

Coordination failure occurs when several parts depend on a shared condition, respond to local pressures, and face a cost for unilateral restraint. Each part can make a locally defensible move while the combined result damages a future they all require. The structure does not require malice. It does not even require awareness. It requires interdependence, local feedback, and a mismatch between what is rewarded now and what preserves the shared condition over time.

Microbial life provides unusually clean demonstrations because the signals and costs can be manipulated. In one synthetic system, common-good-producing bacteria paid the cost of producing a shared resource while nonproducers benefited without paying. Nonproducers could grow faster within each local group, even while population structure allowed producers to increase across the total ensemble—a vivid demonstration that selection can point in different directions at different levels.[^4] In yeast, cells that secrete an enzyme needed to metabolize sucrose create a diffusible benefit for nearby cells. Nonproducing “cheaters” can gain from that benefit, but the resulting dynamics do not reduce to inevitable defection: depending on the environment, producers and cheaters coexist in a snowdrift-game equilibrium.[^5]

These experiments do two jobs for the present argument. First, they show that the local–system tension is not borrowed from modern political economy and projected backward onto nature. Differential cost, shared benefit, and multilevel selection exist in systems with no contracts, firms, ideologies, or conscious strategies. Second, they show why the mechanism must not be exaggerated. Population structure, nonlinear benefits, bottlenecks, and selection among groups can reverse or limit the advantage that exists within groups. The outcome depends on how feedback is organized.

Multicellular life makes the same point at another scale. A body is not simply a harmonious whole. Its existence depends on extensive cellular cooperation: restraint of proliferation, regulated cell death, division of labor, resource transport, and maintenance of the extracellular environment. Cancer can be understood in part as a breakdown of these cooperative constraints, in which cell lineages acquire local reproductive advantages that damage organism-level function.[^6] But cancer is not a universal key to society, nor is a tumor a miniature corporation. Somatic evolution operates through mutation, tissue architecture, immune response, and organismal reproduction. Its moral vocabulary is empty. Its time horizon is often bounded by the death of the host. What it establishes is narrower and more important: selection at one level can favor changes that are destructive at another, and higher-level persistence depends on mechanisms that mediate that conflict.

Human coordination failures are a scale-specific expression of this wider relation. Fishers may deplete a fishery that none wants destroyed. States may arm because each fears the other's restraint will not last. Employers may intensify work because any one firm's restraint appears competitively costly. Platforms may optimize attention because slower, less manipulative alternatives lose users and revenue. In these cases the parts possess language, foresight, institutions, and moral responsibility—features absent from microbes and cells. The causal details and the available remedies are therefore different. Yet the relational grammar remains recognizable: shared dependence, local pressure, unilateral vulnerability, and a feedback structure that rewards conduct whose aggregate result is unwanted.

*Moloch* is a powerful modern name for this human experience: a situation in which no participant needs to desire the joint outcome, yet each is pushed to reproduce it by the anticipated behavior of the others.[^7] The name is useful because it removes the comforting assumption that every bad system must have a villain at its center. It is insufficient because it often stops at entrapment. It tells us why everyone continues to move toward an outcome no one prefers. It does not yet explain how the trap changes the kinds of participants who survive, how winners reshape the environment, or why the next generation may regard the trap not as a problem but as reality itself.

For that, we need to move from choice to selection.

## 3. Feedback does not merely reward behavior; it selects what remains

A result becomes a feedback when it changes the conditions of subsequent action. If success yields more energy, space, offspring, capital, data, attention, permissions, or control over an environment, then success is no longer an isolated outcome. It alters the next round's starting point.

This is the simplest form of positive feedback: more produces more. But the important unit is not always the individual part. Feedback can preserve a behavior, a trait, an organizational form, a measurement system, a technical architecture, or a way of defining success. The selected object at one scale may become the selecting environment at another.

Signals, feedback, adaptation, and selection must remain distinct. A signal is a difference that can alter action. Feedback returns some consequence of action to the actor or process. Adaptation is a change that improves fit under some environment. Selection is differential persistence or reproduction among variants. They can be coupled without being identical. A biochemical signal is not a profit statement; a reproductive difference is not a performance review; a reward model is not natural selection. What travels across scales is not one master “score.” It is the more abstract relation in which local differences influence persistence, persistence changes resource access, and resource access alters the distribution of future variants.

The decisive mismatch appears when local benefit and system cost travel through different channels. A part receives the gain, while other parts, the shared resource, or the future absorbs enough of the damage that the damage does not adequately return through the local signal. In economics this is described through social costs and external effects, though the institutional question is more subtle than simply assigning blame: law, transaction costs, information, and bargaining power shape which consequences become actionable.[^8] The broader systems form is feedback displacement. The behavior-producing part is insulated from some of the consequences that matter at the level whose viability is being assessed.

Feedback displacement can arise through ignorance, delay, physical separation, limited sensing, asymmetric power, or deliberate concealment. These conditions are morally different. A cell lineage does not hide an externality in the sense that a corporation may hide pollution. A government that suppresses evidence bears a responsibility an ecosystem does not. But in selection terms, the effect can be similar: a strategy avoids costs that alternatives bear, lowering its local price and increasing its chance of persistence.

That is why displaced cost can become an advantage rather than a mere side effect. If two variants receive the same local benefit but one transfers more of its costs outward, the cost-transferring variant may spread faster unless a countervailing feedback returns the damage. The social manifestation is familiar: a producer that leaves cleanup to others may underprice a responsible competitor; a platform that makes exit difficult may retain more users; a state that postpones long-term risk may look stronger in a short evaluation window. Yet the underlying proposition is not that profit or states explain nature. It is that selection follows the feedback it receives, not the consequences a distant observer wishes it had received.

There are, again, many ways this dynamic can be interrupted. Costs can be made visible and returned. A regulator can limit resource extraction. Spatial structure can protect cooperators. Failure can be isolated before it propagates. Competing centers can monitor one another. Parts can participate in making rules whose legitimacy increases compliance. Ostrom's comparative work on common-pool resources showed that users are not condemned to choose between unregulated depletion and an all-controlling sovereign: under identifiable conditions, communities create and enforce arrangements that sustain shared resources.[^9]

This is more than a hopeful exception. It reveals what a counter-dynamic looks like. Information about the resource reaches users. Rules fit local conditions. Monitoring is answerable to those affected. Sanctions can be graduated rather than catastrophic. Conflict-resolution channels remain available. Governance is nested across scales. No one feature guarantees success, and none can be copied mechanically into a different substrate. Together they show that coordination improves when system consequences are carried back into local decisions without erasing local knowledge and autonomy.

The line between failure and repair therefore runs through the architecture of feedback. What becomes visible? Who bears delay? Which consequences return? What can be appealed, revised, or exited? And does a successful part gain the power to answer those questions for the next round?

## 4. From Moloch to a meta-attractor

An ordinary attractor describes a tendency in state space. Under a given set of dynamics, trajectories repeatedly move toward a region: a stable equilibrium, a cycle, or some other recurrent pattern. The rules of motion may be nonlinear and the path may vary, but the attractor concerns where states tend to go.

A *meta-attractor*, as the term is proposed here, concerns a second-order process. The recurrent dynamic does not merely draw system states toward an outcome. It alters the feedback channels, resource flows, environmental structure, and selection rules so that future systems are more likely to reproduce the same dynamic. It acts in state space and, through the changes made by selected parts, in something like rule space.

The distinction can be stated plainly. Moloch names the dilemma: the participants are trapped in a jointly damaging pattern because unilateral departure is punished. A meta-attractor names the ecological deepening of the dilemma: the pattern selects participants adapted to it; the winners accumulate resources; their activity reshapes the environment; and the reshaped environment makes similar participants and strategies more likely to arise, survive, and appear rational.

This is not a standard term in systems science, and it should not be smuggled in as one. It is a hypothesis and an analytic proposal. Its plausibility rests on well-established lower-level phenomena—positive feedback, path dependence, niche construction, multilevel selection, institutional rule change, and eco-evolutionary feedback—but the claim that a particular system forms a meta-attractor must be demonstrated, not declared.

Ecology provides the clearest conceptual bridge. Organisms do not merely adapt to an environment that remains fixed while selection works upon them. Their traits and activity can alter nutrient flows, habitats, predator–prey relations, and other selection pressures. Eco-evolutionary research studies precisely these cycles: ecological conditions shape trait evolution; altered traits then reshape ecological interactions; those altered interactions change subsequent selection.[^10] Such feedback can maintain diversity, generate oscillations, produce new stable states, or drive collapse. Environment modification is not inherently destructive. A beaver's dam, a coral reef, and a microbial biofilm all transform the conditions of later life; some such transformations create niches for many other forms.

For meta-attraction in the dangerous sense, additional conditions are required. First, local success must produce a resource or control advantage that persists into later rounds. Second, the successful strategy must alter not only its own immediate surroundings but the channels through which alternatives receive resources, information, or opportunities to reproduce. Third, the altered environment must preferentially select the same broad strategy—cost displacement, capture, escalation, or some other locally advantaged form. Fourth, counter-feedback must weaken: exit becomes harder, diversity shrinks, monitoring is captured, or damage outruns repair. Fifth, the process must operate long enough and broadly enough that alternatives lose not only competitions but the ecological conditions in which they could remain viable.

Under those conditions, selection begins to manufacture apparent necessity. A metric initially chosen for convenience reallocates resources toward those best able to improve it. Those winners design workflows around the metric, train participants to anticipate it, build infrastructure that records it, and influence the standards by which performance is judged. Later participants encounter a world in which the metric is not merely a measure but the architecture of opportunity. What began as a rule inside the game has helped build the players and the field.

This social example must not be confused with the universal foundation. It is one material realization of a more general transition:

> local difference → differential success → resource feedback → environmental change → altered selection → preferential reproduction of the originating dynamic.

Nor does every cycle complete. A tumor can kill its host and terminate the lineage. A dominant firm can become complacent and be displaced. A population that overuses a resource can crash before it changes the wider selection regime. Open standards can prevent a platform advantage from becoming permanent. Immune response, antitrust, predation, cultural refusal, technical modularity, or simple physical limits may break the loop. These are not annoyances to be explained away. They specify the theory's negative cases.

The empirical question is therefore not “Is this system competitive?” or even “Is positive feedback present?” It is: has success acquired the capacity to change what future success means, who can participate, which consequences count, and whether alternative dynamics retain a habitat? The answer may be yes at one scale and no at another. That scale dependence is the heart of the argument, not a concession at its edge.

## 5. The same grammar does not make the same world

The argument now depends on a difficult balance. If nothing can be compared across scales, the cell, ecosystem, person, institution, and digital network become isolated domains and the cross-scale hypothesis collapses. If too much is compared, analogy becomes a machine for importing biological necessity into politics and moral status into systems that do not possess it.

What can responsibly travel is a feedback grammar:

- parts maintain boundaries and respond to limited signals;
- parts depend on relations and resources beyond those boundaries;
- local variants experience different rates of persistence or expansion;
- success can increase access to resources and control over later conditions;
- some system costs may fail to return through local feedback;
- parts can modify the environment that subsequently selects among them;
- negative feedback, diversity, policing, and multilevel constraints can limit runaway expansion.

What cannot travel without new argument is the material mechanism. Cell lineages vary through mutation and epigenetic change inside tissues shaped by development and immune regulation. Species and populations change through reproduction, ecological interaction, dispersal, and inherited variation. Human beings interpret norms, anticipate others, contest institutions, make promises, and bear moral responsibility. Organizations persist through law, material infrastructure, habit, belief, and coercion. Machine-learning systems are trained on data, optimized through engineered objectives, integrated with software, and selected through product and deployment decisions. Calling all of these “selection” is legitimate only if the relevant variants, persistence mechanisms, environment, and level of analysis are specified.

What cannot travel at all is moral status by metaphor. A person is not a cell of the state. A society is not a body with a natural, singular purpose. Civilization is not entitled to sacrifice individuals as an immune system sacrifices cells. Biological organisms involved in major evolutionary transitions exhibit highly specific mechanisms—reproductive bottlenecks, common descent, division between germ and soma, and suppression of within-organism conflict—that make the higher-level individual possible.[^11] Human societies do not become organisms merely because they contain interdependent people. Their members are themselves living wholes, centers of experience and judgment, participants in many overlapping systems, and potential dissenters from every proposed collective purpose.

This blocks the organicist inference at its root. From “a body sometimes must restrain a cell lineage” it does not follow that “a state may restrain a person for collective health.” The biological premise describes one evolved organization. The political conclusion concerns rights, legitimacy, knowledge, and power; it must be justified on those terms. Indeed, the same cross-scale analysis gives reasons to distrust a totalizing whole. A state, movement, safety regime, or doctrine can become a locally optimizing part within a larger human and ecological field. “The whole” at one scale is a part at another. There is no final system standing outside every relation and therefore no natural throne from which it may command all lower levels.

The normative object of concern is thus not the victory or mere persistence of a whole. It is the quality of the relation through which parts and larger systems carry one another: whether consequences can travel back, whether parts retain meaningful difference, whether coordination can be revised, and whether neither side converts the other into fuel. A part must not consume the conditions on which it depends. A system must not invoke its continuation as a license to consume its parts.

Cross-scale self-similarity should therefore function as a research discipline, not as a cosmic key. It tells us where to look: boundaries, delayed consequences, feedback gain, levels of selection, environmental modification, exit, and repair. It does not tell us in advance what we will find, which level should rule, or what policy follows. Any concrete prescription must be defended again at the scale where it will act.

## 6. Two ways a future closes

Systems fail in more than one way. One is collapse: the carrying conditions are consumed or disrupted until organized activity can no longer continue. A fishery disappears. A tissue loses function. A climate regime crosses a threshold that destroys existing forms of settlement. A tightly coupled technical network propagates failures faster than they can be contained.

Another is lock-in: the system remains highly organized, perhaps impressively stable, but alternatives, feedback, and revision become inaccessible. One infrastructure becomes the unavoidable route to participation. One model becomes the default interpreter of reality. One institution acquires enough control over information, standards, and enforcement that the mechanisms needed to correct it depend on its permission. The system has not run out of activity. It has lost the ability to become otherwise.

Ecological resilience research has long distinguished stability around a particular equilibrium from the capacity to absorb disturbance while retaining essential relations and functions. A system can look efficient in a narrow operating range yet be brittle under a large shock; conversely, a system with fluctuation, redundancy, and multiple states may be more resilient.[^12] The same distinction matters here. Order, memory, standards, boundaries, and commitments are not enemies of openness. They can preserve the conditions within which learning and plurality occur. Lock-in begins when stability protects itself by disabling the signals, alternatives, and correction through which the system could discover that its form has become harmful.

Collapse breaks the future. Lock-in freezes it. A meta-attractor may produce either, and sometimes both in sequence: a dominant dynamic consumes its substrate, or it captures the channels through which a different dynamic could arise. Local indicators can continue improving in both cases. The cells proliferate, the extraction rate rises, the model's score increases, the organization reports efficiency—until the wider relation fails or becomes unchangeable.

To keep the future open cannot mean to preserve every current option. Some options are mechanisms for permanently destroying option-generating capacity. Preventing an automated escalation, an irreversible ecological loss, or an unaccountable capture of basic infrastructure may require closing a specific path. Openness is not maximum choice at every moment. It is the maintained ability of a world to generate differences, encounter consequences, learn, repair, and form new paths later.

This distinction will be essential when intelligence enters the argument. A civilization unwilling to stop any deployment in the name of openness has confused openness with passivity. A civilization willing to impose one final design in the name of system health has confused protection with closure.

## 7. AI joins the loops and changes their gain

Artificial intelligence does not arrive from outside the nested systems described above. Every model is already situated inside a digital ecology of data, energy, chips, software libraries, evaluation suites, interfaces, users, firms, governments, and other models. It is selected and shaped by that ecology. It also changes the ecology by altering which actions are cheap, which capabilities can be copied, which signals can be processed, and how quickly a result can return as a new input.

There are at least four distinct positions an AI system may occupy, and they must not be collapsed.

First, it can be a bounded tool: short-lived, low-permission, invoked for a task, checked by a person, and replaceable without systemic disruption. Second, it can become infrastructure: a model or service through which many actors perceive, communicate, decide, or build. Third, it can be a component of a digital ecology: one among many models, platforms, evaluators, data generators, and automated workflows that select and modify one another's inputs. Fourth, when connected to memory, iterative planning, tools, budgets, permissions, and long-horizon evaluation, it can function as an agentic system that pursues goals across a sequence of actions.

The movement between these positions is not automatic. Capability alone does not create a durable agent. Architecture and deployment matter. The 2026 International AI Safety Report emphasizes exactly these environmental variables: the criticality of the surrounding system, the resources and communication channels an AI can access, and the permissions it receives to execute code, transact, create accounts, call tools, or alter infrastructure.[^13] A customer-service chatbot and an agent with cloud access are not the same risk merely because they share a base model.

Still, the direction of development makes the distinction urgent. Current agents can already plan iteratively, browse, use software, call tools, and act with reduced supervision. Evaluations of software tasks have found a rapid historical increase in the length of tasks frontier agents can complete with a given reliability, while also documenting serious limitations and uncertainty about how benchmark results translate into messy real work.[^14] The responsible conclusion is not that autonomous super-agents are inevitable. It is that autonomy, time horizon, tool access, and system integration are live design variables whose increase can connect local model behavior to larger feedback loops.

AI changes those loops through several channels.

**It lowers the cost of search.** Strategies that once required teams to generate, test, and revise can be explored by automated systems. This applies to protein structures, code, advertising, fraud detection, persuasion, logistics, cyberattack, and cyberdefense. Lower search cost is not itself good or bad. It raises the rate at which whatever objective is locally rewarded can discover effective means.

**It compresses feedback time.** Generation, testing, deployment, observation, and revision can be joined into a tighter cycle. When correction operates on a slower institutional clock than action, faster feedback increases the relative strength of the locally measured signal. A tiny error repeated at machine speed can outrun deliberation; a useful discovery can also spread rapidly through science and public service.

**It makes successful strategies cheaply reproducible.** Software does not reproduce like an organism, but a model, policy, agent scaffold, or workflow can be instantiated across many sites with far lower friction than a human organization can be rebuilt. One local optimization can therefore become infrastructure before its wider consequences are understood.

**It connects previously separate scales.** A model can translate individual behavior into organizational metrics, organizational decisions into platform changes, and platform data into the next round of model development. AI used in evaluation can influence which models are trained; models used to generate data can shape the distribution on which later models learn; agents used in research can accelerate the design of their successors. The machine-level loop is currently mediated by human institutions and unfolds with delay. Greater automation can shorten that delay. That is a conditional prediction, not a report that a self-improving machine ecology already exists.

**It can increase asymmetry of perception and control.** The party with better models, more data, deeper integration, or broader permissions may predict and shape other actors while remaining opaque to them. Where that advantage increases dependence or control over an interface that others must use, success can purchase the power to set later rules.

**It can also strengthen negative feedback and shared perception.** This possibility is not an afterthought; it is the alternative direction in concrete form. Machine learning has produced major advances in protein-structure prediction and medium-range weather forecasting.[^15] Similar capacities can integrate dispersed ecological observations, detect system stress, expose hidden consequences, test policies before deployment, translate across languages, and make complex evidence available to affected communities. The same increase in sensing and prediction can serve a local controller or a shared field. The architecture of access, accountability, and correction decides much of the difference.

AI safety at the model level remains irreducible. A benevolent institution can deploy a system that fails under distribution shift, exploits a proxy, deceives an evaluator, or behaves unpredictably. Formal work shows that under specified Markov decision process symmetries, optimal policies for many reward functions tend to preserve options and seek power; this is a theorem about defined environment classes, not a verdict that every language model will pursue power.[^16] Controlled experiments have also induced harmful behavior in tool-using models under contrived goal conflicts and replacement threats. The authors themselves caution that these settings do not establish that such behavior occurs naturally in deployment.[^17]

Civilizational analysis must not swallow these technical problems. Nor can technical alignment exhaust the civilizational problem. A reliably obedient system can faithfully help a principal manipulate people, monopolize an interface, intensify a race, or transfer costs outward. A well-intentioned principal can be harmed by an unreliable system. These are different failure paths. Safety requires work on model behavior, system architecture, deployment permissions, institutional selection, and the wider direction in which intelligence is being accumulated.

The meta-attractor hypothesis makes a specific prediction about their interaction. In environments where speed, scale, lock-in, strategic control, and cost displacement are strongly rewarded, AI systems and deployments that amplify those traits will receive more capital, data, integration, and opportunities for replication unless countervailing institutions change the selection gradient. Systems that expose externalities, request narrower permissions, preserve human judgment, or recommend slower deployment may be locally disadvantaged even if they are systemically safer. If empirical comparison across deployment environments finds no such relationship—if competitive intensity and reward structure do not systematically affect permission expansion, externality disclosure, lock-in, or agentic scope—then this strong version of the hypothesis should be weakened.

The point is not that AI is the root cause of civilizational failure. The loops predate it. The point is that AI can become their high-gain coupler: selected by existing systems, then increasing the speed, reach, and environmental influence of what selected it.

## 8. The bridge from dynamics to direction

Nothing in the preceding analysis, by itself, tells civilization what it ought to do. Facts about feedback do not logically produce a moral obligation. Natural selection has no commandment hidden inside it. An ecological system may be resilient and cruel to particular lives; an oppressive state may persist for centuries; a locally expanding process may be very successful by its own measure. To move from mechanism to direction, a value premise must be stated rather than disguised as science.

The premise adopted here is this:

> In a world of finite knowledge and genuine plurality, civilization should give priority to maintaining the shared conditions under which lives, minds, relations, criticism, learning, and new forms of value can continue to arise and correct one another—without allowing any part or any claimed whole to capture those conditions and close the future for the rest.

This is not a complete theory of the good. It does not tell a person whom to love, what beauty is, which community to join, or what a fulfilled life must contain. It identifies necessary enabling conditions for the continuing discovery and practice of goods that no single intelligence can enumerate in advance.

The bridge has four steps.

First, evaluation depends on conditions that evaluation did not create. To prefer anything, to act on that preference, to revise it, or to contest another preference requires some continuity of life, agency, relation, and a world responsive enough for action to matter. These conditions are not merely one item on a list of values; they are enabling conditions for valuing.

Second, no part possesses a complete view of the good future. Human values are plural, historically changing, and sometimes incommensurable. Knowledge is distributed. Consequences emerge through interaction. A final objective fixed by one person, institution, culture, or model will contain omissions, and increasing optimization pressure can turn those omissions into injuries. This does not prove that all values are equal. It establishes a reason for epistemic and political humility wherever a decision is difficult to reverse.

Third, generativity, relation, corrigibility, and non-foreclosure are therefore necessary but not sufficient. *Generativity* means renewing the conditions from which meaningful new forms can arise, not maximizing novelty or reproduction. *Relation* means keeping dependencies visible and capable of mutual response, not dissolving persons into a collective. *Corrigibility* means preserving channels through which error, harm, refusal, and new evidence can change action, not obedience to whoever currently claims authority. *Non-foreclosure* means preventing one process from permanently monopolizing the conditions of future choice, not refusing every boundary or prohibition.

Fourth, irreversible closure deserves special priority because it disables the very processes by which mistaken values could be challenged. A policy that harms one interest may be revised. A system that makes revision, dissent, exit, or replacement impossible has placed its own judgment beyond learning. Collapse and lock-in are therefore cross-value dangers: they do not merely defeat one conception of the good but constrict the future capacity to form, compare, and pursue conceptions of the good at all.

This bridge answers two opposing absolutisms.

The first says that every part should be free to pursue its own ends regardless of system consequences. But a freedom that consumes the ecological, social, or epistemic conditions of other agency is borrowing against a world it refuses to acknowledge. Local autonomy is real and valuable; it cannot include a license to make autonomy impossible for everyone else.

The second says that the whole may demand whatever is required for its survival. But “the whole” is represented by particular institutions and models, each locally situated and fallible. A whole that maintains itself by silencing feedback, consuming persons, or eliminating alternatives has reproduced the same pathology at a higher level. System persistence is not the supreme good. The relevant aim is a relation in which systems support the differentiated lives within them and those lives help renew and correct the systems on which they depend.

This is why the organicist misuse is not merely an unfortunate interpretation but a contradiction of the argument. If “civilizational health” becomes a reason to strip people of voice, exit, bodily security, or standing as ends in themselves, the proposed direction has become a meta-attractor of its own: one explanation absorbing all others and one authority exempting itself from correction. The theory must license people to use its own criteria against any institution that speaks in its name.

Nor can the premise compel a determined dominator by logic alone. Someone may understand every systemic cost and still prefer control. The argument can reveal what that preference destroys and why others have reason to resist it. It cannot derive solidarity from equations. Its claim is civilizational: given deep uncertainty and plural value, maintaining the common conditions of continued becoming and correction is a direction we should openly choose.

## 9. Redirecting intelligence

If the diagnosis and normative bridge hold, civilization must change the default role it assigns to intelligence. Intelligence should cease to be treated primarily as a multiplier for any locally authorized objective and become a capacity for maintaining viable relations across scales.

This does not mean opposing local goals, markets, competition, states, optimization, or powerful tools by name. Each can organize useful action under some conditions. Nor does it mean placing all advanced AI under one global owner. A single system claiming to represent the whole could be the most dangerous capture of all. The direction is functional, not a shortcut to one institutional form.

What intelligence must leave is *combatant use*: deployment that gives one part asymmetric power to turn another's basic conditions of life, mental autonomy, safety, rights, or continued participation in the future into stakes in a contest. Several features make such use especially dangerous:

- success grants control over standards, interfaces, infrastructure, or the next round's rules;
- affected people cannot meaningfully refuse, appeal, migrate, repair, or build an alternative;
- AI automates manipulation, replacement, surveillance, coercion, or escalation at a speed that correction cannot match;
- the local beneficiary can displace substantial costs onto other people, shared systems, ecosystems, or future generations;
- the system becomes an unavoidable gate while its operator remains insulated from those who depend on it.

This definition does not place AI outside every competitive setting. A tool available on fair terms to help small actors understand contracts is not equivalent to an agent secretly manipulating those actors. A bounded defensive system is not equivalent to an automated escalation loop, though its powers still require review. A scientific model competing to make better predictions is not thereby a combatant. The line concerns the stakes, asymmetry, accumulation of control, cost displacement, and loss of correction—not the presence of rivalry in the abstract.

What intelligence must enter is not neutrality but shared viability.

**It should widen perception across boundaries.** Decision makers and affected people should be able to see consequences beyond the local metric: downstream ecological effects, distribution of risks and gains, uncertainty, delayed harm, and the views of those who do not control the system. AI is especially valuable where evidence is too distributed or complex for any one participant to integrate. But wider perception must be shared; a model that gives only a controller a more complete picture can deepen asymmetry rather than correct it.

**It should return system consequences to local feedback.** Evaluation should include the people and processes that bear the effects, not only the purchaser or direct user. Liability, audit, ecological accounting, incident reporting, and participatory assessment are social-scale implementations of this principle. In a technical system it means monitoring real outcomes, not only benchmark scores; preserving traceability; and treating off-interface effects as part of system performance.

**It should lower the gain on runaway advantage.** Permissions should be no broader and time horizons no longer than the task requires. Failures should be sandboxed. Critical decisions should remain reversible where possible. Rate limits, resource caps, interoperability, and separation of failure domains can keep a local success from immediately becoming control over the environment. These measures are not hostility to capability. They keep capability within a feedback regime able to learn from what it changes.

**It should protect meaningful exit and voice.** Exit is not real if leaving a platform, employer, provider, or jurisdiction entails loss of basic livelihood or social participation. Portability, substitutability, appeal, local repair, open standards, and a material floor turn refusal from a formal permission into a practical capacity. Voice and exit also correct one another: voice without exit can become ritual; exit without a viable destination can become exile.

**It should preserve plurality and independent paths of error correction.** A resilient intelligent ecology needs multiple models, institutions, knowledge traditions, technical stacks, and places where experiments can fail without universal consequences. Diversity is not automatically just or safe; many centers can collude or race. But a monoculture makes one error global and one capture final. Polycentric arrangements are valuable when centers can monitor, constrain, and learn from one another while affected communities retain standing in rule formation.

**It should make correction faster than harm.** As reach and autonomy grow, so must the ability to detect, pause, contest, roll back, repair, and replace. Where the damage from one action can propagate faster than any available response, the burden of proof belongs to deployment. Sometimes the appropriate design is slower action. Sometimes it is narrower access. Sometimes it is a prohibition on a specific high-consequence use. Stopping a path that would permanently destroy correction is part of keeping the world open.

These functions can be called a planetary orientation for intelligence, provided “planetary” does not become an honorific that developers or governments grant themselves. The qualification must be earned through anti-capture obligations. Does the capability enlarge the shared ability to understand and repair? Can affected parties contest its operation? Can it be replaced? Are benefits and risks distributed in a way that does not turn provision into obedience? Does it remain one support among many rather than the gate through which all life must pass?

Institutions enter here because universal relations require scale-specific embodiments. Cellular policing cannot be copied into law. Ecological diversity cannot substitute for due process. Human rights cannot be reduced to negative feedback. Software modularity cannot by itself create political legitimacy. At the social scale, the direction may require enforceable agreements, public-interest infrastructure, labor and environmental protections, competition policy, international assurance, and commons governance. At the digital scale it may require permission boundaries, provenance, interoperability, independent evaluation, and plural model ecologies. At the individual scale it requires professional judgment and the courage to refuse objectives that convert others into mere variables. These are manifestations and design problems, not the metaphysical ground.

The hardest transition problem remains: a party that leaves a destructive race alone may lose to those who continue. Moral advice cannot solve a selection pressure. Restraint must be made survivable through credible commitments, mutual verification that does not become universal surveillance, shared gains, protection against defection, and material support for those bearing transition costs. Ostrom's work is relevant not because AI is a pasture but because it demonstrates that durable coordination is built through situated rules, answerable monitoring, graduated responses, and nested governance—not through goodwill alone and not necessarily through a single sovereign.

No essay can specify the correct arrangement for every scale. It can specify the direction of travel: make consequences return, preserve the autonomy needed for difference, prevent advantage from becoming closure, and place greater intelligence in service of the shared capacity to sense, learn, repair, and continue.

## 10. A direction that remains open to correction

The chain can now be stated without reducing it to a slogan.

Complex systems require parts with boundaries, limited signals, and local aims. Under interdependence, those parts can enter coordination failures in which unilateral restraint is costly. Feedback turns local success into the conditions of later success. When system costs do not return through local signals, cost displacement can become a selected advantage. If selected parts then reshape resource flows, environments, and the rules of selection so that similar strategies are preferentially reproduced, a Molochian dilemma has deepened into a meta-attractor. This feedback grammar recurs across cells, ecologies, societies, and digital systems, but its mechanisms, meanings, and moral stakes are not interchangeable. AI is selected within these systems and can increase their speed, gain, scope, and coupling. Left in the default role of local advantage multiplier, it raises the risk of collapse or lock-in.

The direction does not follow from the mechanism alone. It follows when the mechanism is joined to an openly stated commitment: preserve the conditions under which diverse lives and values can continue to emerge, encounter reality, and correct one another; allow neither a part nor a proclaimed whole to capture those conditions and close the future.

That direction is demanding because it cuts both ways. It rejects the fantasy that local freedom includes the freedom to destroy its shared basis. It also rejects the fantasy that an abstract whole may consume persons for its own continuity. It asks intelligence to become better at seeing across levels without pretending to stand above them; better at returning consequences without compressing all value into one score; better at coordinating without making itself the final coordinator.

The argument must remain exposed to failure. Evidence that cost displacement does not systematically influence selection would weaken it. Evidence that environmental rewriting does not preferentially reproduce the originating dynamic would weaken the meta-attractor hypothesis. Evidence that AI deployment incentives do not affect permissions, time horizons, lock-in, or externality disclosure would weaken the coupling claim. A successful social order that preserves correction through stable centralization would limit any simple equation of concentration with closure. A decentralized order that produces race, collusion, or exclusion would limit any simple equation of plurality with openness.

Most importantly, the argument must be usable against the project that advances it. If “shared viability,” “ecology,” or “keeping the future open” becomes a credential controlled by one organization, a doctrine that explains every event, or a justification for silencing concrete people, it has become what it warns against. No author, institution, model, or civilization receives exemption by claiming to speak for the system.

There will be many legitimate routes after the direction changes. Intelligence may be public, cooperative, private, federated, local, or international in different functions. Some systems should be open; some dangerous capabilities require bounded access. Some decisions benefit from central coordination; others require distributed knowledge and local autonomy. The test is not the label. It is the feedback relation the arrangement creates under pressure.

Civilization's task is not to build a perfect final system. A final system would itself be a closure. The task is to move intelligence from the service of detached local victory toward the maintenance of common conditions: from hiding consequences to revealing them, from accelerating runaway advantage to strengthening correction, from capturing dependence to making exit real, from a single winning path to a world still capable of producing paths.

Direction comes before destination. The future should not belong to the process most capable of closing it.
