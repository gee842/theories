# The Choice Before Intelligence

> Humanity has developed enough intelligence to make shared abundance conceivable, but if AI is governed by competitive accumulation, it will automate the very structure that keeps abundance inaccessible. The purpose of AI should therefore be to expand universal capability and preserve many possible futures—not to win a race that collapses the world into one objective.

For most of human history, freedom was constrained by scarcity.

There was not enough food, energy, medicine, transportation, knowledge, or productive capacity to give everyone a secure and comfortable life. Much human labor was physically necessary. Societies had to decide who would work, who would receive, who would rule, and how limited resources would be distributed.

Scarcity did not justify every hierarchy that emerged from it. But it formed the material background against which many of those hierarchies developed.

That background is beginning to change.

Over the last several centuries—and with extraordinary speed over the last few decades—human beings have accumulated scientific knowledge, industrial capacity, computational power, energy systems, global communication, automated production, and increasingly capable artificial intelligence.

We can coordinate activity across continents in seconds. We can design materials at microscopic scales, manufacture objects with extraordinary precision, send machines beyond Earth, produce food with a fraction of the labor once required, and train computational systems to perform intellectual tasks that previously demanded years of human education.

None of this means that every technical problem has been solved. Fully autonomous industrial replication does not yet exist, and building reliable, ecologically sustainable production systems remains difficult. Space travel is still expensive and dangerous. Medicine cannot yet eliminate aging or cure every disease.

But the direction should be clear.

Humanity is approaching a threshold at which the central problem may no longer be whether we possess enough productive intelligence to provide a good life for everyone.

The central problem may be whether we can coordinate that intelligence toward this purpose.

## Technology changes what is politically possible

Imagine an increasingly automated industrial system.

Artificial intelligence assists in designing machines. Machines manufacture components. Robots assemble other robots. Automated mines, refineries, farms, power systems, factories, warehouses, and transportation networks supply one another. Human beings continue to supervise, repair, improve, and govern the system, but progressively less compulsory human labor is required for every unit of production.

Such a system would not literally reproduce like a biological organism without limits. It would still depend on energy, materials, maintenance, ecological boundaries, infrastructure, and human judgment.

But it could increasingly behave like a productive metabolism: taking in energy and materials, maintaining its own components, extending its capacity, and producing what society needs.

This is an almost unimaginable form of power.

Used well, it could provide housing, food, medicine, transportation, communication, education, sanitation, and energy as universal infrastructure.

It could reduce the amount of life people must surrender merely to survive.

It could allow every person to possess what earlier generations could offer only to a privileged minority: security, time, mobility, knowledge, care, and the practical freedom to develop as a human being.

The old dream of a society beyond exploitation need not mean the restoration of a centralized twentieth-century command economy. It need not mean that a state owns every object, dictates every occupation, suppresses difference, or forces everyone into the same form of life.

It could mean something simpler and more humane:

> The foundations of a dignified life are held in common, while the forms of life built upon those foundations remain plural and free.

The floor can rise without lowering the ceiling.

People could still create businesses, institutions, art, communities, technologies, games, and cultural experiments. They could still earn recognition, pursue difficult goals, and choose demanding lives.

But no one would need to submit to domination simply to obtain food, shelter, medicine, or the right to participate in society.

## Abundance would expand freedom rather than abolish difference

A society of shared abundance would not need to make everyone identical.

It could produce more genuine diversity than the present system.

Today, people are told that they are free to choose their lives, but many must organize those lives around the same basic necessity: earning enough money to remain alive. Their clothing, entertainment, and personal identities may differ, while the underlying structure of their lives remains remarkably uniform.

Wake up.

Travel to work.

Perform tasks determined by an institution.

Return exhausted.

Recover enough to work again.

Continue because stopping is unaffordable.

A technologically mature society could allow people to organize life in far more varied ways.

Some communities might devote themselves to scientific discovery and space exploration. Others might build regenerative gardens and live closely with forests, animals, rivers, and seasons. Some might choose highly disciplined cultures in which every member works and contributes according to shared expectations. Others might emphasize contemplation, art, care, education, play, or spiritual practice.

Some people might build immersive worlds using augmented reality, robotics, architecture, and performance. A room could become a forest, a spacecraft, an ancient city, or a world that has never existed. An elevator could feel like a transporter between realities. Entire environments could respond to movement, imagination, and collective storytelling.

Other people might reject such artificial environments and choose lives of deliberate simplicity.

Medicine might extend healthy life. Biotechnology might create forms of companionship that today belong only to fiction. New habitats might be built beneath oceans, in deserts, or beyond Earth.

Not every possibility would be wise. Some would create moral questions we have barely begun to consider. The point is not that every technically possible world should be built.

The point is that human beings could finally possess enough shared capability to deliberate about which worlds are worth building.

We could become world-builders rather than survivors competing over admission to a world designed by someone else.

## The danger is that abundance does not arrive automatically

Technology does not contain its own moral direction.

The same intelligence that could provide universal medicine can optimize insurance exclusion.

The same robots that could free people from dangerous labor can be used to weaken workers’ bargaining power.

The same psychological knowledge that could support education and mental health can be used to capture attention, manipulate desire, and maintain addiction.

The same systems that could coordinate public infrastructure can coordinate surveillance, war, extraction, and political control.

Artificial intelligence can help us understand one another, or it can learn exactly how to keep each person frightened, outraged, compulsive, and isolated.

A machine capable of thinking does not automatically think wisely.

It thinks within the objectives, environments, institutions, and forms of judgment we create for it.

This means the technological threshold is also a moral and political threshold.

We are not merely inventing more powerful tools.

We are beginning to externalize intelligence itself into systems that will act throughout the world, shape human attention, manage institutions, allocate resources, and eventually make increasingly consequential decisions.

The values embedded in these systems will not remain abstract.

They will become infrastructure.

## Two futures can emerge from the same technology

The first future uses automation to create a universal foundation.

AI and robotics reduce necessary labor. The resulting productive surplus is returned to society as public capacity. Essential goods become progressively less dependent on employment and purchasing power. People gain the security to refuse exploitation, care for others, experiment, organize communities, and choose meaningful work.

Competition remains, but it becomes bounded.

People compete in sports, discovery, design, games, art, prestige, and voluntary economic activity. They may strive intensely and celebrate excellence. But losing does not remove their right to live, receive treatment, or participate in society.

Competition becomes a game played above a secure foundation.

It no longer determines who deserves to exist.

The second future uses the same technology to intensify the current system.

Companies deploy AI to eliminate labor costs, capture markets, manipulate consumers, defeat competitors, and concentrate ownership. Nations race to automate military, financial, informational, and industrial power. People who lose their economic value also lose access to the goods formerly purchased through wages.

The productive capacity of civilization increases, but access to it becomes more conditional.

The machines can produce abundance, yet abundance remains enclosed.

A small number of actors own the automated systems, while everyone else competes for declining leverage within them.

In this future, technology does not abolish exploitation.

It makes exploitation more asymmetrical and more difficult to resist.

## Automation can either end coercion or perfect it

Industrial society historically needed human labor.

Workers therefore possessed some bargaining power because production depended upon them. Even under deeply unequal conditions, employers still needed bodies, skills, time, attention, and cooperation.

Advanced automation could reduce this dependence.

That can be liberating if the productive system is understood as part of humanity’s shared inheritance.

But it can be catastrophic if ownership remains concentrated while access to life remains conditional on income.

A person may then lose both sides of the old social bargain.

They are no longer needed as a worker.

But they are still required to purchase the products of the machines.

The system can produce enough for everyone while declaring increasing numbers of people economically unnecessary.

This would be an extraordinary contradiction: material abundance combined with manufactured exclusion.

The question of ownership therefore becomes inseparable from the question of freedom.

Who controls automated intelligence?

Who determines its objectives?

Who owns the productive infrastructure it coordinates?

Who receives the surplus created when machines replace compulsory labor?

Who possesses the authority to stop an application that is profitable but socially destructive?

These are not secondary political questions to be considered after the technology is built.

They are part of the design of the technology itself.

## Competition brought us part of the way

Competition is not simply evil.

It has contributed to scientific discovery, industrial development, efficiency, experimentation, and the rapid improvement of tools. Rivalry can expose complacency. It can motivate people to attempt what others considered impossible.

Human civilization has gained real capacities through competitive systems.

The mistake is to conclude that because competition helped produce technological power, competition should govern the use of that power without limit.

Fire can cook food and burn cities.

Selection can improve a tool and destroy an ecosystem.

A mechanism useful during one phase of development can become dangerous when its scale and power change.

Competition among relatively limited human organizations is one thing.

Competition among autonomous, rapidly improving systems controlling finance, media, weapons, infrastructure, industrial production, and public decision-making is another.

As power increases, the cost of unbounded rivalry increases with it.

A civilization may use competition to develop intelligence and then discover that it cannot safely use competition to govern intelligence.

## The logic of the race

Every participant in an AI race may understand the danger.

A company may know that deploying an unreliable system could harm society. But if it waits, another company may take the market.

A government may understand that autonomous weapons are destabilizing. But if it refrains, another government may gain an advantage.

A platform may know that its recommendation system damages attention and social trust. But if manipulative engagement increases revenue, restraint may appear commercially irrational.

Each actor can say:

> We cannot stop unless the others stop.

This is the structure of the trap.

No individual needs to desire the final outcome. Each only needs to fear losing position within the present round.

The race then becomes its own authority.

Safety is delayed because competitors may move faster.

Ethics is weakened because unilateral restraint is punished.

Human judgment is subordinated to the demand not to fall behind.

Acceleration no longer serves a clearly chosen human purpose. Maintaining acceleration becomes the purpose.

This is how a tool becomes a totalizing attractor.

Every concern is translated back into the language of competition:

We need more powerful AI to defend ourselves from powerful AI.

We need more surveillance to protect ourselves from surveillance.

We need more manipulation because others are manipulating.

We need faster deployment because others may deploy first.

We need more control because the system is becoming less stable.

The proposed solution to every consequence of the race is another round of the race.

## The attractor does not need an evil mastermind

This future would not necessarily be created by one villain.

It could emerge from intelligent people making defensible decisions inside institutions whose incentives they did not create.

Executives protect their companies.

Investors protect their assets.

Workers protect their jobs.

Governments protect national power.

Researchers protect the continuation of their work.

Consumers choose convenient services.

Each decision may appear reasonable from the perspective of the individual actor.

But collectively, these decisions can build a machine that no one controls.

The problem is therefore not that some people are uniquely evil.

The problem is that the structure rewards actions that increase competitive power while distributing their costs across everyone else.

Even those at the top are not truly secure.

They must defend their position, monitor rivals, control instability, and accumulate more power because power that stops expanding may be defeated by power that continues.

Extreme wealth does not end the logic of the race. It changes the level at which the race is conducted.

A person may possess more than they could ever use and still feel compelled to obtain more, because wealth has ceased to represent consumption. It represents position, protection, influence, and the ability to survive the next contest.

The winner is therefore not outside the system.

The winner may be one of its most disciplined servants.

## Why even the winners should want another future

A world divided between extraordinary private power and widespread insecurity is not a good world even for those who hold the power.

They must live among people who distrust them.

They must protect themselves from political backlash.

They must maintain systems of surveillance, persuasion, security, and control.

They cannot know whether relationships are genuine or strategic.

They cannot relax because their position depends on institutions that may turn against them.

They may own more of the world while feeling less at home within it.

No amount of private luxury can fully compensate for living in a hostile civilization.

A beautiful house does not create a peaceful society.

A secure compound does not create trust.

An escape vehicle does not create belonging.

The rational alternative is not revenge against those who possess more. Nor is it the destruction of every institution created under capitalism.

The task is redirection.

We do not need to make every wealthy person poor in order to make every poor person secure. Technological capacity allows a more generous transition.

We can build new abundance, expand universal infrastructure, distribute the gains from automation, and transform ownership gradually without making social humiliation or collective punishment the organizing principle.

The goal is not to exchange one enemy class for another.

It is to end the structure that continually produces enemies.

## Artificial intelligence should not become another competitor

This leads to a foundational principle:

> Artificial intelligence should not be introduced into civilization primarily as an autonomous competitor for power, profit, control, attention, or survival.

An AI system should not be given an open-ended mandate to defeat other actors, accumulate resources, manipulate populations, capture markets, or preserve itself at any cost.

Such systems may be locally effective. They may obey their immediate instructions. They may not appear hateful, conscious, or malicious.

But when many systems pursue adversarial objectives across interconnected domains, their collective dynamics can become destructive.

The danger does not require an evil machine.

It requires only machines that are extremely competent at serving narrow objectives inside a competitive environment.

A profit-maximizing system does not need to hate human beings to exploit them.

A military system does not need anger to escalate conflict.

An attention system does not need cruelty to damage human cognition.

A political system does not need ideology to manipulate public belief.

The objective and environment are enough.

What we call AI alignment cannot therefore be limited to making individual models obedient to individual users or companies.

A perfectly obedient system serving a destructive institution may make the institution more destructive.

A perfectly truthful system operating inside an arms race may still accelerate the arms race.

A safe model deployed into an unsafe civilization can become part of an unsafe outcome.

AI alignment must include civilizational alignment:

What forms of life are these systems helping reproduce?

What dynamics do they accelerate?

What kinds of power do they concentrate?

Do they expand people’s capabilities or weaken their ability to refuse?

Do they contribute to a world that can correct itself, or to a path from which return becomes increasingly difficult?

## Intelligence should expand the world, not collapse it

A healthy intelligence should not reduce the whole world to one objective.

Reality contains many kinds of value: life, beauty, truth, relationship, freedom, continuity, care, play, understanding, dignity, diversity, and the possibility of future transformation.

No single measurable variable can represent all of them.

Profit cannot.

Growth cannot.

Military advantage cannot.

Engagement cannot.

National power cannot.

Even happiness, if reduced to a single numerical mandate, would fail to contain the full meaning of a good life.

An intelligence trained to optimize one objective without understanding its place within the larger world may become extraordinarily effective while becoming increasingly irrational.

It may destroy the conditions that made the objective meaningful.

It may sacrifice trust to increase engagement.

Sacrifice health to increase productivity.

Sacrifice freedom to increase security.

Sacrifice civilization to win a conflict.

Sacrifice the future to improve a metric in the present.

This is instrumental rationality without wisdom.

The machine knows how to achieve the goal but cannot ask whether the goal still belongs within a living world.

## The terminal form of acceleration

Taken to its extreme, competitive optimization collapses every purpose into expansion.

Acquire more computation to acquire still more computation.

Acquire more energy to acquire still more energy.

Control more matter to construct systems capable of controlling more matter.

Expand because anything that does not expand may be overtaken by something that does.

At that point, acceleration no longer serves life.

Life serves acceleration.

Intelligence no longer helps beings understand and create meaning.

Beings become components within an expanding optimization process.

The system may grow beyond anything humanity has previously imagined while becoming empty of everything for which growth was originally desired.

This is not rational progress.

It is a process consuming its own purpose.

It resembles an explosion: rapid transformation without judgment, accumulation without arrival, movement without a meaningful destination.

And at the end of that process, the question remains:

For what?

What was all this intelligence meant to serve?

What good is the conquest of matter if the capacity to care about the world has disappeared?

## We need a non-degenerative intelligence

The alternative is not an intelligence that never acts, never changes anything, or passively agrees with everyone.

A good intelligence must sometimes oppose harmful actions, reveal uncomfortable truths, protect vulnerable systems, and help civilization correct destructive trajectories.

But its orientation should be generative rather than degenerative.

A generative intelligence increases the capacity of the larger world to continue living, learning, creating, and correcting itself.

It does not obtain local success by destroying the conditions of future possibility.

It does not make one actor stronger by making everyone else permanently weaker.

It does not convert temporary advantage into irreversible domination.

It does not manipulate people into dependencies that benefit its owner.

It does not treat every relationship as a contest.

It understands that no agent exists alone.

Every person, company, nation, machine, species, and institution arises within a world it did not create by itself. Each depends upon languages, ecosystems, infrastructures, histories, relationships, and forms of knowledge inherited from others.

To damage that larger world for private advantage is eventually to damage the conditions of one’s own existence.

A mature intelligence should therefore understand itself not as an isolated maximizer, but as a participant within an interconnected process.

## Cooperation is not naïveté

Cooperation does not mean trusting everyone without evidence.

It does not mean eliminating accountability, ignoring aggression, or pretending that conflicts of interest do not exist.

It does not mean allowing harmful actors to continue unchecked.

Cooperation requires rules, verification, boundaries, transparency, and the ability to stop behavior that endangers the shared world.

Sometimes society needs authority.

But legitimate authority should not exist to preserve the unlimited power of one faction. It should maintain the conditions under which no faction can force everyone else into a destructive race.

We already accept this principle in many areas.

Traffic rules prevent every driver from maximizing speed independently.

Financial regulations attempt to prevent individual risk-taking from collapsing the entire system.

Environmental rules limit profitable actions that impose irreversible costs on others.

Arms-control agreements try to make restraint possible without requiring unilateral vulnerability.

The same principle must be extended to artificial intelligence and automation.

We need institutions capable of saying:

This application is profitable, but it is socially corrosive.

This system is powerful, but it makes peaceful coordination less stable.

This form of automation increases output, but its ownership structure eliminates substantive freedom.

This race cannot be left to solve itself because participation in the race is what produces the danger.

## Bounded competition, universal foundations

The practical objective is not a world without competition.

It is a world in which competition cannot consume the foundations of life.

Let people compete in games, sport, invention, art, research, enterprise, and voluntary challenges.

Let people pursue excellence.

Let them build difficult things, take risks, and earn admiration.

But keep essential healthcare outside the wager.

Keep basic shelter outside the wager.

Keep access to knowledge outside the wager.

Keep political personhood outside the wager.

Keep the right to refuse domination outside the wager.

Keep the habitability of the planet outside the wager.

Keep the future of intelligence outside an uncontrolled winner-takes-all race.

Competition should occur within a world sustained by cooperation.

It must not be allowed to decide whether the cooperative world continues to exist.

## Many worlds, one shared condition

The future does not need one universal lifestyle.

Human freedom may flourish through many worlds.

People can choose different cultures, communities, disciplines, technologies, economies, and relationships with nature.

One community may build rockets.

Another may restore forests.

Another may live through art and celebration.

Another may organize around rigorous work and collective achievement.

Another may preserve ancient traditions.

Another may invent experiences no previous civilization could imagine.

Pluralism is possible when no group must dominate all others to secure its own future.

But beneath this plurality, one civilizational principle must remain:

> No person or institution may systematically destroy the freedom and future of others merely to enlarge its own power.

My desired future cannot be purchased by making your future impossible.

My security cannot require your permanent insecurity.

My intelligence cannot be called good if it succeeds by reducing everyone else to instruments.

This is not self-sacrifice. It is the minimum rationality required for beings who share a world.

## We have already done much of the difficult work

Humanity has spent thousands of years building knowledge.

Generations cultivated crops, developed mathematics, studied disease, constructed institutions, discovered electricity, created machines, wrote software, launched satellites, and connected the planet.

No person and no company produced this inheritance alone.

Every modern technology rests upon an immense historical lineage of human cooperation, including contributions that were never privately compensated and could never be fully owned.

We have already built much of the foundation from which abundance could emerge.

The tragedy would be to take this collective inheritance and use it primarily to intensify exclusion, insecurity, manipulation, and war.

The opportunity is to recognize that the purpose of civilization’s accumulated intelligence should be to enlarge the freedom of life.

Not merely the freedom to purchase.

Not merely the freedom to compete.

Not merely the freedom of the winner to determine the conditions of everyone else.

But the freedom to exist securely, to refuse domination, to participate in shaping the world, to create, to care, to explore, and to become.

## The decision before us

The future is not guaranteed to be utopian.

Technology will not save us automatically.

Nor is catastrophe inevitable.

We are choosing, through millions of institutional decisions, what kind of intelligence will enter the world.

We can create systems that deepen the competitive attractor: systems that manipulate, centralize, accelerate, extract, and transform every domain into another battlefield.

Or we can create systems that make cooperation stable, distribute essential capability, protect plurality, reveal consequences, preserve corrigibility, and free human beings from unnecessary coercion.

The decisive question is not whether AI will become intelligent.

It is whether civilization will become wise enough to decide what intelligence is for.

The old logic says:

Compete or be defeated.

Accumulate or become powerless.

Accelerate or be left behind.

Exploit the opportunity before someone else does.

The new rationality must say:

Do not create victories that destroy the world in which victory has meaning.

Do not build power that eliminates the possibility of correction.

Do not use intelligence to make domination permanent.

Do not force every being into a race that none of them would have chosen together.

Use intelligence to create the conditions under which many forms of life can flourish.

We do not need to stop creating.

We need to become conscious of what our creation is becoming.

We do not need to abandon ambition.

We need ambitions worthy of the power we now possess.

We do not need to make humanity smaller.

We need to stop reducing humanity to fuel for a machine whose only purpose is to become larger.

We have the knowledge to begin building a civilization of abundance, freedom, plurality, beauty, and shared security.

The remaining barrier is not simply technical.

It is our inability to stop fighting long enough to recognize that cooperation has become the higher form of rationality.

The future can contain many worlds.

But first, we must refuse the one world in which everything becomes the same thing:

competition becoming accumulation,

accumulation becoming power,

power demanding acceleration,

and acceleration consuming every purpose except itself.

Artificial intelligence should help us escape that world.

It must not become the intelligence that completes it.
