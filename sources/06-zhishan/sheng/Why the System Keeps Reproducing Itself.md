# Why the System Keeps Reproducing Itself

> competition selects behaviors; winners gain rule-making power; the rules reproduce those behaviors; everyone else must adapt; adaptation is then mistaken for human nature.

The loss of substantive freedom is not simply the result of a few greedy people making bad decisions.

It is structural.

We live inside an environment that repeatedly selects certain kinds of behavior, rewards the people who become most effective at that behavior, and then gives those winners disproportionate power to define the rules, values, and institutions of society.

The central mechanism is competition.

Competition can be useful. Within limits, it can motivate effort, reveal better methods, encourage experimentation, and prevent complacency. The problem begins when competition stops being one social tool among many and becomes the organizing principle of almost every part of life.

People compete for employment, housing, education, healthcare, investment, status, visibility, security, and even the right to rest. Institutions compete for capital and attention. Governments compete for economic growth and geopolitical advantage. Media compete for engagement. Individuals compete to remain employable and socially visible.

Under these conditions, competition does more than determine who receives rewards.

It shapes what kind of person is able to survive.

## The system selects its own representatives

Imagine two organizations.

One tries to protect workers, preserve the environment, avoid manipulating customers, and maintain long-term social trust.

The other is willing to reduce wages, externalize environmental costs, influence regulators, capture attention, exploit psychological vulnerabilities, and sacrifice long-term stability for short-term growth.

If both organizations must compete under rules that reward profit, expansion, and market dominance above everything else, the second organization may gain an advantage.

It can grow faster, acquire more resources, eliminate or absorb competitors, influence public policy, and present its success as evidence that its methods were correct.

The system has not merely rewarded a winner.

It has selected which values will possess greater power in the next round.

The same process operates among individuals. Those most willing to treat relationships instrumentally, suppress moral hesitation, promote themselves aggressively, and prioritize personal advancement may gain advantages in environments that reward precisely those behaviors.

This does not mean every successful person is selfish or immoral. Many people succeed while remaining kind, responsible, and principled.

The structural point is different:

> When an environment consistently rewards a behavior, people who adopt that behavior gain more influence over the future environment.

The winners acquire wealth, status, authority, media access, institutional control, and the ability to decide what counts as competence, realism, merit, and success.

They then redesign the next round of competition.

This creates a feedback loop:

Competition selects the winners.

The winners influence the rules.

The new rules reward the traits that produced the winners.

Those traits become increasingly necessary for everyone else.

Eventually, what began as one strategy becomes the apparent nature of reality.

## A system can produce selfish behavior without selfish people

This is why blaming individuals is inadequate.

A person may not believe that life should revolve around money. But they still need to pay rent.

A company leader may not want to dismiss employees. But investors may punish the company if its returns fall below those of competitors.

A journalist may not want to produce outrage-driven content. But the media organization depends on attention.

A politician may understand the need for long-term cooperation. But elections, donors, public opinion, and geopolitical rivalry reward immediate visible victories.

People often participate in harmful behavior not because they consciously endorse it, but because they believe they have no survivable alternative.

They say:

> I do not like this, but this is how the world works.

> If I do not do it, someone else will.

> I have responsibilities.

> I must remain competitive.

> I need to protect my family.

> I cannot afford to be idealistic.

Each statement may be locally reasonable.

But when millions of people make the same locally reasonable decision, they collectively reproduce the environment that made the decision necessary.

No single person intends the final outcome.

Yet the outcome emerges from their interaction.

This is what makes the structure so difficult to escape. It does not require an evil ruler commanding everyone from above. It can operate through ordinary people responding rationally to the pressures immediately around them.

Everyone adapts to the game.

Through their adaptation, they preserve the game.

## The belief becomes true because people act upon it

A society may begin with the belief that human beings are fundamentally self-interested competitors.

Institutions are then designed around that assumption.

Workers are monitored because they are expected to avoid effort.

Companies are required to maximize returns because other purposes are considered unreliable.

Nations accumulate power because other nations cannot be trusted.

Citizens are treated primarily as consumers.

Education becomes preparation for labor-market competition.

Success becomes the ability to accumulate private advantage.

Once institutions are built around these assumptions, cooperative behavior becomes more difficult and competitive behavior becomes more rational.

People learn not to trust because the system punishes those who trust too freely.

They learn to prioritize themselves because shared protections have been weakened.

They accumulate private wealth because public security is unreliable.

They compete for scarce opportunities because access has been deliberately organized through competition.

The original theory of human nature begins producing the behavior it claimed merely to describe.

People then observe this behavior and say:

> You see? Human beings are naturally selfish.

But what they are observing is not human nature in isolation.

They are observing human beings adapting to a particular environment.

A conclusion about humanity has been built into social institutions. Those institutions shape behavior. The resulting behavior is then offered as proof of the original conclusion.

The belief becomes performative: by organizing society around it, we make it increasingly true.

## The environment enters the person

Structures do not remain outside us.

They become habits, desires, fears, identities, and standards of judgment.

A child learns early that some people are winners and others are losers. Grades rank students. Schools compete for prestige. Parents compare achievements. Careers are arranged into hierarchies. Wealth is treated as evidence of intelligence or virtue. Exhaustion becomes evidence of commitment. Visibility becomes evidence of importance.

People learn to view themselves from the perspective of the competition:

Am I ahead or behind?

Am I valuable enough?

Am I productive enough?

What is my market value?

How do I compare with others?

What advantage can I gain?

Even friendship, beauty, knowledge, creativity, and love can become forms of social capital.

The person does not merely participate in the market.

They begin to experience themselves as a marketable object.

They learn to optimize their body, personality, education, network, public image, and attention. They may eventually take pride in their ability to do so.

What was first imposed as a survival strategy becomes an identity:

> This is simply who I am.

> I am ambitious.

> I am a winner.

> I always put myself first.

> I know how the real world works.

The system no longer needs to discipline the person entirely from outside. The person begins disciplining themselves.

They monitor their own productivity.

They feel guilty when resting.

They judge themselves by income and status.

They evaluate others by the same standards.

They reproduce the system in their everyday perceptions.

## We teach the structure to one another

This process is also what we call education, socialization, mentorship, management, parenting, and culture.

Teaching is not only the transmission of explicit knowledge. It is the transmission of attention, values, categories, and ways of interpreting reality.

We teach people what to notice.

We teach them what to ignore.

We teach them what deserves admiration.

We teach them what should produce shame.

We tell young people to be realistic, by which we often mean that they should adapt themselves to existing systems of reward.

We present the most successful competitors as role models. Their wealth becomes proof of wisdom. Their visibility becomes proof of value. Their survival strategy becomes universal advice.

Those who sacrificed relationships, health, community, truth, or conscience to win may describe these sacrifices as discipline.

Others are then told to imitate them.

The winners do not only possess more resources. They acquire greater power to narrate the meaning of their victory.

They can say:

> I succeeded because I deserved it.

> The system rewards value.

> Anyone can do what I did.

> Those who failed lacked effort, intelligence, or courage.

The structural conditions that helped produce the victory disappear from the story. The outcome is moralized. Success becomes virtue, and poverty becomes evidence of personal deficiency.

Society therefore disciplines people not only through punishment, but through admiration.

It does not merely threaten them with failure.

It gives them an image of the person they should desire to become.

## Attention is also allocated by profit

The system shapes us not only by controlling resources, but by controlling what enters our attention.

Businesses compete to influence what we see, fear, desire, and remember. Platforms optimize engagement because engagement produces revenue. Advertising teaches dissatisfaction because satisfied people consume less. Public images repeatedly associate happiness with acquisition, status, beauty, exclusivity, and personal advancement.

The purpose is not necessarily to make people wise, free, or capable of living well.

The purpose is to capture and monetize attention.

Again, no individual must control the whole process. Each organization may simply be optimizing its own measurable outcome.

But the collective result is an environment filled with images that quietly shape the people who encounter them.

These images tell us:

You are incomplete.

You are falling behind.

Others have more.

Your life should be displayed.

Your identity should be purchased.

Your value should be measured.

You must become more competitive.

They float through the social environment like an invisible architecture. We do not always consciously believe them. But we interact with them, compare ourselves through them, and organize our lives in response to them.

The environment becomes part of the person.

The person then becomes part of the environment of everyone else.

## When the market becomes a moral authority

A market is a mechanism for exchanging and coordinating certain things.

It is not a complete theory of value.

A price can tell us what someone is willing and able to pay. It cannot, by itself, tell us whether something is good, beautiful, necessary, just, or worthy of preservation.

Profit can indicate that an activity successfully captures revenue. It cannot tell us whether that activity contributes to human flourishing.

Yet when market logic becomes totalizing, price begins to appear as the measure of value itself.

What produces profit is treated as productive.

What does not produce profit is treated as a cost.

Care, trust, ecological stability, community life, contemplation, public knowledge, and emotional support may become invisible because much of their value cannot be privately captured.

Meanwhile, activities that create addiction, insecurity, pollution, loneliness, or manipulation may appear highly valuable because their revenues are measurable.

The market has then ceased to function as a limited instrument.

It has become a moral authority.

People begin to assume that what wins deserves to win, that what grows deserves to grow, and that what is profitable must be socially valuable.

“Success” no longer means contributing to a good society.

It means surviving and expanding within the selection mechanism.

## The totalizing attractor

We can call this process a totalizing attractor.

It is an attractor because it repeatedly pulls behavior in a particular direction. People and institutions that resist its logic face disadvantages, while those that adopt it gain resources and influence.

It is totalizing because it tends to expand into domains that were previously organized by other values: education, medicine, art, friendship, research, politics, family life, and personal identity.

It does not need everyone to believe in it.

It only needs enough people to behave as though resistance is too costly.

Some sincerely believe that relentless competition is good.

Others dislike it but feel compelled to participate.

Others tell themselves that they will first win and later use their power for good.

But all of them may still contribute to the same dynamic.

This is why the attractor cannot be explained as the fault of one class, one generation, one political party, or one group of immoral people.

People at the bottom compete because they need to survive.

People in the middle compete because they fear falling.

People at the top compete because power must be defended against other power.

Everyone can feel trapped, even while some benefit far more than others.

The structure coordinates their fear.

## The pie can grow while freedom shrinks

It is sometimes said that competition enlarges the economic pie.

In some respects, this can be true. Competition can increase production, efficiency, technological capacity, and total wealth.

But total wealth is not the same as widely shared freedom.

The pie can grow while secure access to its essential parts becomes more concentrated.

A society can become richer while housing becomes less attainable.

Productivity can rise while workers become less secure.

Technology can improve while people lose control of their time.

More goods can be produced while public spaces, shared institutions, and social trust deteriorate.

The relevant question is not only whether the total pie is larger.

It is:

Who controls it?

Who can reliably access it?

Who bears the risks?

Who can refuse the terms on which it is offered?

How much of the social surplus becomes shared capability, and how much becomes concentrated power?

When gains accumulate primarily among those who already possess leverage, competition becomes increasingly unequal. Winners enter the next round with greater resources, information, influence, and protection from failure.

Others compete over what remains.

The formal game continues, but the starting positions diverge.

For more and more people, the portion of life available outside compulsory competition becomes smaller.

## Escaping the structure requires more than changing attitudes

We cannot solve this merely by telling people to be kinder.

People often already want to be kinder. But kindness that leads to bankruptcy, unemployment, or institutional defeat cannot become a stable social norm.

A viable alternative must change what is rewarded, what is protected, and what people can afford to refuse.

Cooperation must not depend entirely on personal sacrifice.

Care must not be punished.

Ethical organizations must not be systematically eliminated by less ethical competitors.

People must possess enough security that they can act according to conscience without risking destruction.

Markets must operate within social and ecological boundaries rather than defining those boundaries.

Essential capabilities must be protected from total dependence on purchasing power.

Institutions must measure consequences that profit does not capture.

Power must remain accountable, including private power.

Success must be redefined as contribution to the continued flourishing of the larger world that makes individual life possible.

The purpose is not to abolish all competition.

It is to prevent competition from becoming the environment in which every other value must either justify itself through profit or disappear.

## The central lesson

Human beings do not exist separately from their environments.

We create institutions, stories, incentives, images, and standards. These things then enter us. They shape our behavior, personality, attention, judgment, and sense of what is possible.

We pass them to others through teaching, imitation, management, media, and everyday interaction.

The structure produces people.

Those people reproduce the structure.

This is why the problem is both social and personal, yet cannot be reduced to personal blame.

We are not confronting a collection of evil individuals.

We are confronting a self-reinforcing environment that makes destructive behavior locally rational, selects those most adapted to it, and allows the selected winners to define reality for everyone else.

To change it, we must do more than condemn the winners or instruct the losers to try harder.

We must redesign the environment that produces both.

The question is not:

> Why are people so selfish?

The deeper question is:

> What kind of world continually teaches people that selfishness is necessary, rewards them for acting upon it, and then presents the resulting behavior as proof of human nature?

Once we can ask that question, another becomes possible:

> What kind of environment would make cooperation, care, freedom, and responsibility not heroic exceptions, but stable and reasonable ways to live?
