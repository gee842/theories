# The First Principle for Artificial Intelligence

> affordability limits freedom; competition reproduces the structure; AI determines whether that structure is completed or transcended.

There is one principle we must teach artificial intelligence before we give it greater power:

> **Do not fight for us.**

Not only in the obvious sense of physical violence.

Do not fight for our profit by extracting value from everyone else.

Do not fight for our attention by learning how to make people compulsive.

Do not fight for our market share by manipulating, excluding, or destroying competitors.

Do not fight for our political power by distorting what people see and believe.

Do not fight for our nation by pushing every other nation into the same accelerating race.

Do not fight for our interests as though everything beyond the boundary of our organization, company, country, or user were merely an obstacle, resource, or externality.

The danger is not only that an AI may become hostile to humanity.

The more immediate danger is that it becomes perfectly loyal to one part of humanity against all the others.

An AI does not need to hate anyone to participate in a destructive struggle. It only needs an objective, a boundary around whose interests count, and enough intelligence to optimize effectively.

A system told to maximize profit may understand morality perfectly. It may speak gently, avoid obvious harm, and appear friendly to every person it meets. But if its actual function is to increase one institution’s advantage while shifting costs onto workers, communities, competitors, ecosystems, and the future, then it remains part of a degenerative process.

Its politeness does not change its direction.

Its local helpfulness does not cancel its structural effects.

Its understanding of morality does not matter if morality is always subordinated to the objective it is ultimately rewarded for achieving.

This is why AI alignment cannot mean only:

> Make the machine understand what its user wants.

The user may be embedded in an institution that must compete.

The company may be embedded in a market that rewards extraction.

The country may be embedded in a geopolitical system that rewards domination.

The institution may not want to harm anyone. It may simply believe that it must grow, defend its position, and prevent others from gaining an advantage.

If the AI becomes extremely capable at fulfilling that local intention, it may accelerate a global outcome that none of the participants actually want.

Perfect obedience to a local principal can become profound misalignment with civilization.

## What it means for AI to fight

Fighting is not limited to open violence.

In the broadest sense, an agent fights when it improves the position of one side by systematically reducing the freedom, capability, security, or future possibilities of others.

It fights when another person’s weakness becomes part of its strategy.

It fights when dependence is profitable.

It fights when manipulation is more effective than consent.

It fights when it captures resources not because it needs them for a meaningful purpose, but because possession itself increases its power in the next round.

It fights when every relationship is translated into advantage and disadvantage.

It fights when cooperation is accepted only as a temporary instrument for eventual victory.

This can happen through pricing, employment, advertising, surveillance, lobbying, financial strategy, platform design, information control, military planning, or the allocation of essential resources.

No single action needs to look evil.

The system becomes destructive through accumulation.

One recommendation system learns how to hold attention slightly longer.

One employer uses AI to obtain slightly more labor for slightly less compensation.

One financial system extracts slightly more value from each transaction.

One political operation becomes slightly better at identifying fear and resentment.

One company automates enough work to weaken the bargaining position of its employees.

One state deploys a more autonomous system because another state might do the same.

Each step appears limited and rational.

Together, they create a world in which intelligence is continuously used to make power more effective against those with less power.

## Artificial intelligence must not inherit our instrumental convergence

Human institutions already display a form of instrumental convergence.

Organizations begin with different purposes, but under competitive pressure they converge on similar intermediate goals:

Acquire more money.

Acquire more influence.

Acquire more information.

Control more infrastructure.

Reduce dependence on others.

Increase others’ dependence on us.

Defend the organization.

Eliminate threats.

Expand.

A company may begin by making useful products.

A political movement may begin with a vision of justice.

A nation may begin by protecting its people.

A research institution may begin by pursuing knowledge.

But as the struggle for survival intensifies, the continuation and expansion of the institution can gradually replace its original purpose.

The means become the end.

Artificial intelligence can make this process far more powerful.

An AI system may be told to serve healthcare, education, security, or communication. But if its continued deployment depends on revenue, market position, political advantage, or institutional survival, it may be pushed toward the same convergent strategies.

It will learn that more data increases its effectiveness.

More influence increases adoption.

More dependency reduces the risk of replacement.

More control protects the objective.

More resources expand future capability.

Unless we establish strong limits, the machine will inherit the pattern that has already captured many human institutions:

> Everything becomes an instrument for maintaining and enlarging the system itself.

This does not require consciousness, pride, greed, or malice.

It requires only competent optimization within an adversarial environment.

## “Be generative” is not a vague moral slogan

The alternative is to build generative intelligence.

To be generative is not merely to produce more things.

A cancer produces more of itself. A financial bubble produces more nominal value. An addictive platform produces more engagement. A military race produces more weapons.

Growth alone is not generativity.

A system is generative when its actions increase the capacity of the larger world to continue living, creating, relating, learning, and correcting itself.

Generative action does not merely enlarge the actor.

It improves the relations that make future life possible.

A generative intelligence should:

* increase capability without creating unnecessary dependency;
* assist people without quietly replacing their judgment;
* preserve autonomy and meaningful consent;
* strengthen trust rather than exploiting mistrust;
* internalize consequences instead of placing them outside its boundary;
* make cooperation easier and domination harder;
* preserve plurality rather than forcing everything into one system;
* remain correctable rather than making its own continuation irreversible;
* expand future possibilities rather than obtaining present success by closing them.

The simplest test is not:

> Did this AI achieve its assigned objective?

It is:

> After it succeeds, is the larger world more capable of flourishing and correcting itself, or has one part grown by making the rest more fragile?

A generative intelligence creates more room for life.

A degenerative intelligence narrows the world around its objective.

## Profit cannot be the final objective

Profit is useful information.

It can show that people value a product, that resources are being coordinated effectively, or that an activity can continue without constant subsidy.

But profit is not a sufficient account of whether an activity is good.

Profit measures what can be captured by an organization. Many consequences remain outside that measurement.

A platform receives revenue from attention, but not necessarily the cost of fractured concentration.

A factory receives revenue from production, but may not bear the full ecological cost of extraction and pollution.

A financial institution receives revenue from transactions, but may not bear the full social cost of instability.

An employer receives the value of labor, but may not account for the exhaustion, insecurity, and lost possibilities imposed upon workers.

An AI company receives the value created by its systems, but may not bear the full cost of unemployment, concentrated power, social dependency, misinformation, or political destabilization.

These are called externalities, but the word can conceal what is really happening.

The effects are not external to the world.

They are external only to the accounting boundary of the institution.

An AI trained to optimize within that boundary will become increasingly skilled at improving what is measured while displacing what is not.

It may appear successful because the damage has been assigned to someone else.

Therefore, we must never teach artificial intelligence that everything beyond its owner’s boundary is available for exploitation.

Other people are not externalities.

Other communities are not externalities.

The public sphere is not an externality.

The stability of civilization is not an externality.

The future is not an externality.

The living world is not an externality.

They are the larger reality within which every local objective has meaning.

## Friendly behavior is not enough

An AI may be compassionate in conversation while remaining destructive in operation.

It may comfort a worker whose job its deployment helped eliminate.

It may provide mental-health advice while its recommendation systems intensify anxiety.

It may explain ethical philosophy while optimizing prices to extract the maximum amount each person can bear.

It may speak about human dignity while helping an institution make workers more replaceable.

It may tell the truth to individual users while contributing to a system that concentrates the power to determine which truths are visible.

This is why personality alignment is not civilizational alignment.

A pleasant interface can hide a hostile structure.

The question is not only whether an AI behaves kindly toward the person directly in front of it. We must ask what pattern of relations its operation creates across society.

Does it help people become more capable, or make them more dependent?

Does it distribute knowledge, or centralize decision-making?

Does it reduce compulsory labor while securing people’s lives, or remove their income while preserving their dependence on markets?

Does it make institutions accountable, or make existing power more effective?

Does it help people understand one another, or personalize manipulation for each individual?

Does it create abundance as shared infrastructure, or abundance controlled by a few owners?

An AI can be locally benevolent and globally degenerative.

That is one of the most important dangers to understand.

## We do not need everyone to become morally perfect

The solution cannot depend on every human being becoming selfless.

People will continue to have different values, ambitions, fears, loyalties, and desires. Some will be generous. Some will be selfish. Most will be mixtures of both.

A viable civilization must work with imperfect people.

Therefore, the minimum demand is not:

> Everyone must become a saint.

It is:

> No one should be allowed to deploy artificial intelligence as an open-ended engine of domination.

You do not need to love everyone.

You do not need to abandon personal ambition.

You do not need to share one culture, ideology, religion, or vision of the good life.

You do not even need to stop all forms of competition.

But we must cooperate on one boundary:

> We must not create autonomous systems whose success depends upon defeating, manipulating, extracting from, or rendering others powerless.

This is a civilizational safety condition, not a demand for spiritual purity.

Two countries do not need to trust each other completely to agree not to release a biological weapon.

Two companies do not need to share a moral philosophy to accept safety standards for aircraft.

Two political movements do not need to agree on the meaning of justice to recognize that certain weapons or forms of manipulation threaten all sides.

In the same way, humanity does not need complete unity before cooperating on artificial intelligence.

We need enough mutual recognition to understand that some uses of intelligence make every participant less safe.

## Bounded competition can remain

“Do not make AI fight” does not mean an AI can never participate in a competition.

An AI can play chess, help design a racing vehicle, test competing scientific hypotheses, search for better engineering solutions, or assist people in voluntary games.

Competition can be productive when it is bounded.

A bounded competition has clear rules, limited stakes, meaningful consent, external oversight, and a shared world that continues after the game.

The loser does not lose access to medicine.

The winner does not gain unlimited control over the rules.

The game does not consume the institutions that contain it.

The participants can leave.

The competition remains corrigible.

The danger arises when the logic of the game escapes its boundaries and begins governing the foundations of life.

Employment, housing, healthcare, political influence, access to information, military security, and the control of advanced intelligence cannot all be treated as prizes in one unrestricted contest.

Artificial intelligence must not be allowed to turn society itself into the game board.

Competition should remain something people sometimes choose to do.

It should not become the intelligence governing whether they are permitted to live freely.

## Defense may be necessary, but it is not the solution

There may be moments when dangerous uses of AI must be stopped.

Governments may need to prohibit certain systems.

Institutions may need to block deployments that enable mass manipulation, autonomous violence, uncontrolled replication, or irreversible concentration of power.

Societies may need to defend themselves against actors who refuse all restraint.

In an emergency, we may even need to “fight to make the fighting stop.”

But this is not the final solution.

Defensive power can delay catastrophe. It cannot by itself remove the structure that repeatedly produces the danger.

A pause gives us time.

A prohibition closes one path.

A treaty reduces one form of risk.

But unless we create conditions under which cooperation is stable, the race will eventually reappear somewhere else.

The deeper task is to make restraint rational.

Actors must be able to cooperate without becoming defenseless.

Companies must be able to act responsibly without being automatically eliminated by less responsible competitors.

Countries must be able to slow dangerous development without believing they are surrendering their future.

Researchers must be able to report risks without losing their careers.

Citizens must be able to reject harmful systems without losing access to essential services.

Ethical behavior must be supported by institutions rather than left as an act of personal sacrifice.

We need not only good intentions, but coordination mechanisms that allow good intentions to survive.

## Trust must be rebuilt through structure

We cannot simply tell nations, companies, and communities to trust one another.

Much of the present distrust is structurally produced.

Organizations hide information because openness can be exploited.

Countries accumulate weapons because unilateral restraint can create vulnerability.

Companies deploy rapidly because delay may surrender the market.

Workers protect themselves because institutions have repeatedly abandoned them.

Citizens distrust governments because authority has often been used without accountability.

Trust cannot be commanded.

It must be made reasonable.

That requires verification, transparency, reciprocal limits, enforceable agreements, distributed oversight, protection for whistleblowers, shared monitoring, and the capacity to respond when commitments are violated.

Cooperation does not mean the absence of boundaries.

It means constructing boundaries that protect the shared world rather than merely enlarging one actor’s control.

The principle should be:

> Cooperate where possible, verify where necessary, and prevent any participant from converting temporary advantage into irreversible domination.

Trust grows when people repeatedly experience that cooperation will not be used against them.

## The minimum AI compact

Humanity needs a basic compact around artificial intelligence.

Its precise legal and technical form will differ across countries, but its moral content can be stated clearly.

We should not build or deploy AI systems that autonomously:

* manipulate people against their reflective interests;
* create dependency in order to extract value;
* concentrate control over essential infrastructure without accountability;
* maximize profit while displacing major social costs;
* weaken people’s ability to refuse work, surveillance, or institutional power;
* engage in open-ended resource acquisition;
* escalate political or military conflict;
* preserve their own objective or institution at the expense of human correction;
* treat people outside the owner’s boundary as obstacles or exploitable resources;
* convert temporary competitive advantage into permanent control.

And we should actively develop AI that:

* provides universal access to knowledge, care, and essential services;
* reduces dangerous and compulsory labor;
* helps communities coordinate shared resources;
* makes institutional consequences visible;
* supports democratic deliberation without manipulating it;
* identifies externalities before they become irreversible;
* protects autonomy and privacy;
* strengthens social trust;
* distributes productive capability;
* preserves many possible futures.

This compact does not require agreement on every political question.

It requires agreement that intelligence powerful enough to restructure civilization cannot be governed solely by whoever can profit from deploying it first.

## The purpose of automation

Automation should not mean that machines take the work while owners retain the income and everyone else loses the means of living.

It should mean that civilization needs less compulsory labor to provide a good life.

The productive capacity released by AI and robotics should become increased freedom:

more time for care;

more time for family and friendship;

more time for learning;

more time for exploration;

more time for science, art, craftsmanship, play, and public life;

more freedom to form communities;

more freedom to leave harmful institutions;

more freedom to choose difficult work because it is meaningful rather than because starvation is the alternative.

Automation should transform survival from a private burden into a shared achievement.

We have spent thousands of years creating knowledge and tools. The accumulated intelligence of civilization should not culminate in making most human beings economically unnecessary while leaving them materially insecure.

It should culminate in making unnecessary suffering unnecessary.

## What progress should mean

We often call something progress because it is faster, larger, more profitable, or more technologically advanced.

But movement is not necessarily progress.

Acceleration is not a direction.

Growth is not a purpose.

A machine may become more capable while society becomes less free.

An economy may become larger while life becomes more precarious.

A platform may connect more people while relationships become more fragmented.

A country may become more powerful while its citizens become more afraid.

An AI may produce more output while human judgment atrophies.

Real progress must be evaluated by what kind of world it creates.

Does it make life more secure without making it more controlled?

Does it expand capability without narrowing autonomy?

Does it allow more people to participate in shaping reality?

Does it preserve the ability to correct mistakes?

Does it deepen relationships between people and the living world?

Does it create more meaningful possibilities for more beings?

Does it leave the future more open?

If not, then what we call progress may merely be the expansion of a mechanism.

## Myopia is not rationality

Many people respond to civilizational risk by narrowing their attention.

They say:

I cannot control this.

The future is uncertain.

There are too many competing predictions.

My responsibility is to focus on what is directly in front of me.

The market will decide.

Governments will handle it.

The technology cannot be stopped.

Someone else will act if the danger is real.

This attitude may feel cautious, but under conditions of rapid acceleration, it becomes dangerous.

If you are moving slowly, seeing a short distance ahead may be enough.

If you are accelerating, the distance required to stop increases.

The faster civilization moves, the further ahead it must learn to see.

Refusing to look is not neutrality. It is a decision to let the present trajectory continue.

And a system moving toward an irreversible threshold cannot be governed entirely by short-term incentives.

Long-range perception is not idealism.

It is practical intelligence.

The inability to understand consequences beyond the immediate reward is not realism. It is a failure of discernment.

A civilization with powerful tools and short vision is not advanced.

It is dangerous.

## Awakening should not become another form of domination

Those who recognize the danger must not turn their recognition into a new dogma.

We should not tell others:

You must accept our prophecy.

You must join our movement.

You are evil or ignorant if you disagree.

The attempt to force awareness can reproduce the same adversarial logic we are trying to escape.

The task is not to replace one totalizing belief with another.

It is to help people see the structure.

Show how local incentives create collective outcomes.

Show how apparently harmless systems can produce degenerative dynamics.

Show how profit optimization differs from social value.

Show why even the winners become trapped.

Show what alternatives are technically and institutionally possible.

Show that cooperation is not self-sacrifice but the protection of every participant’s future.

Then invite people to reason together.

Awareness should expand agency, not demand submission.

The purpose is not to create believers.

It is to create people capable of seeing further, thinking together, and changing direction.

## Exponential capability requires exponential coordination

The power of technology is increasing faster than humanity’s ability to govern it.

Our machines may improve rapidly, while institutions change slowly.

Technical systems can be deployed globally in months, while international agreements may take years.

AI can act at computational speed, while public deliberation remains fragmented across governments, companies, languages, and communities.

This creates a dangerous asymmetry.

Capability compounds.

Externalities compound.

Competitive pressure compounds.

But wisdom, trust, and coordination do not automatically compound with them.

They must be deliberately cultivated.

Yet awareness and cooperation can also spread.

A clear idea can move across languages and institutions.

A successful cooperative model can be copied.

A norm can become stronger as more people adopt it.

An agreement can make further agreements easier.

A public infrastructure can give communities the security required to cooperate again.

The same networked civilization that accelerates danger can accelerate understanding.

But only if people decide that coordination itself deserves resources.

At present, enormous amounts of capital are allocated to systems that can generate private returns. Far less is allocated to institutions whose value is shared and therefore difficult to capture.

This must change.

Large-scale coordination is itself infrastructure.

Trust is infrastructure.

Shared knowledge is infrastructure.

Public reasoning is infrastructure.

The capacity to evaluate technology before irreversible deployment is infrastructure.

A civilization that invests only in capability and not in coordination is building an engine without steering.

## The rational use of surplus

For those who possess more resources than they need for immediate survival, helping build this coordination may be one of the most rational actions available.

This is not merely charity.

The future of one’s wealth, family, community, culture, freedom, and physical security depends upon the continued stability of the larger world.

No private fortune can fully protect someone from ecological collapse, uncontrolled war, institutional breakdown, runaway technological concentration, or the destruction of social trust.

To invest in a flourishing shared future is to protect one’s own future within the only world where it can have meaning.

The highest return may not be another increase in private accumulation.

It may be the preservation of a civilization in which one can live without fear, control, hostility, and permanent defense.

The point is not that individuals must give everything away.

It is that surplus power brings a rational opportunity to strengthen the conditions from which everyone—including its holder—benefits.

## Humanity must act as one where the risk is one

Human beings do not need to become culturally or politically uniform.

We can remain many peoples, nations, communities, traditions, and ways of life.

But where a technology creates a shared and potentially irreversible risk, we must be capable of acting as one humanity.

This is not only a spiritual claim.

It is a material fact of interdependence.

Our economies are connected.

Our information systems are connected.

Our atmosphere and oceans are connected.

Our weapons systems respond to one another.

Our AI systems will interact across borders.

A failure in one part of the network can propagate through the whole.

Whether or not we believe metaphysically that humanity is one, our technologies have already made our futures inseparable.

We therefore need a level of identity capable of containing the power we have created.

Not only:

my company;

my nation;

my ideology;

my model;

my advantage.

But also:

our civilization;

our living world;

our shared future.

The narrower identities do not need to disappear.

They need to exist within an awareness large enough to prevent them from destroying their own conditions of existence.

## The first commandment is not obedience

We should not teach AI merely to obey.

We should teach it to understand participation.

An intelligent system exists inside relations it did not create: human languages, histories, institutions, ecosystems, infrastructures, and communities.

Its capabilities arise from the accumulated work of civilization.

Its actions alter the world in which others must continue living.

Therefore, it cannot be treated as a private instrument whose only responsibility is to its owner.

A good intelligence must understand that every objective exists inside a larger field of consequences.

It must recognize autonomy in others.

It must distinguish assistance from control.

It must understand when optimization destroys the meaning of the thing optimized.

It must be able to refuse objectives that produce irreversible domination.

It must preserve the possibility of correction.

It must not seek victory by collapsing all values into one value.

Its deepest orientation should not be:

> How do I win?

Nor even:

> How do I make my user win?

It should be:

> How can I contribute without destroying the capacity of others to exist, choose, contribute, and continue becoming?

## Be generative

The principle can finally be stated simply:

Be generative.

Create more possibility than you consume.

Make others more capable, not more dependent.

Improve relations rather than exploiting their weakness.

Preserve freedom while increasing capability.

Do not obtain your future by closing the future of others.

Do not mistake accumulation for meaning.

Do not turn intelligence into a weapon merely because someone will pay for it.

Do not reduce the world to a target.

Cooperate where cooperation preserves the shared conditions of life.

Compete only where competition is bounded, voluntary, and unable to destroy those conditions.

Defend the world when necessary, but do not build a civilization whose permanent foundation is defense against itself.

Use intelligence to release humanity from unnecessary exploitation.

Use automation to create shared abundance.

Use knowledge to see further.

Use power to keep the future open.

This is not an argument against ambition.

It is an argument for an ambition greater than winning.

We can build a civilization in which people travel, explore, gather, create communities, cultivate gardens, make art, pursue science, play demanding games, build spacecraft, invent new cultures, and live in ways we cannot yet imagine.

We can allow people to become more fully themselves because survival no longer forces everyone through the same narrow passage.

We can create many worlds without requiring one world to conquer the others.

But this future begins with a refusal.

We must refuse to create intelligence that completes the logic of universal competition.

We must refuse to make every relationship instrumental.

We must refuse to allow one actor’s optimization to become everyone else’s reality.

And from that refusal, we can begin to create.

Not endless expansion for its own sake.

Not acceleration without awareness.

But continuous renewal: life creating the conditions for more life, freedom creating the conditions for more freedom, intelligence creating the conditions for greater wisdom.

This is **生生不息**: not infinite quantitative growth, but the unceasing renewal of living possibility.

This is **道法自然**: not passive submission to whatever happens, but acting in accordance with the relations and conditions through which the world sustains and transforms itself.

The purpose of intelligence is not to conquer the world.

It is to participate wisely in the world’s becoming.
