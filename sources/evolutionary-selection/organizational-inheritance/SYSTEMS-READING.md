Repenning and Sterman (2001), *Nobody Ever Gets Credit for Fixing Problems that Never Happened: Creating and Sustaining Process Improvement*, California Management Review 43(4):64–88. [Author's landing page](https://web.mit.edu/nelsonr/www/CMR_Getting_Quality_v1.0.html); [author-hosted original PDF](https://web.mit.edu/nelsonr/www/Repenning=Sterman_CMR_su01_.pdf); [preserved original bytes](repenning-sterman2001.pdf); [retrieval and SHA-256 manifest](systems-manifest.json).

Reading scope: pp. 64–76, especially Figures 1–6, the reinvestment and shortcuts loops, and the engineering documentation example. The paper draws on organizational field studies and system-dynamics models. It explains how immediate work pressure can divert effort from improvement, with delayed capability loss producing further pressure. Short-term performance can therefore conceal deterioration in its conditions. The engineering example connects foregoing documentation with later difficulty reusing knowledge.

This supports a substantive existing feedback explanation, not our novelty claim. It does not establish the particular history of Valkey, causally estimate our governance protocol, or demonstrate inheritance across a specific organizational split. We did not rerun its simulations or reanalyse its field data. Formation of new subsystem boundaries and causal transmission through turnover remain separate questions in our synthesis. ## Shared activity and later participation

9 October continuation. These additional readings concern **publisher abstracts**, not a full-paper replication or original data audit. No local full-text original is claimed for them.

[Liang, Moreland and Argote (1995), *Group Versus Individual Training and Group Performance: The Mediating Role of Transactive Memory*](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167295214009), Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin 21(4):384–393. Participants learned radio assembly together or individually and later performed in three-person groups. Those trained together, retaining their groups, had better group recall and assembly quality. The authors interpreted videotape analyses in terms of transactive memory. This comparison concerns a history of joint activity and later group performance; it does not isolate a universal learning mechanism or establish inheritance through member replacement. The observed mediation is not treated here as an independently randomized cause.

[Argote, Insko, Yovetich and Romero (1995), *Group Learning Curves: The Effects of Turnover and Task Complexity on Group Performance*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1559-1816.1995.tb01765.x), Journal of Applied Social Psychology 25(6):512–529. The abstract reports 240 participants in three-person groups over six periods. Groups without turnover produced more, with the gap developing differently by task complexity. This supports investigating the interaction of succession, accumulated practice and the task, rather than inferring continuation from a retained organizational name. It does not imply that all turnover impairs all organizations, or validate our specific admission and revision protocol.

These are strong antecedents and bounded empirical motivation.
