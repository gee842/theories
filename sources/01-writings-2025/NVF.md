# Definition of Narrative-Value Frame (NVF)

A Narrative-Value Frame (NVF) is a structured framework through which an entity (individual, organization, or institution) interprets reality, constructs meaning, and justifies actions based on an embedded hierarchy of values. It defines what is valuable, which values and actions take precedence over others, and what strategies are acceptable or necessary to pursue those values—often reinforced through biology, stories, myths, institutional structures, and discourse power. These strategies can range from cooperation and altruism to manipulation, hoarding, or exploiting loopholes, depending on the NVF’s structure and reinforcement mechanisms.

NVFs exist at multiple levels:
- Individual NVFs shape personal perception, justification, and decision-making.
- Collective NVFs emerge when individual NVFs align, becoming dominant social, political, or economic paradigms.
- Dominant collective NVFs shape future individual NVFs through rituals, moral framing, education, socialization, institutional reinforcement, and other mechanisms.

When entities that embody a particular NVF gain power, wealth, or legitimacy, they can:
- Reshape institutions to align with their NVF, codifying it into laws, rules, and systemic incentives.
- Influence social norms, making their NVF seem "natural" or "common sense."
- Control discourse power, framing competing NVFs as irrational, impractical, or dangerous.

If power accumulation and the Narrative-Value Frame reinforce each other in a feedback loop, the dominant NVF becomes increasingly entrenched, making it harder to challenge—until a crisis, internal contradiction, or a new narrative disrupts the system.

## The Biological and Social Construction of NVFs

While NVFs shape our understanding of the world, they also define what we believe to be "human nature" itself. Many traits we assume to be innate—selfishness, endless accumulation, zero-sum competition, and hierarchical dominance—are not fixed aspects of our biology but products of historical, economic, and technological reinforcement. The very idea of an immutable "human nature" is itself part of an NVF, shaping what we consider natural and inevitable.

Humans are biologically wired for a range of behaviors, from competition and self-preservation to deep cooperation, empathy, and contentment once basic needs are met. Different NVFs elevate different aspects of our potential. For much of history, societies operated under more egalitarian NVFs, where cooperation and reciprocity were dominant. If history had unfolded differently, we might take peace, mutual care, and sufficiency as obvious, "natural" human traits—just as today, we take individualism and competition for granted.

Even deeply rooted biological drives, such as reproduction, can be reshaped by NVFs. Monastic traditions, voluntary celibacy, and philosophical commitments have shown that even the most fundamental instincts can be redirected. This suggests that no single NVF is inevitable. What we see as "natural" is, in many ways, a reflection of the narratives we have collectively accepted.


## NVF in Human Society

In human society, NVFs manifest as cultural, religious, or ideological systems that define what is valuable, how to justify actions, and what behavioral strategies are considered valid or effective in achieving those values. These systems not only dictate moral imperatives but also shape practical decision-making, influencing whether individuals and institutions prioritize collaboration, manipulation, competition, or exploitation as their primary mode of engagement with the world.

While the current dominant NVF has rewarded accumulation, competition, and power-seeking, alternative NVFs are possible. Societies have functioned under different value systems before, and humans have the capacity to adapt, reconstruct, and redefine what is valuable, right or wrong, good or evil. The challenge is whether we can collectively shift toward an NVF that aligns better with human well-being, sustainability, and cooperative potential—rather than one that perpetuates scarcity-driven competition and unchecked power dynamics.

## The Evolution and Mutation of NVFs

Just as genetic information mutates and evolves, so too do NVFs. Every act of interpretation is an act of transformation. When one individual perceives another’s words, actions, or worldview, they do so through the filter of their own NVF. What is received is often not an exact replica of what was intended but a version shaped by the interpreter’s prior beliefs, biases, and environmental context.

This means that NVFs are not static; they drift, mutate, and evolve over time. These changes can be organic—shaped by misunderstandings, cultural shifts, or environmental pressures—or intentional, through deception, manipulation, or deliberate propaganda. An NVF designed to serve a particular agenda can be propagated through media, institutions, or ideology, yet its survival depends on how well it aligns with external conditions and human cognition.

## Multiple levels of Narrative-Value Frame

NVFs exist at multiple levels:

### Individual NVFs guide personal perception, justification, and decision-making

---

#### **1. How NVFs Shape Perception (What We See & Pay Attention To)**
**Perception is not neutral**—people **filter reality through their NVF**, which determines:  
✅ **What they focus on** (salience).  
✅ **How they categorize information** (classification).  
✅ **What they consider "truth" or "valid"** (epistemic authority).  

🔹 **Example: Wealth Perception**  
- In a **Capitalist NVF**, someone perceives a billionaire as **a visionary leader** who worked hard for their success.  
- In a **Socialist NVF**, someone perceives a billionaire as **a product of systemic exploitation** who amassed wealth unfairly.  

💡 *Same external reality, but different NVFs filter it into different perceived truths.*  

🔹 **Example: Climate Change Perception**  
- In a **Technocratic NVF**, climate change is seen as **a solvable problem through innovation and market incentives**.  
- In an **Eco-Centric NVF**, climate change is seen as **a symptom of deep systemic failures requiring radical social change**.  

💡 *NVFs determine what is "seen" as the core issue and what solutions feel legitimate.*  

---

#### **2. How NVFs Shape Justification (How We Explain & Rationalize Choices)**
NVFs determine **which justifications are valid and socially acceptable** in a given situation.  

✅ **What counts as a "good reason" for action?**  
✅ **What moral or logical framework is applied?**  
✅ **What is dismissed as irrational or irrelevant?**  

🔹 **Example: Justifying Wealth Inequality**  
- In a **Meritocratic NVF**, extreme wealth is justified as **the result of individual effort and talent**.  
- In a **Collectivist NVF**, extreme wealth is unjustified and seen as **a failure of economic fairness**.  

🔹 **Example: Justifying War**  
- In a **Patriotic NVF**, war is justified as **defending national values and security**.  
- In a **Pacifist NVF**, war is never justified because **violence is always morally wrong**.  

💡 *The same action (e.g., becoming wealthy, going to war) can be framed as just or unjust depending on the NVF that guides reasoning.*  

---

#### **3. How NVFs Shape Decision-Making (What Actions We Take & Why)**
When making choices, an individual **uses their NVF as a reference point** for evaluating:  
✅ **What is an acceptable action.**  
✅ **What goals are worth pursuing.**  
✅ **What risks are justified.**  

🔹 **Example: Career Decision**  
- In a **Capitalist NVF**, an individual may prioritize **high-paying jobs** even if they dislike them.  
- In an **Artistic NVF**, an individual may prioritize **creative fulfillment** even at the cost of financial instability.  

🔹 **Example: Voting Decision**  
- In a **Libertarian NVF**, someone votes for policies that **reduce government intervention**.  
- In a **Social Welfare NVF**, someone votes for policies that **expand public services and redistribution**.  

💡 *NVFs function as a **decision-making compass**, determining what is "rational" or "right" in different contexts.*

### Collective NVFs emerge when individual NVFs align, becoming dominant social, political, or economic paradigms

---

For a **Collective NVF** to emerge, individual NVFs must align through **interaction, reinforcement, and coordination**. This happens in several key stages:

#### **(A) Initial Similarity & Small-Scale Convergence**
- Some individuals naturally have similar NVFs due to **shared background, experiences, or beliefs**.  
- **Social circles, professional networks, and ideological communities** reinforce alignment by exposing individuals to **similar narratives and justifications**.  
- **Language and symbols** help individuals recognize shared NVFs (e.g., political slogans, religious texts, memes).  

🔹 *Example: Early Capitalist NVF*  
- Early merchants and traders valued **market competition, wealth accumulation, and private ownership**.  
- As they interacted, they **reinforced** and **normalized** these values through trade networks, contracts, and financial institutions.  
- This alignment **laid the foundation for capitalism as a dominant economic NVF**.  

---

#### **(B) Coordination & Institutionalization**
- When aligned individuals gain power, they **begin shaping institutions, norms, and laws** to reflect their NVF.  
- **Organized movements, businesses, political parties, or cultural institutions** amplify and reinforce the NVF.  
- The NVF becomes **embedded in education, governance, and economics**, making it harder for competing NVFs to challenge.  

🔹 *Example: Formation of Democratic NVF*  
- Philosophers like Locke and Rousseau introduced **individual rights and representative governance** as core values.  
- Early democratic movements **coordinated protests, formed parties, and established legal systems** that reflected these values.  
- As more nations **institutionalized democracy**, it became **the dominant NVF for governance** in much of the world.  

---

#### **(C) Mass Adoption & Social Reinforcement**
- Once an NVF becomes **dominant**, it starts shaping **education, media, and cultural norms**, reinforcing itself across generations.  
- People born into the system **internalize the NVF as "common sense"**, reducing resistance.  
- **Social rewards (status, career success, approval) and punishments (marginalization, exclusion)** ensure continued alignment.  

🔹 *Example: Individualism in Western Culture*  
- Over time, the **individualist NVF** (self-reliance, personal ambition, private property) spread through economic and cultural forces.  
- Today, it’s **reinforced in schools, media, corporate policies, and even psychological frameworks**.  
- As a result, people **assume individualism is "natural"**, making alternative NVFs (e.g., collectivism) harder to sustain.

### Dominant collective NVFs shape future individual NVFs

#### **1. Media & Narrative Control**  
- **Propaganda & Mass Media** → Information is selectively framed to reinforce the dominant NVF (e.g., corporate media shaping economic narratives, state propaganda reinforcing national identity).  
- **Cultural Production** → Movies, TV, literature, music, and art **embed the NVF into everyday life** (e.g., "rags-to-riches" stories reinforcing capitalist NVFs, religious epics shaping moral NVFs).  
- **Algorithmic Curation** → Social media platforms amplify content that aligns with the dominant NVF, limiting exposure to competing NVFs.  

🔹 *Example:* The dominance of **consumerist NVFs** is reinforced through **advertising, celebrity culture, and social media influencers**, making material success seem natural and desirable.  

---

#### **2. Rituals & Traditions**  
- **Cultural Rituals** → Weddings, funerals, coming-of-age ceremonies, and national holidays reinforce NVF-based values.  
- **Workplace & Institutional Rituals** → Annual reviews, corporate awards, and academic rankings reinforce NVFs around productivity, competition, or hierarchy.  
- **Daily Habits & Routines** → Everyday behaviors (like tipping in service industries, shaking hands in business, or saying pledges in schools) reinforce **unquestioned NVF assumptions**.  

🔹 *Example:* A **meritocratic NVF** is reinforced through rituals like **graduation ceremonies**, which symbolize the belief that success is earned through individual effort rather than systemic advantage.  

---

#### **3. Law, Policy & Economic Structures**  
- **Legal Systems** → Laws encode NVFs into governance (e.g., property laws reflecting capitalist NVFs, religious laws shaping moral frameworks).  
- **Taxation & Incentives** → The way tax systems and economic rewards are structured makes **certain NVFs more viable than others**.  
- **Bureaucratic & Organizational Rules** → Corporate policies, government procedures, and professional licensing **determine what behaviors and beliefs are rewarded or punished**.  

🔹 *Example:* A **profit-maximization NVF** is embedded into economic systems through **corporate law requiring businesses to prioritize shareholder returns**, making alternative NVFs (like cooperative business models) less viable.  

---

#### **4. Punishment & Deterrence Mechanisms**  
- **Social Ostracization** → Individuals who reject the dominant NVF may be ridiculed, excluded, or seen as irrational.  
- **Criminalization of Deviance** → Laws and policies criminalize behaviors that threaten the dominant NVF (e.g., anti-union laws under capitalist NVFs, blasphemy laws under religious NVFs).  
- **Economic & Career Penalties** → Job opportunities, promotions, and funding tend to go to those who align with the NVF, discouraging deviation.  

🔹 *Example:* In a **hyper-individualist NVF**, those advocating for collectivist approaches (e.g., universal healthcare) might be labeled as "radicals" and excluded from mainstream policy discussions.  

---

#### **5. Psychological & Emotional Conditioning**  
- **Moral Framing** → NVFs influence what is seen as "good" or "evil" (e.g., wealth seen as virtuous in capitalist NVFs, self-denial seen as virtuous in religious NVFs).  
- **Guilt & Shame Mechanisms** → Individuals internalize NVF norms through emotional responses (e.g., feeling guilty for not working hard enough under a productivity-based NVF).  
- **Fear & Security Framing** → Dominant NVFs use existential fears to maintain control (e.g., "without capitalism, society will collapse" or "without traditional values, moral decay will spread").  

🔹 *Example:* **Religious NVFs** often use guilt and sin narratives to **internalize obedience**, ensuring individuals self-regulate rather than needing external enforcement.  

---

#### **6. Information Control & Selective Exposure**  
- **Historical Revisionism** → History is rewritten to **justify** and **legitimize** the dominant NVF while erasing or distorting counter-NVFs.  
- **Selective Education & Curriculum Design** → Schools and universities **curate what knowledge is considered legitimate**, shaping future generations' thinking.  
- **Gatekeeping in Scientific & Intellectual Fields** → Research funding, journal publications, and academic credibility are often structured to **align with dominant NVFs**.  

🔹 *Example:* **Colonial NVFs** rewrote history to portray imperialism as "civilizing" rather than exploitative, reinforcing hierarchical worldviews for generations.

## Current dominant NVF we take for granted as "human nature" 

Many aspects of the dominant NVF are presented as "human nature," but they are, in reality, shaped by historical and social conditions. While biological factors influence human behavior, our NVF determines **which aspects of our nature are amplified, suppressed, or redirected**.  

### 1. **Endless Growth & Accumulation as a Core Drive**  
   - It’s often assumed that humans have an insatiable desire for wealth, status, and material accumulation.  
   - In reality, while humans do have drives for security and social recognition, many societies historically functioned with reciprocal, gift-based, or subsistence economies where excessive accumulation was discouraged or even shunned.  
   - Capitalist structures have reinforced the idea that "more is always better," aligning incentives toward perpetual accumulation and growth.  

### 2. **Zero-Sum Competition as Natural**  
   - Many assume that human nature is inherently competitive and that society must be structured around scarcity and zero-sum games (one person’s gain is another’s loss).  
   - While competition exists, humans are also biologically wired for cooperation, reciprocity, and prosocial behavior. Studies in anthropology and neuroscience show that cooperation is more deeply ingrained in us than ruthless competition.  
   - The dominance of hyper-competitive systems today (e.g., markets, corporate hierarchies, geopolitics) has framed competition as the only natural mode of human interaction, obscuring alternative modes of collaboration.  

### 3. **Work as Moral Duty & Identity**  
   - The idea that humans must work tirelessly to justify their existence is taken as a given. Many feel guilty or ashamed if they are not constantly productive.  
   - Historically, many societies, including hunter-gatherers and pre-industrial communities, worked only as much as necessary to sustain life and spent significant time on leisure, ritual, and social activities.  
   - The Protestant work ethic, industrial capitalism, and economic structures have reinforced the idea that work is an intrinsic moral duty rather than a socially constructed necessity.  

### 4. **Hierarchical Power Structures as Inevitable**  
   - Many assume that social hierarchies, centralized authority, and top-down control are the only ways to organize large groups of people.  
   - In reality, early human societies were often egalitarian, with power distributed more fluidly. Even in complex societies, alternative governance models (e.g., consensus-based decision-making, decentralized networks) have existed.  
   - The entrenchment of state, corporate, and institutional hierarchies has made it difficult for people to imagine non-hierarchical forms of organization as viable.  

### 5. **Rational Self-Interest as the Default Human Mindset**  
   - Economics and political theory often assume humans are primarily rational, self-interested actors who maximize personal gain.  
   - However, humans frequently act against pure self-interest in favor of community, fairness, and collective well-being. Experiments in behavioral economics (e.g., ultimatum game, public goods game) reveal a strong tendency toward fairness and punishment of unfair behavior—even at personal cost.  
   - The dominant NVF reinforces self-interest as "rational" while framing altruism and collective thinking as naïve or idealistic.  

### 6. **Transactional Relationships as the Norm**  
   - Many believe human relationships—whether social, economic, or romantic—must be transactional, based on mutual benefit, clear value exchange, or utility.  
   - Historically, many societies prioritized non-transactional, kinship-based, or communal relationships where reciprocity was implicit rather than contractual.  
   - The expansion of market logic into all aspects of life (e.g., networking, dating, social capital) has normalized transactionalism as a fundamental aspect of human interaction.  

### 7. **Scarcity as an Unchangeable Reality**  
   - The idea that scarcity is a fixed, eternal condition of human existence is deeply embedded in modern economic thought.  
   - While physical limitations exist, scarcity is often artificially created (e.g., through planned obsolescence, intellectual property laws, controlled resource distribution).  
   - Many pre-modern societies operated under abundance-oriented worldviews, where redistribution and communal access reduced scarcity’s role in structuring life.  

### 8. **Reproduction & Family Structures as Fixed Biological Imperatives**  
   - The idea that humans are "meant" to reproduce, form nuclear families, and structure societies around kinship is widely accepted.  
   - While reproduction is a biological drive, different societies have constructed family and relationship structures in highly varied ways, from communal parenting to celibate monastic traditions.  
   - Changing NVFs (e.g., urbanization, career prioritization, philosophical shifts) have altered reproductive and family behaviors, showing that even deeply rooted biological imperatives can be reshaped.  

---

### 9. Land, Nature, and Resources as Private Property
   - It’s taken for granted that land, water, and natural resources can be owned, bought, and sold as commodities.
   - Many indigenous cultures viewed land as a shared responsibility, not something to be possessed.
   - The enclosure of commons and privatization of resources were historical shifts that solidified the idea that private ownership is natural.

## **Selection Pressure on NVFs**  

For an NVF to function, it requires a **collective** to embody it—whether that be people, institutions, or soon, AI. NVFs do not exist in isolation; they must be carried by networks of agents that act upon them.  

Certain NVFs tend to dominate based on how well they spread, reinforce themselves, and adapt to their environment:  

### **Some Traits That Increase NVF Selection and Survival**  

- **NVFs That Spread the Fastest**: The ability to propagate quickly—through memes, institutions, algorithms, or social contagion—gives an NVF a survival advantage.  
- **NVFs That Target Their Audience**: Some NVFs spread efficiently because they are tailored to specific psychological or ideological groups, often reinforced by recommendation algorithms and social segmentation.  
- **NVFs That Create Strong Identities**: NVFs that give individuals a sense of belonging, purpose, and in-group identity tend to be more resilient, as their members actively defend and promote them.  
- **Survival of Effective NVFs**: NVFs that effectively coordinate large groups, legitimize power, or provide psychological coherence tend to spread and persist.  
- **Positive Reinforcement Cycles and Convergence**: NVFs can create self-reinforcing feedback loops. Those that successfully reinforce behaviors and beliefs beneficial to their own reproduction tend to converge, becoming dominant within societies.  
- **Power-Justifying NVFs**: NVFs that help individuals or groups gain power, justify that power, and convert that power into discourse dominance are strongly selected. These NVFs shape institutions and social norms, making alternative frames harder to challenge.  
- **NVFs That Reinforce Stable Power Structures**: NVFs that provide legitimacy to long-term, stable hierarchies tend to be selected, as they reduce internal conflict and maintain order.  
- **Hostile NVFs with Expansionist Tendencies**: NVFs that aggressively assert themselves as the only truth—whether in politics, religion, war, or ideology—often outcompete others due to their ability to control populations, justify conquest, or drive progress. However, their dominance does not necessarily mean they are beneficial in the long term.  
- **NVFs That Provide Knowledge, Truth, and Good Strategies**: NVFs that offer valuable heuristics, predictive accuracy, and effective strategies for navigating reality tend to be passed down.  
- **NVFs Aligned with Productivity**: NVFs that enhance labor efficiency, technological progress, or economic growth tend to be reinforced by societal structures, as they increase collective capacity for wealth and power.  

However, **dominance does not guarantee persistence.** Many highly successful NVFs collapse due to internal contradictions, resource exhaustion, or loss of adaptability.

## **Collapse Dynamics of NVFs**  

Even the most dominant NVFs are subject to breakdown. Some of the failure points include:  

- **Lack of Sufficient Intelligence to Navigate Complexity**: If an NVF's embodied collective (people, institutions, AI) lacks the cognitive ability to accurately comprehend and respond to reality, the NVF becomes maladaptive. A common failure mode is when environmental complexity outgrows the collective's ability to manage it.  
- **Simply Outcompeted**: More effective or aggressive NVF may overtake an existing one, either through ideological shifts, technological advances, or sheer force.  
- **Alienation by Other NVFs**: If an NVF isolates itself too much or becomes too rigid, surrounding NVFs may outmaneuver or suppress it, leading to its decline.  
- **Dependency on Expansion**: NVFs that require continual growth—whether territorial, economic, or ideological—eventually face limits and destabilize when expansion is no longer possible.  
- **Overoptimization and Resource Depletion**: NVFs that prioritize short-term gains (e.g., maximizing efficiency, conquest, or profit) may exhaust resources, making them unsustainable in the long run.  
- **Loss of Contact with Reality**: NVFs that become too self-referential—rejecting external data or failing to adapt to changing conditions—collapse when their explanatory power breaks down.  
- **Feedback Loops Leading to Self-Destruction**: NVFs that reinforce increasingly extreme behaviors or power structures may reach a tipping point where they implode due to internal contradictions.  
- **Technological or Social Disruption**: A shift in material conditions (e.g., new technology, environmental collapse, demographic change) can render an NVF obsolete, leading to its rapid decline.  

Because NVFs operate within **complex adaptive systems**, their longevity depends on their flexibility, alignment with reality, and ability to regulate their own excesses.
