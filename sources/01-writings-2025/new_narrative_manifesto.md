# Beyond the Old Myths: A Manifesto for a New Political Narrative

We live in an age of political theater and cultural combat. Each day brings a fresh spectacle of symbolic politics and binary moralizing – leaders trading insults on social media, movements casting opponents as monsters or fools, and pundits framing every issue as a showdown of good vs. evil. Many citizens have grown **disillusioned** by this shallow play of symbols and emotions. They sense *cracks in the system* – a feeling that our public discourse has become unmoored from reality and genuine human needs. Instead of enlightenment or solutions, we get emotional manipulation: fear stoked for votes, outrage weaponized for clicks, virtue signalers on one side and strongman idolaters on the other. This manifesto is for those who refuse to accept this as normal. It is a call to clearly understand **why** the old narratives hold power and **how** we can craft a new one – one based not on hatred and hollow moralizing but on cognitive empowerment, emotional literacy, and shared meaning.

We must begin by understanding the *emotional and symbolic dynamics* that make demagogic figures like Donald Trump so powerful. But we will not lazily label such leaders or their followers as simply evil or stupid. That reflexive moral dismissal is part of what got us here. Instead, we will analyze the deeper **emotional mechanisms** – fear, desire, anger – and the potent **symbolic projections** – wealth, strength, intelligence – that fuel their appeal. Only by seeing clearly why the *old myths* enthrall so many can we hope to tell new stories that inspire a different future. In turn, we will critique why current progressive and oppositional movements have so often failed to counter this appeal: too often they have offered indignation instead of insight, or abstract ideals instead of models that connect to people’s lived experiences. Moral superiority contests and finger-pointing have proven to be dead ends, unable to break the spell of the demagogue. Finally, we will chart a path forward – a new lens for politics rooted in **cognitive empowerment** (teaching ourselves to understand and resist manipulation), **emotional literacy** (engaging fear and anger without being ruled by them), **collective meaning-making** (forging shared purpose from the ground up), and **narrative awareness** (recognizing the stories that shape our world). Without *new stories*, we will remain prisoners of old myths. With them, we can rally the disillusioned into constructive action. This is a manifesto of both urgency and hope – a vision to stir mind and spirit toward a politics worthy of our highest humanity.

## The Emotional Allure of the Strongman  

To explain the power of figures like Trump, we must start with the **emotions** they harness. Human beings are not purely rational actors; we are driven by hopes and fears, angers and yearnings. Demagogues understand this viscerally. Rather than offering complex policy, they offer a *feeling* – the catharsis of venting anger, the relief of having someone to blame, the thrill of vicarious strength. Modern strongman leaders tap into a well of **fear, desire, and anger** that runs deep in the public psyche.

Consider fear: the world is changing in bewildering ways, and many feel adrift or threatened. A leader like Trump paints a picture of a nation under siege – by immigrants, by elitist bureaucrats, by cultural change. People who feel afraid or left behind understandably latch onto a simple promise of safety. In fact, historians of populism note that followers are often motivated not by hatred per se, but by *anxiety* about decline and disorder. A famous study of Nazi supporters found that many were driven by *“a sense of the decline”* of their nation, *“a desire to rediscover past greatness, a fear of social disorder and the longing for a strong leader.”* The same was true of Trump’s base. **Fear** of chaos or outsiders primes people to seek a champion who will turn back the clock to a golden age and vanquish the threats. Trump’s endless talk of American carnage, invasion, and “losing our country” served exactly this purpose – to raise the specter of chaos and stoke a desperate desire for rescue.

Then there is **anger** – often intertwined with fear. Years of economic stagnation, cultural marginalization, or humiliation create a reservoir of resentment. A skilled demagogue converts that free-floating anger into a directed force. At his rallies, Trump would actively *create an enemy* for the crowd to hate: pointing to journalists, protesters, or opposing politicians as villains. By leading chants against scapegoats (“Crooked Hillary!” “Build the wall!”), he gave his followers an intoxicating outlet for their anger. Psychologically, this is powerful. People who feel looked down upon or ignored relish seeing someone “punch back” on their behalf. Trump even played on **humiliation**, arguing that *“we have been humiliated and manipulated”* by China, by Mexico, by laughably weak leaders – and that his own bullying shows he is tough enough to *“take on those who have humiliated us”*. The *pain of humiliation* often burns hotter than fear. As one analysis noted, when people feel humiliated “they get angry and when they get angry they attack,” and thus *“the antidote to humiliation is a strong leader who will humiliate others”* in return. Trump’s merciless mockery of opponents – deriding them as *“losers”*, *“weak”*, or *“crazy”* – was not a liability to these followers; it was proof that he would turn the tables and make *someone else* hurt for a change. 

Yet it’s not all negative emotion – there is also **desire** and *yearning* at play. The strongman presents an image of something his supporters long for. In Trump’s case, he embodied a certain *fantasy of success*. Here was a brash billionaire promising that *“you can win bigly too.”* He projected **wealth, strength, and confidence** – symbols that many frustrated people *aspire* to or take comfort in. Unlikely as it may sound, Trump managed to style himself as the **prototypical “ordinary American”** in the story he told. Not typical – he was absurdly wealthy and privileged – but *prototypical*, representing an exaggerated version of the traits his base admired. He **flaunted his wealth** openly – gold-plated everything, private jets, a supermodel wife – but did so in a way that connected with the working class imagination: *“He lived the rich man’s life most working men dreamed of… without sacrificing a way of talking about the world that would not be out of place on the construction sites”*. In other words, Trump’s persona said to his followers: *I have the money, power, and fame you wish you had, but I still talk like you and thumb my nose at the snobs*. This **symbolic projection** is incredibly potent. It allowed struggling Americans to *identify* with a billionaire as *their* champion. His expensive suits and brazen swagger telegraphed strength and success, while his crude language and rule-breaking showed he wasn’t an elitist outsider. The way he constantly **talked about “strength”** – promising to be *“tough”* where others were weak, to *“knock the hell out of”* ISIS, to *“build a great wall”* – sold an image of masculine force that many found comforting. And when he **boasted of his intelligence**, calling himself a *“stable genius”* or claiming *“I have the best words,”* supporters took it as part of his larger-than-life mythos – a folk hero who might be a bit outrageous but *on their side*.

Research confirms that these emotional currents are key to populist appeal. A recent large-scale study across many countries found that *“feelings of sadness, anger, and despair”* strongly predict support for populist leaders like Trump. In fact, such negative emotions form *“fertile ground for populists”*, who can exploit pain and grief just as readily as fear. Crucially, this is not simply ignorance or stupidity on the part of voters – it is unmet emotional needs. People who are **sad or anxious about their future** will understandably grasp at a story that promises pride and hope (even a false hope). People **angry** at corrupt elites or a system that left them behind will cheer someone who *“tells it like it is”* and upends polite norms. Trump’s **charismatic “emotional campaign”** played on all of this: he stayed *“on the emotional level”*, largely dismissing logical policy detail, and thus could leverage the *“propaganda tool”* of charisma and fear-mongering without being pinned down by fact-checkers. This was *by design*: as one observer put it, Trump’s speeches rarely offered concrete plans, just *strength, catastrophe, and the promise of rescue*. He understood that when **“people are anxious and afraid they turn to someone who promises certainty, solutions, and strength.”* In a very real sense, *the strongman’s power comes not from some magical genius on his part, but from the emotional energy his supporters project onto him*. He becomes a **symbol** – of their anger, their aspirations, their frustrated common sense. As long as he embodies that symbolic role convincingly (wealthy but “one of us,” rule-breaker but “our voice”), normal standards of truth or decency simply don’t apply in the minds of his followers. He is playing in the realm of myth and emotion, where facts matter far less than feelings.

None of this is to *excuse* demagogues who manipulate these emotions. Rather, it is to *explain* their appeal so that we can respond wisely. It is easy – and emotionally satisfying, in its own way – for opponents to dismiss the followers of such a leader as nothing but a “basket of deplorables,” irredeemably racist or ignorant. But that response is a huge mistake. However toxic the rhetoric, the underlying human emotions are real and widespread. Fear of loss, anger at indignity, desire for recognition – these are universals. Populist strongmen channel them into a **clear narrative**: *“The world is dangerous and corrupt, but **I** will make you safe, respected, and proud again.”* Trump succeeded, as two social psychologists noted, by providing *“a categorical grid – a clear definition of ‘us’ and ‘them’ – that allowed many Americans to make sense of their lived experience and entertain hope”*. In other words, he told a **simple story** that explained their pain (blame “those people”) and offered an emotionally satisfying solution (support the bold hero who fights for *us*). Until we offer a compelling alternative story, that formula will continue to win converts. 

## The Failure of Hollow Opposition  

If the rise of leaders like Trump is fueled by emotional narrative, one might expect their opponents to counter with *a stronger narrative of their own*. Yet in recent years, many progressive and establishment voices have fallen into a trap – **fighting symbolism with technocracy, and outrage with moral self-righteousness**. The results have been dispiriting. Too often the progressive or centrist response to Trumpism has been to double down on facts, policies, and moral condemnations, **without addressing the emotional and narrative battlefield** where populism thrives. This is a critical failure of the current oppositional narrative.

One aspect of this failure is the reliance on **moral superiority and finger-pointing**. It is an understandable reaction: when faced with a leader who lies, bullies, and traffics in bigotry, good people feel an urge to *denounce* and *shame* the indecency. Indeed, holding the line on basic morality is important. But as a political strategy to *win hearts and minds*, constant moral scolding is utterly counterproductive. It has devolved into what one commentator called *“moral superiority contests”* within progressive culture – a race to signal virtue by declaring others vile. In practice, this comes across as **elitist disdain** for anyone who doesn’t already agree. Calling Trump supporters “racists,” mocking their education, or implying they are simply dupes of propaganda might make the accuser feel righteous, but it only entrenches the divide. Social psychology warns that when groups compete for moral high ground, each side just digs in deeper: *“Each group…seeks to push [rivals] down by advancing claims of superior virtue…insulting or shaming them”*. We have seen this pattern play out: some liberals brand all Trump voters as ignorant bigots; in turn, those voters see liberals as arrogant, contemptuous hypocrites. Everyone ends up feeling attacked and confirmed in their view of the other’s perfidy. **No minds are changed.** In fact, this cycle of mutual disdain only *fuels* the populist fire – it fits perfectly into the demagogue’s narrative that *“the elites think you’re garbage.”* Every sanctimonious tweet from a movie star or wonky think-tanker telling Trump country how awful they are just *proves* to those voters that they made the right choice in rejecting “elitist moral bullying.” In short, trying to *shame* people into enlightenment is a fool’s errand. It indulges in *symbolic politics* of its own – the performance of virtue – while doing nothing to foster understanding.

Another aspect of the failing oppositional narrative is its frequent retreat into **abstraction and technocracy**. Faced with the visceral storytelling of populists, mainstream progressives often respond with dry policy analysis or high-minded ideals. There is a sense that *“if only we correct the lies and present the facts, people will come around.”* Unfortunately, that Enlightenment-era faith in reason is not how real politics works in our media-saturated, emotionally-charged world. Progressive movements, NGOs, and experts frequently speak in **jargon and data** that may be perfectly rational, yet utterly uninspiring to the public. As one human-rights advocate observed, liberals have *“become technocrats: detached from communities, speaking in jargon no one else understands,”* relying on policy details decided in elitist circles most people *“cannot enter.”* While populists appeal to common sense and gut feeling, the anti-populists appeal to process and expertise. The **passion** and **story** are missing. Unsurprisingly, this leaves many people cold. *“People do not vote on the basis of facts alone, if at all,”* the advocate notes; *“they do so on the basis of their values, following those that are able to tap into their emotions, and going where they feel a sense of belonging.”* When progressives counter a stirring myth of *“Make America Great Again”* with a PowerPoint about marginal tax rates or a lecture about globalization, they are bringing a spreadsheet to a story fight. As the saying goes, *“you don’t bring a knife to a gunfight”* – and you certainly don’t bring a policy memo to a culture war. Yet time and again, that is exactly what has happened. One analyst quipped that political moderates acted as if the antidote to Brexit and Trump was *“more fact-checking and white papers,”* even as the other side conjured wild but compelling narratives of national rebirth and heroic “little guys vs. corrupt elites.” The result: *“Too often, political progressives try to fight these hugely resonant stories with policy memos…hoping that rational arguments and empirical data will somehow win out against brilliant political narratives”* of grievance and identity.

Compounding this, the opposition’s attachment to **abstract ideals** can become a crutch – a way to *assert moral truth without engaging lived reality*. For example, proclaiming “diversity is our strength” or “love trumps hate” are fine values, but they risk being empty slogans if not backed by a narrative that connects with someone’s personal struggles. It is one thing to champion **democracy, equality, compassion**, etc., but simply *asserting* those ideals (or worse, scolding others for not holding them) does little to convince someone who feels *democracy has betrayed them*, or that *equality* for some meant *misery* for them. Without a concrete story or model that addresses people’s *perceptions and pain*, high ideals float above reality. The left, for instance, often spoke about *tolerance and inclusivity* – yet to those fearful of cultural change, this sounded like *“you shut up while we change your world.”* The **failure to translate ideals into relatable narrative** – into *emotional understanding* – meant that even when progressives were *right*, they failed to *feel* convincing. Hillary Clinton’s 2016 campaign was a stark example: she had mountains of policy knowledge and rational plans, but she came across as a *“policy wonk”* lacking emotional appeal or clear narrative vision. Trump, with his reality-TV charisma and gut-level catchphrases, **out-narrated** her despite offering almost no substance. Voters *“were not voting [their] self-interest, but how they felt,”* as one scholar noted of that election. And Clinton’s appeals to experience and competence could not match the emotional *“sizzle”* her opponent provided. This pattern repeats: progressives speaking to the head, while populists speak to the heart (or the gut). 

To be fair, many on the progressive side now recognize this problem. Thinkers like cognitive linguist George Lakoff have for years urged liberals to stop imagining that *“just giving people the facts”* is enough. Lakoff argues that progressives are *“trying to do 21st century politics with an 18th century mind”* – clinging to an outdated model of reason that ignores emotion. Enlightenment rationality assumed facts and logic alone guide people, but neuroscience and experience show that **identity and emotion** guide human decision-making far more. Conservatives understood this and invested in **morally-based framing** – telling stories about freedom, faith, family, nation that resonate with people’s values. Progressives, however, often put their faith in wonkish pragmatism or moral indignation, and thus have *“ignored emotion”* to their peril. When the **opposition’s message** to those taken in by Trump boils down to *“You fools, he’s lying to you. Here, read this 10-page fact-check or accept that we’re better people than you,”* the battle is already lost. Not only does this approach fail to persuade – it actively reinforces the populist narrative of arrogant elites. It cedes the emotional terrain to the demagogue. As an analyst of political myths put it bluntly, *“progressives should worry about the myth gap”* – the void of inspiring story on their side – because wherever there is such a gap, *“it’s all too easy for dark ‘anti-myths’ to fill the void.”* And indeed, nationalist and reactionary myths have rushed in to occupy the cultural imagination, from Trump’s American carnage to various conspiracy-fueled worldviews.

The takeaway is clear: **without a new narrative, the opposition to demagoguery will remain hollow**. Simply standing on a pedestal of moral high ground or rattling off facts has not worked. We cannot *shame* people into enlightenment, nor *technocrat* our way to the common good. We need to offer a different *understanding* – one that equips people with a deeper grasp of their own emotions and of the societal forces at play. It is not that progressive ideals of equality, inclusion, and justice are misguided; it is that they must be embedded in a story and practice that empower people, rather than talking down to them. To truly counter the Trumps of the world, we must **upgrade our approach**: less lecturing, more listening; less about our moral purity, more about collective problem-solving; less trying to *win debates*, more trying to *win hearts*. In short, we need a new lens on politics altogether, one that transcends the false choice between cynical emotional manipulation and naive rationalism.

## Awakening Minds and Hearts: A New Lens for Politics  

What might this new approach look like? At its core, it would mean shifting from *manipulating* people’s emotions and identities to **empowering** people’s understanding of them. We need a political culture that prizes **cognitive empowerment, emotional literacy, narrative awareness,** and **collective meaning-making**. These phrases may sound lofty, but their essence is simple: give people the tools to truly understand themselves and the world, rather than treating them as pawns in someone else’s game. It’s about leveling up the public’s awareness so that demagogues can’t so easily hijack our brains – and so that we can actively shape the stories that define our society.

- **Cognitive Empowerment** – This means helping people see the *frames and forces* that influence their thinking. In a propaganda-rich environment, it is an act of empowerment to reveal *how* politics and media work on our minds. For example, understanding common tricks of demagogic rhetoric (such as scapegoating, ad hominem attacks, repetition of emotionally charged slogans) can inoculate citizens against their effects. Cognitive empowerment also implies teaching critical thinking and media literacy on a broad scale: how to spot misinformation, how to identify biases (in others and in ourselves). It even involves **critical self-reflection** about one’s own group identities and assumptions. In academic terms, this is related to what Paulo Freire called *conscientização* (critical consciousness) – people becoming aware of the social forces shaping their lives and thereby gaining control over them. One practical facet is recognizing how **cognitive biases** and group psychology affect politics. For instance, understanding that humans have confirmation bias (we notice evidence that fits our story and ignore what doesn’t) helps explain why simply *giving more facts* doesn’t change minds. Part of cognitive empowerment is unveiling these hidden biases so they have less hold. *“The cognitive component of empowerment involves an awareness of the forces that shape policies and systems,”* as one definition puts it. Imagine if civics education, public discourse, and activism all prioritized explaining *why* certain rhetoric appeals, or *how* our brains react to fear-based messaging. Rather than talking down to people, we would be *sharing the playbook* by which politicians try to sway us. An informed citizenry that understands these mechanisms is far less likely to be swayed by empty slogans or to demonize others blindly. Cognitive empowerment is essentially about **respecting people’s ability to understand**, rather than exploiting their ignorance. It treats voters not as targets to be manipulated but as collaborators in diagnosing society’s problems. This must be a cornerstone of any new political narrative: *we all must become students of how narratives and power operate*. Only then can we start to rewrite those narratives together.

- **Emotional Literacy** – Hand in hand with cognitive insight must come a new focus on **emotional understanding**. Emotional literacy means being able to recognize, name, and productively manage our emotions – and to empathize with others’ emotions. Up to now, politics has often done the opposite: it has **weaponized emotions** (fear-mongering attack ads, outrage media) or tried to suppress them (“calm down, be rational”). Neither extreme serves us. We need to cultivate a citizenry (and leadership) that is *emotionally self-aware*. For an individual voter, this might mean learning to notice: *“Am I being made to feel afraid right now? Angry? Why? Is that fear justified by facts, or is someone stroking it for their own agenda?”* Instead of just reacting, people could pause and understand their own emotional triggers. Imagine watching a political debate and not just thinking *“I agree with her”* or *“I hate him,”* but also *“The way he’s speaking is trying to make me angry at a scapegoat – do I accept that, or question it?”* This kind of emotional mindfulness can be taught and learned. It also means acknowledging the *validity* of emotions that people have. Dismissing voters’ fears or anger as irrational is itself irrational if we want to persuade them. A new narrative would encourage us to **listen** to what emotions are telling us – the legitimate grievances or needs beneath them – without letting emotions alone dictate our actions. In practice, emotional literacy in politics would encourage dialogues where people can express anxieties or resentment *without* immediately being judged, and then examine those feelings critically. It would also train leaders to **alleviate rather than aggravate** public emotions. One study about populism urged that *“all politicians should alleviate rather than avail themselves of negative emotions”*, noting that if anger and fear are left to fester, populists will seize on them. Emotional literacy thus has a collective dimension: as a society, recognizing when a population is grieving (e.g. communities gutted by job loss) or feeling shame (e.g. demographic groups made to feel left behind) – and addressing those wounds through policy *and* narrative, rather than ignoring them until a demagogue exploits them. In short, emotional literacy calls for **compassionate awareness**: treating emotions as important signals to be dealt with in healthy ways, not as toys to be played with by propagandists. This would fundamentally change the tone of politics – likely making it more humane and resilient.

- **Collective Meaning-Making** – This phrase refers to communities actively coming together to interpret their situation and decide on their shared values and goals. Right now, meaning-making in politics often happens *to* people, not *with* them: elites craft narratives and broadcast them, while citizens passively consume (or reject) the messaging. A new narrative for the disillusioned must invite people to be *authors* of their collective story. This could mean public forums, local assemblies, online platforms – any space where diverse people collaborate to ask “What do we want our future to be? What does America (or any society) stand for at its best? What problems do we face, and what do they mean to us?” Such questions go beyond left-right talking points. They invite a community to construct its own narrative from the ground up. Historically, *social movements* have done this well: for instance, the Civil Rights Movement reframed the black American experience from one of victimhood to one of heroic resilience and moral claim to equality – a narrative forged in churches, meetings, marches, and shared struggle. Today, we need similar meaning-making processes that engage those who feel alienated by *both* Trumpism *and* the establishment. **Collective meaning-making** is essentially democratic storytelling. It recognizes that **people need meaning** – a sense of belonging and purpose – as much as they need policy details. If we don’t provide arenas to build positive meaning, they will latch onto negative meaning (e.g. QAnon’s wild conspiracies give a kind of twisted purpose to some). A healthy body politic would encourage *local narratives* to flourish – stories of communities taking control of their destiny, of citizens bridging divides to solve problems. These local stories can then federate into a *new national story* that feels authentic because it *emerged* from the people, not from a PR firm. Political leaders in a new narrative era would see themselves not as solo storytellers but as **facilitators of the people’s story** – amplifying the voices of constituents, weaving together threads from many communities into a coherent vision. This is a more messy, bottom-up approach than the old top-down messaging, but it’s ultimately more legitimate. People will fight for a story they had a hand in creating. Moreover, collective meaning-making builds **trust and social bonds**, which undercuts the polarization that demagogues thrive on. When you’ve sat in a circle with someone from the “other tribe” and jointly crafted a statement of shared values, it’s harder to later demonize them. Our new narrative must therefore be *radically inclusive and participatory* in its construction. It is the antidote to both the false unity of demagogic populism (*“only my followers are the true people”*) and the fragmented identity politics of the left that sometimes lacks a unifying myth. We need an **inclusive mythos** – one that aspires, as one author put it, to *“a larger us”*. These broad themes can only come alive if people at every level contribute to fleshing them out in concrete terms for their context.

- **Narrative Awareness** – Finally, underpinning all the above is a general awakening to the power of **narrative** itself. We must foster a citizenry that is conscious that *stories* are not just entertainment – they are the lifeblood of politics and culture. Narrative awareness means recognizing that how we frame our reality – the metaphors, the heroes and villains we imagine, the arc we see our society following – will shape what we do. It means teaching that **humans are storytelling creatures**; as the literary scholar Barbara Hardy said, *“we dream in narrative, daydream in narrative… live by narrative.”* We use stories to make sense of chaos, to decide what matters, to transmit values. A population unaware of this is easy prey: they will internalize whatever story is most loudly repeated. But a population that *knows* this can begin to choose their stories *consciously*. They can ask, “What story am I part of, and do I accept it?” They can detect when someone is trying to *enroll* them in a narrative of hate or fear, and say “No, that story is not who I want to be.” They can also actively *seek out better stories*. Narrative awareness in practice might involve learning about the great myths and political narratives of history – how they rise and fall, how they unite or divide. It could involve exercises in *reframing* issues: for example, taking an issue like immigration and viewing it through different narratives (a threat narrative, a humanitarian narrative, an economic opportunity narrative) to see how each shapes our feelings and responses. By doing so, people realize narratives are *constructed*, not given by nature – and thus can be *reconstructed*. **Without new stories, we remain trapped in old ones.** This truth must become common knowledge. One political scientist noted that research consistently shows *“evidence and arguments matter less than we think…instead, it’s the values held by our families, friends and colleagues…which are in turn shaped by myths and stories.”* In other words, *we are guided by the narratives around us*. If we don’t consciously craft new narratives, we will subconsciously follow whoever’s narrative dominates (however destructive it may be). The encouraging flip side is that **stories can be changed**. As another scholar explained, *“shared stories are humanity’s essential tool for overcoming the obstacles to collective action”* – they help forge common interests and motivate cooperation . History provides examples: the New Deal era in the U.S. created a story of social solidarity out of the Depression; South Africa averted civil war by creating the narrative of the “Rainbow Nation.” These were intentional narratives, promoted by leaders but owned by the people. We need that level of narrative ambition again. By spreading narrative awareness, we invite everyone to see themselves as *storytellers of the collective*. Rather than being passive consumers of partisan media narratives, people can participate in shaping the national story. This is empowering and antidotal to cynicism. When narrative awareness grows, **demagoguery loses power**, because a populace that understands story can spot a *cheap story* a mile away. They can say, “Ah, I see, Candidate X is casting immigrants as the villain in a simple fairy tale to gain power – I won’t fall for that two-dimensional plot.” And they will demand richer, more truthful narratives instead.

In summary, this new lens for understanding politics calls for a citizenry that is *both intellectually and emotionally* empowered. It calls for politics to treat people as **whole humans** – thinking, feeling, meaning-seeking beings – rather than as data points or sheep to be herded. Cognitive empowerment gives us the *intellectual armor* against manipulation; emotional literacy gives us the *emotional resilience* and empathy to resist hate; collective meaning-making gives us the *democratic forum* to write our own destiny; narrative awareness gives us the *meta-cognitive insight* to choose our myths wisely. These are not instantaneous changes – they represent a cultural shift, a reorientation of education, media, activism, and leadership style. But they offer a way out of the current dysfunction. Instead of a politics of *spectacle and outrage*, we envision a politics of *reflection and imagination*. Instead of *“us vs. them”* tribal wars, we work towards an *inclusive “larger us”* where *us* simply means *all of us*. Instead of being trapped by primitive emotional triggers, we become *students of our own psychology* and gradually masters of it. This is the groundwork for a new story of how society can function.

## A New Mythos: From Fracture to Wholeness  

All the principles above point toward one ultimate imperative: **we must tell new stories or be forever enslaved by old myths**. Humans cannot live without stories; they are how we organize reality. Right now, much of the world is enthralled by **old myths** – some of them very dark. The nationalist myth of “Make America Great Again” is really a recycled story told throughout history: a tale of past glory lost due to impure forces, and a heroic leader who will restore the rightful order. Variations of this myth are on the march worldwide (Trump, Farage, Putin, Erdoğan, Modi – different faces of a similar archetype). These narratives are *powerful* in their simplicity and emotional punch, but they are fundamentally **regressive** – they drag us backward into division, scapegoating, and authoritarianism. Yet if we do not provide a compelling alternative myth, millions will continue to find meaning in these dangerous fictions. As one commentator warned, in the current “myth gap” we inhabit, *“it’s all too easy for dark ‘anti-myths’ to fill the void”*. Stories of **“us vs. them”** and **“scarcity and doom”** proliferate: from the consumerist myth that *“you are what you buy”*, to apocalyptic myths of inevitable civilizational collapse, to xenophobic myths of blood and soil. Indeed, *“the nationalist myths of Trump and Farage, Putin and Le Pen”* – those visions of making the nation great by excluding or conquering others – *“were at the very core of 2016 and its bitter harvest.”* We are reaping the consequences of failing to update our shared narrative.

It is not enough to resist or debunk these myths; **we must create new ones**. This manifesto issues a call to all who see the cracks in the current system: let us become the **myth-makers** of the 21st century. By “myth” I do not mean a falsehood, but a grand narrative that provides meaning and orients society toward ideals. We need myths that inspire at least as much passion as the old ones, but which lead in a constructive direction. What might such a new mythos contain? It is still early – we are collectively *groping* for the story of our future – but certain themes glimmer on the horizon. One is the idea of **universal dignity**: a mythos that expands the circle of “us” to include all people. Instead of defining identity in opposition to some demonized other, the new story would define a larger identity that *deliberately includes everyone*. It could be the nation reimagined not as a tribe of one ethnicity or ideology, but as *a tapestry of many, unified by shared humanity and destiny*. Or it could be an even broader human story – a “Spaceship Earth” narrative that all of us on this planet rise or fall together. This theme of **interdependence** can answer the fear-based tribal myths with a hopeful vision of solidarity: *we are many, but we can be one without erasing our uniqueness*. Another theme is **long-term responsibility** – what Alex Evans called *“thinking in terms of a longer now.”* The new myth might emphasize that we stand at a crossroads in history, with echoes of a deep past and the possibilities of a deep future in our hands, and that *we must become good ancestors* for generations unborn. This counters the nihilistic and zero-sum myths (like “grab what you can before collapse”) with a call to stewardship and purposeful progress.

Crucially, the new narrative must *acknowledge our emotional reality* and provide a healthy outlet for it. It should channel anger into **righteous action** against actual injustices (rather than scapegoats), channel fear into **courageous cooperation** (rather than xenophobia), channel desire into a vision of **shared prosperity and meaning** (not just individual gain). It must offer *belonging*: people should see themselves in the story. For example, rather than writing off the rural factory worker or the urban gig worker or the immigrant family, the story should weave all their struggles into one common plot – perhaps a struggle against dehumanizing systems and for community revival. Instead of *“the little guy vs. the remote elites”* being a narrative owned by right-wing populists, the new myth can frame it as *all ordinary people vs. any structure (left or right) that denies their dignity*. This could unite those who currently think they are enemies. It’s notable that one of Trump’s appeals was railing against “elites” and “corrupt politicians”; a new progressive myth could **agree** that corruption and oligarchy are enemies, but broaden the definition of who “the people” are (not just one race or nationality) and clarify that the battle is not against imagined foreign foes but against inequality, climate crisis, and loss of community. The *antagonists* in the new story would not be scapegoated groups, but rather the impersonal forces (greed, hatred, ignorance, fear) that all of us together must overcome. This avoids demonizing particular populations and instead calls on everyone to combat the “inner demons” and collective challenges we share.

To build this mythos, we need **multi-front action**. The work spans culture, education, media, politics, and personal relationships. Artists, writers, and filmmakers have a role: they can start infusing popular culture with narratives of collaboration across difference, of humans triumphing not by conquering enemies but by transcending old divisions. Educators and activists can experiment with deliberative dialogues and assemblies (the practice of collective meaning-making described earlier) to prototype the new narrative on a small scale – **local myths of renewal** that can inspire larger regions. Thought leaders and spiritual voices can articulate moral visions that resonate broadly – for instance, a modernized civic ethos that marries liberal values with communal solidarity, so that neither atomized individualism nor authoritarian collectivism prevails, but something new. Even **technology** can help: social media and forums could be reoriented away from outrage amplification and towards storytelling and solution-sharing platforms. Imagine online networks where communities compete not in vitriol, but in showcasing the best version of their community’s narrative and progress, learning from one another. Politics itself must be a front: emerging leaders who grasp narrative and emotional intelligence need to step up and model a new style of leadership – empathetic, story-driven, but honest and humble. These leaders would treat their *campaigns* not just as fights to win votes, but as opportunities to convene the public in writing a people’s platform (co-creating vision and policy). 

All these fronts are beginning to stir. We see nascent efforts: dialogues between ideological opposites seeking common ground, movements like the “Love Army” that Van Jones started (calling for patriotism without exclusion), or transpartisan initiatives to revive local economies and democratic councils. We see younger generations largely rejecting the old left-right paradigm and searching for something more holistic. The task now is to connect these dots, to recognize them as all part of *one larger project*: the birthing of a new cultural narrative. We must realize that *we are still in the early chapters of defining this new mythos*. It’s okay that it isn’t fully clear yet – the *work of imagination* is collective and ongoing. This manifesto does not pretend to have the final story neatly written. Rather, it calls on all who hear its message to join in **storycrafting**. We are the narrators now. 

Each of us who is disillusioned by the present state of affairs has a choice: either resign ourselves to cynicism (leaving the myths to the Trumps and tyrants), or become storytellers of a better future. Let us choose the latter. Let us gather in our communities – whether physical or virtual – and swap stories of the future we want. Let us listen radically to those we disagree with to find the threads of a narrative that can hold us together. Let thinkers and plumbers, farmers and programmers, the religious and the secular, the *ordinary folk of every kind*, see that we all have been cast in a tragedy by the old narratives – and that together we can imagine a new drama, one more gentle and just.

## Conclusion: A Rallying Vision  

This manifesto is both a critique and a call. We have diagnosed how **symbolic politics and emotional manipulation** have brought us to a perilous place, where fear and anger often drown out reason and empathy. We have recognized that simply *condemning* the demagogues and lamenting the ignorance of the masses is a dead end. Instead, we turn the mirror on the opposition itself, seeing how our own failure to provide meaning and respect has left a vacuum for authoritarians to fill. But we do not stop at critique. We outline a path toward **cognitive and emotional awakening** in our politics – a path that treats people as capable of growth and understanding, not as objects to be ruled. 

Ultimately, we issue a **rallying vision**: that a new story is possible, one that can galvanize those who have lost faith in the old ones. This is a vision of *politics as collective empowerment* – where the goal is not simply to win power but to **distribute power** to every citizen’s mind and heart. In this envisioned politics, leaders are narrators and facilitators, not saviors. The people are protagonists, not an audience. Our debates center not on which tribe is better, but on how we can all live better together. Our policies flow from a shared narrative of national (and global) renewal – a narrative shaped by input from all corners, not imposed from above.

We acknowledge that we are **at the beginning** of this journey. Great myths are not written overnight. But already the cracks in the old system let in light. More and more individuals are “waking up” to the inadequacy of the current story – they feel the confusion that comes before a paradigm shift. If you are one of those who *senses the falsehood in the choices presented to you*, take heart: you are not alone, and you are in fact the seed of the new narrative. By seeking language and clarity beyond the cliches, you are participating in its creation.

The call now is to **act and coordinate** on multiple fronts. This means building communities of trust where new ideas can flourish, engaging in conversations that bridge divides, supporting leaders and creators who embody the new narrative values, and pushing institutions (schools, media, parties) to embrace reform and reflection. It also means working on ourselves – cultivating that cognitive and emotional skillset that inoculates us against demagogues and prepares us to be responsible citizens of a true democracy. 

Above all, it means **believing in the power of story** to change the world. Nearly every social transformation in history was preceded by a revolution in narrative – people began to see themselves differently and to tell their common story in a new way. We are on the cusp of such a narrative revolution. You who read this manifesto are invited to be authors of what comes next. Reject the despair that says people are only motivated by base emotions – instead, *engage those emotions and elevate them*. Reject the notion that everything must be polarized – instead, show how pluralism can weave a richer tapestry. Reject the temptation to fight myth with cynicism – instead, **fight myth with better myth**, one that speaks to our highest aspirations and deepest truths.

Our new story will not be a utopia or a perfect consensus – humans will always have conflicts and flaws. But it can be a story that acknowledges those flaws without succumbing to them; a story that gives us direction and dignity, that replaces fatalism with agency. It can be, in short, a **myth of empowerment**: the tale of how we, the people, learned to resist manipulation, learned to understand each other, and took back the reins of our shared destiny. 

Such a myth, once internalized, becomes a self-fulfilling prophecy of sorts. If enough of us believe in a narrative of unity and empowerment, we will behave in ways that make it real. We will build the coalitions, pass the laws, create the art, teach the children – all reinforcing that narrative. Over time, the old myths of hate and fear will recede, not by force, but by obsolescence, as fewer minds give them credence. 

This is the vision to rally around. It is **philosophical** in that it demands we think deeply, and **poetic** in that it demands we dream boldly. It is **urgent** because the crises of our time (from climate change to social unrest) leave no luxury for apathy – yet it is **hopeful** because it trusts in our collective capacity to grow and change. It asks each of us to be both realist and idealist: clear-eyed about the psychological and cultural battles at hand, yet refusing to abandon the belief that people can find common light.

In closing, remember the simple truth that has been a theme throughout: *Without new stories, we fall prey to old myths.* Let us, then, **become the storytellers**. Let us craft a narrative of America – and the world – that is worthy of the diversity, complexity, and nobility of its people. Let us tell of a future where politics is not war but **collaboration**, where culture is not spectacle but **community**, where leaders do not divide and conquer but **unite and empower**. 

This is not a naive fantasy; it is a myth-in-the-making, and its power grows with each person who embraces it. To those who have hungered for such a vision – who have felt the loneliness of perceiving the failures of the current system but not having the words to articulate a different way – take this manifesto as an invitation. Add your voice. Refine the ideas. Most importantly, **live out** the values of the new narrative in your daily actions and organizing. As we do so, what was once only a dream or a conversation will solidify into a movement, then into a new common sense. 

The old myths are **marching off** into the sunset of history, even if they thrash while going. A new dawn of narrative awaits – one we have a hand in writing. Let’s pick up the pen, together, and begin. The story of cognitive empowerment, emotional connection, and shared meaning is ours to tell, and through it, we will reclaim our future.
